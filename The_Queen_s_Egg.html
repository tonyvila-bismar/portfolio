The Queen's Egg

by Sidonei ne Sangbelles

Let me tell you about the most prestigious university in the Lands Between and the woman they keep in the library.

Raya Lucaria. The academy of sorcery. Glintstone spires, moonlit halls, a thousand years of accumulated brilliance in the study of stellar magic, and at the center of it — in the grand library, on the floor, surrounded by the crawling wet remnants of her own failed experiments — Rennala, Queen of the Full Moon, sits in the dark and tries to make children out of nothing.

She's been doing this for a while now. Hard to say exactly how long. Time moves strangely in the academy since they sealed the gates. But she has her amber egg and she has her Great Rune and she has whatever's left of her mind, and she uses all three to rebirth things. Sweetings, she calls them. They hatch from the egg wet and wrong and they crawl around the library floor making sounds that aren't quite language and aren't quite pain but sit in the horrifying valley between the two, and the scholars step over them on their way to the stacks.

The scholars step over them.

That's the part I want you to sit with. Not the tragedy of a great queen reduced to madness. Everyone tells that part. Bards and historians and people at dinner parties who think "what a shame" constitutes moral engagement. The part I want you to sit with is that the faculty of Raya Lucaria — the most educated people in this world, the inheritors of stellar wisdom, the people who can pull light from stones and reshape matter through applied mathematics — looked at a broken woman on the floor of their library surrounded by her screaming failures and decided the correct institutional response was to work around her.

They didn't help her. They didn't remove her. They didn't even close the library. They just... adjusted. Stepped over the sweetings. Rerouted foot traffic. Made it a known thing. Don't go to the west wing of the grand library after dark, the queen is rebirthing again and the floor gets slippery.

I know what that looks like from the inside. Not the rebirthing specifically — my biological experiments were conducted with considerably more precision and considerably less amber, thank you — but the being-in-a-room-where-everyone-has-agreed-you-are-a-problem-to-be-managed-rather-than-a-person-to-be-addressed. That silence. The way people's eyes slide past you. The way the institution develops a vocabulary for your presence that is technically neutral and practically annihilating. Rennala is not called "the mad queen" in official academy correspondence. She is called "the custodian of the Grand Library's restricted section." Which is how you know nobody in that building has the faintest shred of honesty left, because calling a woman who is performing failed biological generation on a library floor a "custodian" is not diplomacy. It is contempt dressed up in a nice robe.

And here's the thing about the robe. Radagon gave it to her. Not the robe — the egg. The Great Rune of the Unborn. A parting gift from the husband who left her to go marry a god. Radagon walked out of Raya Lucaria and back to the Erdtree and on his way out the door he handed Rennala the one piece of cosmic power that would let her rebirth things endlessly without ever actually creating anything viable, and if you don't recognize the shape of that particular cruelty then you have not been paying attention to how powerful people dispose of the people they no longer need.

You don't kill them. Killing creates martyrs.

You give them just enough to keep busy.

The Great Rune of the Unborn allows rebirth. Real rebirth — you can walk into the grand library, hand the queen a Larval Tear, and she will remake you. Different face. Different build. Different allocation of whatever passes for innate potential in this world. The Tarnished use it. The mechanics work. The rune is genuine. But what Rennala does with it when left to her own devices, without Larval Tears, without subjects, without purpose — she generates. She pushes biological matter through the rune's template and what comes out is... well.

I've seen botched biological work. I've done botched biological work. There is a specific quality to a living thing that was made wrong — a tension between the organism's will to exist and the wrongness of the vessel it's been given, a kind of perpetual flinch at the cellular level. The sweetings have that quality. They are alive in the way a fever dream is alive: urgently, temporarily, with the desperate energy of something that knows on some deep chemical level that it was not supposed to be here and is burning through whatever fuel it was given as fast as possible before the error corrects.

Rennala doesn't see this. Rennala sees her children.

(I need a drink. Give me a moment.)

The biological mechanism — and I'm going to talk about this clinically because the alternative is not clinical and I'm not ready for that — the biological mechanism of the Great Rune of the Unborn is template imposition. The rune contains a pattern. You feed it biological substrate. It imposes the pattern on the substrate and out comes a shaped thing. With a Larval Tear as catalyst, the shaping is precise: the tear provides the malleability, the rune provides the template, the result is a complete and functional organism reorganized to specification. It works. It's elegant. Whoever designed the system — and let's be clear, this was designed, Great Runes are engineering, not theology — whoever designed it built something genuinely beautiful.

The Larval Tear is the key to the whole thing and nobody seems interested in talking about that either. A Larval Tear is a biological substrate rendered perfectly plastic — all differentiation erased, all structural commitment dissolved, matter returned to a state of pure potential. You know what else does that? Stem cells. Embryonic tissue. The undifferentiated blastocyst before the body decides what it's going to be. The Larval Tear is artificial embryogenesis in a bottle, and the rune is the developmental program that shapes it.

Someone invented this. Someone understood biological plasticity well enough to bottle it and pair it with a cosmic template system, and that someone's name is not recorded anywhere because in the Lands Between, if a technology works well enough, it becomes theology and the engineer becomes a footnote. This is a pattern I have some personal familiarity with.

Without the tear, the shaping is... approximate. The rune tries to impose a pattern on matter that hasn't been prepared for pattern-imposition, and the result is what you get when you try to stamp a shape into clay that's too dry: the edges crack, the form distorts, what comes out looks almost like the thing it was supposed to be and is worse for the almost.

The sweetings are almost children. They have limbs. They have faces, sometimes. They have the impulse to crawl toward Rennala, which might be programmed behavior or might be something worse — some fragment of the maternal template bleeding into the product, a recursive loop where the rune encodes the desire for the mother into the child it's failing to make properly.

I've seen this kind of loop in my own work. Not with children, obviously. With plants. I did a series of grafts in the early years of my exile — trying to force compatibility between species that shouldn't be compatible, because when you have been expelled from the only laboratory that matters and you still need to practice your craft, you work with what the roadside gives you.

I got this exact failure mode: the graft site would produce tissue that was biologically active but structurally incoherent, and the tissue would grow toward the host plant as if seeking the parent organism, and the growth pattern was always wrong in a way that looked like longing if you squinted and looked like cancer if you didn't.

The fourth graft was the one that nearly got me. I was trying to splice a luminous moss variant onto a standard broadleaf — the moss had properties I needed for a tincture, and the broadleaf had the root system to support it — and the graft took, technically. The tissue merged. The moss grew. And then the merged tissue started producing a third thing, neither moss nor broadleaf but something that used both as raw material for its own agenda, and the something grew fast and it grew toward my hands every time I reached into the pot.

I burned it. I burned the pot. I burned the table the pot was on. And then I sat in my rented room above a tavern in a town whose name I have deliberately forgotten and I did not work for three weeks because I had seen what happens when you give biological matter a template without adequate constraint and the answer is: it reaches for you.

Rennala's sweetings reach for her.

I stopped squinting. Rennala never did.

The academy's response to this is instructive. Here is what they did: they provided Rennala with a steady supply of raw materials (biological substrate, ambient sorcerous energy, privacy), they maintained the environmental conditions in the grand library that the rune requires to function (temperature, humidity, the specific quality of moonlight that Rennala's magic draws on), and they posted scholars at the doors to ensure that the sweetings did not escape into the wider campus.

Not to protect the sweetings. To protect the campus from the sweetings. The distinction matters.

I talked to a Cuckoo soldier who'd been posted at the library door for six months. Nice woman. Professional. She told me the sweetings sometimes make it as far as the second corridor before they collapse. She described it the way you'd describe a plumbing problem — routine, mildly inconvenient, part of the job. She had a specific protocol for disposal. She did not use the word "disposal." She used the word "clearing." The academy has a vocabulary for this, the same way every institution that produces waste has a vocabulary for the waste that makes it sound like maintenance rather than what it actually is.

The Cuckoo soldier asked me, very politely, not to go into the library. I asked why. She said it was restricted. I asked on whose authority. She said the academy's. I asked which specific officer of the academy. She thought about this for longer than you'd expect and then said she didn't know. The restriction predated her posting. It predated most postings. It was just the rule.

Rules without authors. My absolute favorite kind. Rules that have calcified so thoroughly into institutional bedrock that challenging them would require not just authority but archaeology. You'd have to dig down through forty years of "that's how we've always done it" to find the original order, and when you got there you'd find it was signed by someone who's been dead for thirty years and whose authority derived from a committee that no longer exists.

They are enabling her. They are maintaining the conditions that allow a broken woman to endlessly produce broken things. They are doing it because the alternative — confronting Rennala, removing the rune, acknowledging that the queen of the academy is incapacitated and that the institution has been effectively leaderless since Radagon left — would require admitting something the academy has spent decades carefully not admitting, which is that Rennala's position was never about Rennala. It was about Radagon. The marriage united Raya Lucaria with the Golden Order. The queen was the seal on a political alliance, and when the alliance's other signatory walked away, the seal became meaningless. But you can't void a seal without acknowledging it was a seal, and acknowledging it was a seal means acknowledging the marriage was a transaction, and acknowledging the marriage was a transaction means the academy's autonomy — its centuries-old claim to scholarly independence, its entire identity as an institution that stands apart from political power — was purchased by a man's decision to sleep there for a while.

So they keep her.

They keep her the way you keep a receipt you can't use but can't throw away. Proof of a transaction nobody wants to discuss. The queen on the floor. The sweetings on the stairs. The scholars stepping over both, eyes forward, carrying their sorceries to their studies, pretending that the sound from the library is just the wind.

The Cuckoo soldiers guard the gates. The glintstone keys lock the doors. The institution hums along. Nobody enters. Nobody leaves. Nobody addresses the woman on the floor. The system is sealed, self-sustaining, and structurally complete, and at its center is a human being performing the same failed act of creation over and over and over, because a man gave her exactly enough power to suffer with and called it a gift.

I know what that looks like.

I know exactly what that looks like, and I'm going to pour another drink and talk about something else now, because the metaphor is getting a little too load-bearing and I refuse — I absolutely refuse — to sit here and be the exile who sees his own story in every ruined woman in every sealed tower in every broken world. That's a trap. A narcissism trap. The moment you start seeing yourself in every victim you encounter, you've stopped seeing the victim and started using them as a mirror, and Rennala deserves better than being my mirror.

She deserves better than a lot of things.

She was brilliant, you know. Before. The full moon sorceries, the Carian battle magic, the lunar spells that could pull tides and madness in equal measure. She was the real thing. Not a political appointment, not a figurehead, not a trophy wife with a crown — an actual, genuine, world-class sorcerer who could have torn the academy apart and rebuilt it better.

She had real children, too. Three of them. Ranni, who killed her own body and became a doll because anything was better than remaining in the shape the Greater Will intended for her. Radahn, who learned gravity magic so he wouldn't have to abandon his horse and held back the stars until his mind rotted away. Rykard, who fed himself to a snake.

Three children. All brilliant. All catastrophically broken. All gone.

And Rennala sits on the floor of the grand library making more. Making versions that won't leave her. Making versions that can't leave her, because they can't survive long enough to walk to the door. The sweetings don't have the developmental integrity to form intentions beyond crawling toward the closest source of warmth, which is their mother, who is also the thing that made them wrong.

If you want to understand what the Great Rune of the Unborn actually is, don't look at the mechanics. Look at the result. A woman whose real children all chose, in their different terrible ways, to escape their inheritance — and a cosmic power source that lets her produce children who are physically incapable of choosing anything at all. The rune didn't give her creation. It gave her captivity. A nursery where nothing grows up.

Radagon saw that. Fell in love with it, supposedly. Left it. Gave her an egg.

The egg is the cruelest thing in the Lands Between and nobody talks about it because nobody wants to look at what it means. It means: here is the power to create without the power to create well. Here is the template without the catalyst. Here is the shape of what you want without the substance to fill it. Here is enough to keep you trying and not enough to let you succeed.

Here is a door that opens onto a wall.

I was given things when I left Sangbelles. Not an egg. Not a rune. But the knowledge. They couldn't take that out of my head — the psychic training, the herblore, the biological engineering principles that I had learned from the best practitioners in the world. They expelled me and they left me with everything I needed to practice the craft they expelled me for practicing, and the knowledge has kept me alive and it has kept me working and it has kept me up at three in the morning staring at a failed graft wondering whether the tissue growing toward my hands is reaching for its parent or reaching for the person who made it wrong.

Rennala and I are not the same. I want to be very clear about that. She was a queen. I was never a queen. She lost a husband. I lost... well. Something different. She sits in a library. I sit in rented rooms above taverns. The scale is different. The dignity is different. She had moonlight and Carian royalty and the respect of a thousand scholars. I had a talent for potions and a knack for making the wrong people angry.

But the gift is the same. The parting gift that isn't a gift. The tool that works just well enough to keep you using it and not well enough to let you stop needing it. The power that maintains the wound by giving the wound something to do.

I think about Rennala sometimes. More than I should. I think about her on the floor of the grand library surrounded by her sweetings, and I think about the scholars stepping over them, and I think about the Cuckoo soldier with her clearing protocol, and I think about Brother Kolm who wanted to trace the feed line and got transferred to permit-checking for twenty years — no, wait, that was a different institution. A different sealed building full of people pretending the obvious catastrophe in the middle of the room is a maintenance issue.

They're all the same building. That's the thing. They're all the same building, and I have been inside every one of them, and the view from the floor is remarkably consistent.

Anyway. The academy is lovely. I hear the glintstone research is world-class. Marvelous spire work. Very impressive gates.

Shame about the library.
