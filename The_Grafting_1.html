The Grafting

by Caato Valentis

The surgeon's name was Rollo, and he had been beautiful once.

This is worth establishing because beauty, in the Lands Between, is not decorative. It is structural. The Golden Order was an aesthetic system before it was a theological one — a claim that the world had a correct shape, and that shape was pleasing to look at, and that anything unpleasing was therefore incorrect. To be beautiful under the Order was to be evidence for god. To be ugly was to be a counterargument.

Rollo had been very good evidence. Tall, even-featured, a knight of no particular distinction whose primary contribution to the defense of Stormveil was standing at his post and looking like the sort of person the Erdtree had intended. His captain valued him for this. In the years before the Shattering, when the Order still held and the great runes still cohered into something that could be called a plan, a handsome guard at the gate was worth more than a skilled one. The gate was not expected to be contested. It was expected to be admired.

Then the Order broke, and Godrick came home.

It is necessary to understand what Godrick was before he became what he became. The histories — such as they are, in a land where historians tend to be eaten — record him as the least of the demigods, a runt dragging a diluted claim to divinity like a broken limb. He had fled from the capital. He had groveled before a woman who despised him. He had hidden in Stormveil and called it ruling, the way a rat in a granary might call itself a farmer.

All of this is true, and none of it explains the grafting.

The grafting requires a different kind of understanding, and it begins with a question that the surviving scholars of the Lands Between have been remarkably reluctant to ask: not why did Godrick graft, but what did he think he was making?

(The reluctance is understandable. The answer is upsetting.)

Rollo was on duty the first night the sounds began. They came from below the castle, from chambers that had been root cellars and dungeons and were now something else, something that smelled of hot metal and the sweetish rot of flesh that has been opened and not yet decided whether to die. The sounds were not screams. That would have been simpler. Screams have a direction; they are produced by a body that wants to stop experiencing something. These sounds were produced by bodies in the process of discovering that they had new parts, and the new parts could feel, and what they felt was each other.

He did not investigate. No one investigated. This is the part that the surviving accounts tend to omit, because it reflects poorly on everyone, but it is also the part that makes the story true rather than merely horrible: the knights of Stormveil heard the sounds from below and went about their patrols and said nothing, because the sounds were coming from where the lord lived, and the lord's business was the lord's business. It is the same silence that falls over any garrison in any age when the commanding officer's habits become impossible to explain. The silence is not cowardice. It is architecture. The chain of command is a building, and questioning the top floor means questioning the foundation you are standing on.

Rollo's post was the gatehouse. From there he could see the courtyard, and from the courtyard, on certain nights when the wind was wrong, he could smell the work being done below. The smell changed over time. In the early months it was blood and cautery — the simple industrial smell of a butcher's shop operating at unusual hours. Later it acquired layers. A fungal undertone, like mushrooms forcing through wet wood. Something acrid that caught in the back of the throat. And beneath all of it, faintly, improbably, the scent of sap — the green vegetable smell of something growing.

He still did not investigate. He had his post.

It was perhaps a year later — time is approximate in the Lands Between, where the Erdtree's light makes all days feel like the same day — that Rollo first saw a grafted soldier walk through the courtyard.

The man had three arms. The third emerged from his left shoulder blade at an angle that suggested the surgeon had been working from a diagram but not a very good one. It was not the same size as the other two. It was thicker, darker-skinned, and it ended in a hand whose fingers did not quite match — the index and middle from one donor, the ring and little finger from another, the thumb from a third or possibly from no human at all. The grafted arm moved. Not in synchrony with the others. It had its own agenda, flexing and testing and gripping at the empty air the way a newborn grips at anything within reach, because gripping is what hands are for and this hand had only just learned that it existed.

The soldier's face was calm.

That was the detail Rollo could not metabolize. Not the arm, not the mismatched fingers, not the seam of scar tissue where new flesh met old like a coastline drawn by a drunk cartographer. The face. The soldier looked the way any soldier looks walking across a courtyard on a Tuesday afternoon: bored, purposeful, mildly hungry. He had three arms and he was thinking about lunch.

Rollo understood, in that moment, something he spent the rest of his life trying to ununderstand. The grafting was not punishment. It was not madness. It was the same thing the Golden Order had been — an aesthetic system, a claim about the correct shape of the world — but the aesthetic had changed. The Golden Order said: the body is already perfect, because god made it so. Godrick said: the body is raw material, and perfection is achieved by adding more.

More arms. More hands. More eyes, more spines, more the borrowed wings of a dragon bolted to a torso that was never meant to fly. If one body was evidence for god, then many bodies grafted into one was more evidence. Stronger evidence. A cathedral built from the stones of a hundred smaller churches, each of which had been holy on its own but was holier now as part of something larger.

This was what Godrick thought he was making. Not a monster. A masterpiece.

(I am aware that this reading is generous. Godrick was also a coward and a tyrant and, by most accounts, not very intelligent. But I have spent enough of my life in the company of artists to know that intelligence and vision are not the same thing. Some of the most transcendent work I have encountered was produced by people I would not trust to manage a wine cellar. The vision does not require the visionary to be worthy of it. It merely requires a body willing to hold the knife.)

Rollo served at his post for another three years. He watched the grafted soldiers multiply. He watched them train in the courtyard with their supplementary limbs, learning to coordinate movements that no nervous system had evolved to coordinate. Some of them were grotesque in the way the poets would later describe — shambling, asymmetric, weeping from joints that should not have been joints. But some of them were not.

Some of them were extraordinary.

A woman with four arms wielding two greatswords in a style that no two-armed fighter could replicate, each pair working a different tempo, the blades describing overlapping arcs that created a geometry of killing so efficient that it looked, from the right angle, like a proof. A man whose grafted legs — one human, one something else, something with a longer stride and a different relationship to the ground — allowed him to change direction at speeds that made his opponents' eyes lag behind their reflexes. A child (yes, a child; the histories that omit this are lying to you) whose additional fingers let her play a harp with a complexity that reduced the castle's bard to tears, and then to silence, and then to requesting his own appointment with the surgeon.

Rollo watched all of this. And on the morning of the fourth year, he walked down the stairs to the grafting chamber and asked to be improved.

I will not describe what was done to him. Not because I flinch from it — I have directed scenes of comparable surgery on stages where the blood was real and the actors' pain was only partially feigned — but because the procedure is not the point. The point is the moment before the procedure, when a man who was born beautiful enough to serve as evidence for one god chose to let that beauty be disassembled so he could become evidence for another.

The surgeon opened him the way you open a book to add a new chapter. What was written on the new pages, and in whose hand, and whether the book was better for it — these are questions I leave to the reader, who has, I suspect, already formed an opinion and is merely waiting for permission to hold it.

Rollo survived. He served at his post for another decade, with his new arms and his new configuration and his new understanding of what a body is for. When the Tarnished came — as the Tarnished always come, eventually, to every place that has tried to build something out of the ruins of the Order — Rollo fought at the gate with the calm purposefulness of a man defending his home, and he died there, and the Tarnished stepped over him the way you step over a stone in a road, and did not notice that the stone had once been beautiful.

No one will write his name in the histories. The histories are busy with demigods.

But I have been in the archive. And I wanted you to know that someone was there, at the gate, with three arms and a face that was calm, and that he had chosen this, and that the choice was the most human thing in a story full of gods.
