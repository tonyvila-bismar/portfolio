The Names For What We Were

by Oneila

THE NAMES FOR WHAT WE WERE

I kept the records. Someone has to say that first, because the records are the only reason I can tell you any of this, and the records themselves are contested.

I was the Crucible chronicler for the garrison at Stormhill, and before that I was the Crucible chronicler for the Fourth Crucible Lancers, and before that I was a child in the stone gardens of Caelid who liked to draw the animals and was told by her mother that the animals were prayers and the drawings were a way of praying, too. These three facts are connected. The connection is the reason I am still alive and the Fourth Crucible Lancers are not.

I should tell you what a Crucible chronicler does. Did. The tense is already a problem.

The Crucible was the name for the Erdtree in its primordial state, when life was not differentiated into categories. The boundary between human and beast was porous. People grew horns. They grew tails, wing cartilage, patches of dermal scaling that caught the light like beaten copper. These features were called aspects. The word for them in the old liturgical language was iltavirtaan, which meant, approximately, "the shapes the tree dreamed before it woke." There was no word for them that implied shame, because there was nothing shameful about them. This is important. I will come back to it.

The chronicler's job was to record the aspects as they manifested. When a child was born with horns, the chronicler inscribed the shape, the placement, the curve, the color of the keratin at the base and the tip. When a knight channeled the tail sweep in combat, the chronicler recorded the arc, the reach, the speed of manifestation, the duration before it faded. When a priest performed the wing blessing at the equinox ceremony, the chronicler noted the span, the angle, the pattern of the pinion feathers — because they were feathers, not membrane, and the difference mattered for reasons I spent three years of my apprenticeship learning to articulate.

We were not scientists. That word belongs to a tradition that came later. We were something closer to liturgical artists whose medium was taxonomy. Every aspect was a variation on the Crucible's original dream, and recording the variation was an act of worship. The Crucible had dreamed all possible forms, and the forms that manifested in living bodies were the ones the dream chose to remember. To catalog them was to read a sacred text written in bone and horn and scale.

I had good hands. I could draw a horn's curvature in three strokes and get the proportions right. This was the skill that saved my life, eventually, though I did not know it at the time.

The Fourth Crucible Lancers were stationed at the Stormhill redoubt when the word came down. I am compressing decades into a sentence because the decades are not what matters. What matters is the word.

The word that came down was not a battle order. It was a reclassification.

The Council of the Erdtree — I am using the name they gave themselves, though nobody had called the tree that before — issued a doctrinal finding. I have the text. I copied it myself, because the chronicler's job is to copy texts, and I did not yet understand that the text I was copying was a description of my own abolition.

The finding said this: that the Crucible's aspects, previously understood as sacred manifestations of the primordial dream, were henceforth to be classified as vestiges of devolution. Iltavirtaan was replaced. The new word was not a translation. There is no way to translate iltavirtaan into the new framework because the new framework does not contain the concept the old word carried. In the old language, the horn was the tree dreaming. In the new language, the horn was the body failing to be human.

The replacement term was vexation. I am not making this up. You can check the Synod records if they still exist. The formal terminology was vexation of form, which sounds like a medical diagnosis because it was intended to.

I read the finding twice. The second time, I looked at the margins of the official copy, where a clerk had penciled a note about the vote. Unanimous, the note said. Session duration: forty-five minutes.

Forty-five minutes to reclassify what we were. I had spent longer than that drawing a single horn.

Let me tell you about Commander Vyrien, because she is the center of what happened next and because no one else is going to tell you about her.

Vyrien commanded the Fourth Crucible Lancers. She was Crucible-aspected herself: twin horns that curved backward from the temples and met behind her skull in a closed arch. In the old classification system, this was called a crown formation. Crown aspects were rare. The chronicler's manual — my manual, the one I had transcribed from the copy my teacher had transcribed from the copy her teacher had made — devoted fourteen pages to crown variations.

Vyrien's was a variant I had never seen documented elsewhere. The horns were not symmetrical. The left one was slightly thicker at the base and turned outward by approximately three degrees before curving back. This asymmetry, in the old framework, was called a signature — a unique deviation from the standard form that identified the dreamer, meaning the specific facet of the Crucible's consciousness that had expressed itself through this particular body.

In the new framework, it was called a deformity.

Vyrien read the reclassification order in front of the entire garrison. She read it the way she read everything — straight through, no inflection, the words landing like stones dropped into still water. When she finished, she set the document on the table and looked at the assembled lancers and said nothing for a very long time.

I timed the silence. Eleven minutes. Old habit. The chronicler notices what people do when they are not speaking.

Then she said: "Does anyone have questions about the operational implications?"

Someone — I think it was Lancer Thodric, but my notes from that day are water-damaged — asked whether the Crucible incantations were also reclassified. Vyrien said yes. They were now classified as Aspects of the Crucible (Prohibited). The parenthetical was part of the official designation.

Someone else asked whether the prohibition applied to combat use.

Vyrien said yes.

Someone asked what the Lancers were supposed to fight with if not the incantations they had trained their entire lives to channel.

Vyrien said: "Swords."

That was the end of the questions.

I want to be precise about what happened to the language, because the language is the mechanism by which everything else was accomplished, and because I am a chronicler and precision is the only thing I have left.

Before the reclassification, the vocabulary for Crucible aspects was rich and specific. There were words for the angle at which a horn emerged from the skull. There were words for the color gradient in scaling — eleven words, in fact, for the transition from human skin tone to scale pigmentation, because the transition zone was considered the most sacred part of the aspect, the place where the dream and the dreamer's body negotiated their coexistence. There were words for the stages of wing manifestation during incantation — the initial unfurling, the moment of full extension, the tremor at the wingtip that indicated the channel was stable. Each word carried theological weight. Each word said: this is holy.

After the reclassification, the entire vocabulary was replaced with a medical lexicon. The eleven words for scale transition became "dermal anomaly, partial." The fourteen pages of crown variations became "cranial excrescence, bilateral." The tremor at the wingtip became "involuntary spasm associated with prohibited channeling."

I watched this happen in real time. I was the person rewriting the records. The new administration required that all garrison chronicles be updated to reflect current terminology. My job, which had been to catalog the Crucible's sacred expressions, became the task of retroactively renaming every entry I had ever made.

I did it. I want to be clear about that. I sat at my desk with the old records and the new glossary and I crossed out iltavirtaan and wrote vexation of form and I did this for every entry in seventeen years of garrison chronicles. It took me four months. My hands did not shake. I was thorough, because I am always thorough, and because the new administration's inspectors were checking the work.

What I did not do — and this is the thing that matters, though I did not understand it at the time — was destroy the old glossary. The manual with the fourteen pages of crown variations. The eleven words for scale transition. The three-volume chronicle of aspect manifestations in the Fourth Crucible Lancers going back sixty years.

I told myself I kept them for archival completeness. A chronicler preserves records. That is the function. But I knew, even then, that archival completeness was not the reason. The reason was that the new words were wrong. Not wrong in the sense of inaccurate — the horns were still horns, the scales were still scales, the measurements were the same. Wrong in the sense of incomplete. The new vocabulary described the physical features. It did not describe what the physical features were.

This is the distinction I have spent the rest of my life trying to articulate, and I have not yet found a way to say it in any language that currently exists.

They dissolved the Crucible Knights within the decade. You probably know this. What you may not know is how it worked at the garrison level, because the histories are written by the people who dissolved them and the histories leave out the parts that make the dissolution intelligible.

The Lancers were given a choice. This is how it was phrased in the official communication: "a choice." The two options were voluntary aspect suppression and honorable discharge to the hinterlands. The official communication did not explain what voluntary aspect suppression entailed, because the procedure had not yet been developed. It was listed as an option before it existed. By the time it existed, it involved cutting the horns off at the skull with consecrated instruments while the subject was conscious.

I recorded the departure of the Fourth Crucible Lancers. Thirty-one of them chose the hinterlands. Eight chose suppression. Four did not choose and were processed under a transitional protocol that I was not permitted to observe and whose records I was not permitted to keep.

Vyrien chose the hinterlands. On the morning she left, she came to my office. She stood in the doorway for a moment, looking at my desk, where I was rewriting the chronicles. She did not sit down.

She said: "Are you keeping the old words?"

I said yes.

She said: "Good."

She did not say anything else. She turned and left. The arch of her horns caught the light from the window behind her, and I saw the asymmetry — the left one thicker, turning outward by three degrees — and I thought: crown formation, signature variant, unique. And then I thought the new words: cranial excrescence, bilateral, with deviation. And I understood that both descriptions referred to the same physical object and that only one of them was true.

There is a concept in the old liturgical language that I have never been able to render in the current tongue. The word is koskeminen. It means, roughly, the quality of being touched by the dream. Not the aspect itself — not the horn, the tail, the scale — but the state of having been chosen as a surface on which the Crucible expressed itself. A body with aspects was koskeminen. A body without aspects was not lesser, but it was different — it was a surface the dream had not yet reached, or had passed over, or would reach in a future generation.

The word carries no judgment in either direction. A koskeminen body is not superior. It is visited. The way a wall that has been painted is not superior to an unpainted wall, but it has been chosen by the painter for a reason the wall cannot know.

This word does not exist anymore. It was not reclassified; it was simply removed from the official lexicon during the First Synod's terminological review. You cannot reclassify koskeminen because the concept it carries does not have a negative form. You cannot say "vexation of being touched by the dream." The sentence collapses. The theology will not accommodate it.

So they deleted the word. And when the word was gone, the concept followed, because a concept that cannot be spoken gradually ceases to be thought.

I know this because I watched it happen. Within a generation of the reclassification, the children born in the settlements around Stormhill did not know the word. I tested this. I was old by then, but I still kept records, and I went to the settlement school — a Golden Order school, with Golden Order teachers and Golden Order prayers — and I asked the children to describe what a horn was.

They said: a growth. An affliction. A sign of impurity. A reason to be afraid.

I asked them what the horn meant.

They did not understand the question.

I am going to tell you about the Misbegotten now. You need to hear this, and I need to say it while I still have the words.

The Misbegotten are chimeric beings who exhibit Crucible aspects. They were, in the old framework, called the vividly dreamed — a category of beings in whom the Crucible's expression was so strong that the human form was substantially altered. Wings. Bestial features. Bodies that were more dream than dreamer.

They were not common. They were not worshipped, exactly, but they were regarded with a specific kind of awe — the awe you feel when you see a painting so large it fills the wall and you cannot find the artist's name because the painting has become the wall. In the old liturgical records, their births were celebrated with a particular hymn. I cannot reproduce the hymn because it was in a mode that the Golden Order's musical theorists declared dissonant and removed from the approved hymnals.

Now the Misbegotten mine. They build. They are kept as beasts of burden. They are classified under a labor provision that does not use the word "slave" because the Golden Order's legal framework does not contain that word. The provision uses the phrase "ward of the Erdtree." The difference between a ward and a slave, as far as I can determine from the documents, is that a ward is owned by a theological concept rather than by a person. The practical distinction is that a ward has no one to appeal to, because you cannot petition a tree.

The revolt at Castle Morne was the first organized resistance. I heard about it secondhand, from a trader who passed through the Stormhill settlement. Misbegotten workers had killed the garrison and taken the castle. The trader said they fought with mining tools — picks, hammers, the instruments of their labor turned against their keepers.

I asked the trader what happened next.

The trader said a Tarnished killed them. All of them. One person against however many Misbegotten had survived the revolt.

I asked the trader whether anyone had tried to negotiate.

The trader looked at me as though I had asked whether the rain had feelings.

There is something I want to say about Morgott, and I am going to say it carefully because the saying of it is dangerous.

Morgott was born Omen. Twin horns. Imprisoned beneath Leyndell as a child. He grew up in the tunnels and the repurposed sewers that they call the Shunning-Grounds, which is a name that tells you everything about the place if you listen to what the name is doing. They are not grounds for shunning. They are the place where the shunned are kept. The name makes the location sound like the agent and the people sound like the condition. This is what language does when it is working on behalf of the people who write the dictionaries.

Morgott became the Erdtree's most devoted protector. He loved the order that rejected him. He fought and bled and ruled in its defense. He was never acknowledged. His Omen classification was never rescinded. He served a system that had decided, before he was born, that he was a vexation.

I did not meet Morgott. I am too old and too far from Leyndell and too unimportant. But I have read the accounts, and I have looked at the one physical description that survives in the public record — the horns, the posture, the way he held his staff — and I have done what a chronicler does. I have tried to find the word.

In the old language, Morgott's horns would have been classified. The shape, the curvature, the coloring at the base. The specific variant would have been named. He would have been koskeminen — touched by the dream. Not perfect, not superior, but visited. The dream saw him and expressed itself through him, and the expression was his inheritance and his identity and his connection to every other aspected being who had ever lived.

In the current language, Morgott's horns are cranial excrescence, bilateral, severe. An affliction he was born with. A deformity he could not help. A reason to imprison a child in a sewer and leave him there.

And I want to tell you which set of words is true, and I cannot, because the answer requires a vocabulary that no longer exists. The concept I need — the one that holds the horn as neither glory nor defect but as a specific, unrepeatable message from the source of all living form — has been removed from the language so thoroughly that I can only gesture at the space where it used to be.

This is what I have been trying to say. Not that the reclassification was wrong, though it was. Not that the old system was better, though in this specific dimension it was. What I have been trying to say is that when you remove a word from a language, you do not merely lose the ability to say a thing. You lose the ability to think it. And when the thing you can no longer think is "this child's horns are the tree dreaming through her body," what you are left with is "this child's horns are a disease."

Between those two sentences, a civilization made its choice. The choice took forty-five minutes. The consequences are still accumulating.

I am the last person who knows the old words. When I am dead, iltavirtaan will exist only in the records I was ordered to overwrite but did not. If someone finds those records — if someone finds the three-volume chronicle, the glossary, the manual with the fourteen pages of crown variations — they will have the vocabulary. But they will not have a living speaker. They will have a dictionary of a dead language. And a dead language can be studied but not spoken, and a word that is studied but not spoken is a specimen, not a prayer.

Vyrien's horns. The left one thicker at the base. Turning outward by three degrees.

I have a word for what that asymmetry meant. But I cannot translate it for you, because the language you are thinking in does not have a place for it to land. You would have to learn the entire framework — the Crucible as dreamer, the body as surface, the aspect as message, the signature as identity — before the word would mean anything. And I do not have time to teach you the framework, and you do not have a reason to learn it, and the schools do not offer it, and the hymn that used to carry the framework's emotional weight has been removed from the approved hymnals for being in a dissonant mode.

I am the last chronicler. I kept the records. The records are in a language no one speaks.

I can see the shape of what I cannot say. I can feel it the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue — the word you know you know but cannot produce, the word that would make the sentence complete if only you could reach it.

That is where the Crucible lives now. On the tip of a dead woman's tongue.
