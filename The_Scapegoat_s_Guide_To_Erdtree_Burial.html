The Scapegoat's Guide To Erdtree Burial

by Sidonei ne Sangbelles

The Scapegoat's Guide to Erdtree Burial

Sidonei ne Sangbelles

Look, I know what you're going to say — "Sidonei, you can't possibly compare your exile from Sangbelles to a headless knight strapped to a walking mausoleum for eternity, that's absurd, that's self-aggrandizing, that's pathologically dramatic even by your standards" — and you're right, obviously, the mausoleum knight had it worse in at least three measurable ways, not least that decapitation thing, but here's what I need you to understand: the mechanism is identical.

The mechanism is: you give everything to an institution. The institution takes your head. The institution keeps walking.

I've been thinking about this because I spent last week in the Altus Plateau — not the capital proper, you understand, I'm not suicidal, just the fringes where the merchant caravans still run and a man with the right connections and the wrong reputation can buy fireweed at merely outrageous rather than impossible prices — and I saw one of the Mausoleums from a distance. Felt it first, actually. The ground does this thing, this rhythmic shudder, like the heartbeat of something that forgot to die. The merchants barely looked up. "Oh, that's just Old Stumbler," one of them said. "Comes through every few weeks. Don't stand in its shadow or you'll have bad luck for a month."

Old Stumbler.

They named it. They named the massive ambulatory tomb of a soulless demigod the way you'd name a cow that keeps getting into the vegetable garden. And the headless knights still circling its base, shields up, wing ornaments catching what passes for light in that ash-choked excuse for a sky — those poor bastards don't even know they've become local color. They severed their own heads. That's the part that gets me. Nobody held them down. Nobody forced the blade. They looked at their soulless lord, a demigod whose soul was ripped away by Destined Death, a being that may never come back, that almost certainly will never come back, and they said "yes, I will follow you into this, I will cut away the part of me that thinks and sees and chooses, and I will guard your empty body forever."

And the Golden Order called it heroic.

The Golden Order — and "Order" is doing a lot of work in that phrase, propping up about six different lies simultaneously like a load-bearing wall in a condemned building — the Golden Order loved that kind of devotion. Loved it the way a fire loves kindling. Lhutel the Headless earned Erdtree Burial for it. Erdtree Burial! The highest honor the faith could bestow. She sacrificed her life so she could keep protecting a demigod who has no soul, who cannot recognize her, who cannot reward her, who is for all practical biological purposes a very fancy corpse, and the institution looked at that and said "yes, this, more of this please, wrap it in gold and hold it up as an example."

You know who else they buried with honor? Kristoff, the ancient dragon knight. Captured Godefroy the Grafted during the First Defense of Leyndell. Fine. Legitimate military achievement. But Lhutel didn't defeat anyone. Lhutel just... stayed. She committed to an act of devotion so total and so pointless that it became, by the sheer weight of its absurdity, sacred.

I respect it. I do. I'm not mocking her.

I'm mocking the people who used her.

Because here's the trick — and I know this trick, I've seen it performed from every angle, I've been the volunteer sawed in half — the trick is that the institution gets to define what sacrifice means after you've already made it. You cut off your head? Heroic. You question whether the head-cutting was necessary? Heresy. You point out that the soulless demigod you're guarding might have been rendered soulless by the very same divine order that's now handing out burial honors? You are an enemy of the faith, you are a blasphemer, you are "exiled" (there it is, my favorite scare-quoted word, never gets old, never stops tasting like bile).

Morgott understood this. I think about Morgott more than is probably healthy for a man with my specific catalog of resentments. Born Omen. Horned. Accursed. Shoved underground as a child because the Golden Order decided that Crucible traits — traits that were divine in an earlier age, that the Crucible Knights channeled as the living breath of primordial creation — were impure. The horns that once meant "touched by god" now meant "hide it in the sewers." And Morgott, this magnificent fool, this beautiful idiot, crawled out of the dark and dedicated his entire existence to defending the Erdtree that would never acknowledge him.

"He loved not in return, for he was never loved, but nevertheless, love it he did."

I've read that description eleven times. I've read it drunk and I've read it sober and I've read it at three in the morning lying on a borrowed bedroll in a Liurnian cave that smelled like bat guano and blue cuckoo feathers, and every single time it hits the same way, which is like a fist to the sternum followed by the slow realization that you can't breathe.

Because — and here's where the metaphor is going to get away from me and I'm just going to let it this time, I'm too tired to wrestle it back — because I know what it is to love something that will never love you back. I don't mean a person. People at least have the decency to look you in the eye when they betray you. I mean a system. A structure. The canopy of a living tower, the way the light filtered through in the mornings, the smell of — no. Not doing this. Not tonight.

The point. The point is Morgott.

Morgott defended Leyndell alone. Let that sink in. The other demigods were off collecting Great Runes and waging their little wars of succession and feeding themselves to serpents and kidnapping their half-brothers and generally behaving like the cosmic equivalent of toddlers fighting over a toy that's already broken, and Morgott was standing at the gates of the capital with his cursed blood and his sawed-off stumps of pride, holding the line. He even went out in disguise — showed up at Stormveil as Margit the Fell Omen, a false name, a mask over a mask — to stop Tarnished from even getting close.

And nobody thanked him.

The citizens of Leyndell sealed their doors with yellow wax and sat in the ash-dark of their golden city and they never once, not once, looked at the Omen King on their throne and said "we see you, we know what you are, and we are grateful." They couldn't. Acknowledging Morgott's sacrifice would mean acknowledging that their Order was broken, that the system that classified Omen as subhuman was the same system that relied on an Omen to save it, and that contradiction — that gorgeous, nauseating, load-bearing contradiction — was the one thing the Golden Order could never, ever permit to be spoken aloud.

So he fought. And he fell. And history will record him as a footnote at best and a monster at worst.

I met a man in Riverstone once — this was years ago, back when I was still doing blood work out of a rented basement, before I got pushed out of that racket too — who told me he'd served in a lord's army for thirty years and been discharged without pension because a new commander decided his unit was "politically inconvenient." Thirty years. Three decades of service, and the institution crumpled up his life like a piece of scrap parchment and tossed it. You know what he was doing when I met him? Selling salvaged armor pieces at a night market. Polishing them first. Making sure the insignia was clean. He was still proud of the army that threw him away.

I sold him a tincture for his bad knee at cost. Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation to maintain.

But I recognized something in his eyes. That particular quality of devotion that has survived its own betrayal and come out the other side not as cynicism but as something harder to name. Morgott had it. The mausoleum knights have it. It's not faith — faith implies the possibility of reward. It's not duty — duty implies a contract. It's something closer to biological compulsion, like the way a vine will grow toward light even when the light source is a fire that will burn it, even when every tendril that reaches the flame comes back blackened and curled, the vine keeps growing because growing toward light is what vines do, it's encoded in the tissue, in the cells themselves, and you can't cut it out without killing the whole organism, and —

The metaphor got a little away from me there.

Here's what I actually want to talk about. The Erdtree is dead.

I know. Everyone knows. Nobody says it. The whole golden canopy that dominates the sky of the Lands Between is a spectral projection — an afterimage of a tree that already burned once. The evidence is everywhere if you bother to look, which nobody does, because looking would mean the entire theological basis of the Golden Order is propped up on a ghost, and then what? Then the wars were pointless. Then the exiles were pointless. Then Morgott died defending a gravestone.

I've done enough biological work to understand what happens to an organism after death. The physical processes are straightforward. Cells lose coherence. Structures collapse. The chemical architecture that maintained identity breaks down into constituent parts that get recycled into something else — soil, nutrients, the foundation for new growth. That's honest. That's how nature works. Death feeds life. The old becomes the new. There's a brutal elegance to it that I've always found more comforting than any golden prayer.

But the Erdtree doesn't get to do that. The Erdtree is held in a state of false permanence — dead but appearing alive, gone but still glowing — because the people who built their power on its existence cannot afford to admit it's over. And so the ash piles up, and the sap stops flowing, and the golden seeds fall from illusory trees, and everyone acts as if this is fine, this is the natural order, this is how it has always been and will always be.

And anyone who says otherwise gets —

Well. You know what happens to them.

I think about the Omen sometimes. Not just Morgott and Mohg but all of them — the unnamed ones, the ones born with horns in Leyndell's lower wards, the ones who had their horns sawed off as children by surgeons who told themselves it was mercy. Crucible traits. Vestiges of primordial life. The oldest expression of divinity in the Lands Between, predating the Erdtree, predating the Golden Order, predating every system that would later declare them monstrous.

You want to talk about scapegoats? The Omen are the original. Every society needs its category of the impure — the thing that must be rejected so that the rest can call themselves clean. For the Golden Order it was the Crucible. The mingling of forms. Horns and tails and feathers and scales, all the gorgeous chaotic evidence that life was once bigger than the narrow channel they forced it into. The Order looked at the full spectrum of existence and said "no, only this. Only the human form as we define it. Everything else is contamination."

And the Dung Eater — and I can't believe I'm about to say something that isn't entirely contemptuous about the Dung Eater, this is what happens when you drink alone and think too hard — the Dung Eater looked at that and had the one insight nobody else would permit themselves to have: if the curse is the problem, make everyone cursed. If defilement is the category of exclusion, make defilement universal. "For every curse, a cursed blessing." It's monstrous. It's an act of such comprehensive violence that it makes the blood drain from your face just thinking about it. And it is, structurally, the most egalitarian proposal anyone in the Lands Between has ever made.

Which tells you something about the Lands Between.

I'm not advocating for it. I'm not advocating for anything. Advocacy is for people who still believe the system can be addressed through its own mechanisms, and I lost that particular delusion somewhere between my third exile and my fourth black-market basement. I'm just telling you what I see. I'm just describing the shape of the cage so precisely that for a moment you can feel the bars.

The nomadic merchants understood. They had a whole culture — the Great Caravan, gems in every color decorating their hats, trade routes across the entire continent — and the Golden Order looked at them and decided they were heretics. Rounded up the whole clan. Buried them alive. Underground, in the dark, beneath the foundations of the civilization that killed them, those merchants chanted a curse of despair and summoned the Frenzied Flame.

The most destructive force in the Lands Between — the power that would burn all of existence back to undifferentiated nothing, that would annihilate every distinction, every boundary, every law of nature — exists because a religious institution could not tolerate difference.

Let me say that again, because it's important and because I want to make sure the irony is landing with appropriate force: the Golden Order, which defines itself through order, through hierarchy, through the meticulous categorization of what is sacred and what is profane, created the one force capable of destroying all categories. They generated their own apocalypse. They buried it alive and it grew.

If I were a better man I would find that tragic. I find it hilarious.

(I also find it terrifying, and the two reactions are not mutually exclusive, and if you think they are, you have never been buried alive by anything, which — good for you, honestly, I mean that, enjoy it while it lasts.)

I sell potions now. Did I mention that? Herbal compounds, mostly. Biological modifications. Blood tests when the client can afford it. I work with what I can get — cave mushrooms instead of proper reagents, fireweed from the plateau instead of the materials I was trained on, jury-rigged techniques assembled from five different traditions none of which I formally belong to anymore. It works. Not elegantly. Not the way it would work if I had access to proper facilities, proper ingredients, proper institutional backing. But it works, and the fact that it works with substitutes proves that most of what the proper institutions guard is not knowledge but access.

That's the demystification. That's the trick behind the curtain. Every great institution — the Academy, the Golden Order, the Carian courts, all of them — maintains its power not through superior understanding but through controlled scarcity. They don't know more than you. They have more than you. The knowledge is in the world. The reagents grow in the ground. The techniques can be learned by anyone with hands and patience and a sufficiently reckless disregard for personal safety. What the institutions control is the door.

And what they do, when someone figures this out, when someone starts practicing outside the walls, when someone demonstrates that the sacred knowledge is just knowledge —

They call you a heretic. They call you dangerous. They find the thing you did wrong, the one mistake, the inevitable error that comes from working without a safety net, and they hold it up and they say "look, look at what happens when you let the wrong people have access," and they exile you, and the door stays closed, and the institution survives.

Thops. Let me tell you about Thops.

A sorcerer. Couldn't afford the Academy's entrance fee. Sat outside studying alone. Developed Thops's Barrier — a genuine breakthrough, a forcefield that deflects sorceries, a contribution to magical knowledge that future generations will study in their curricula. The Academy sneered at him. He died trying to get in. They found his body with his bell bearing still clutched in his hand.

"Future generations will learn," the description says. "They will know the foolishness of the sneering sorcerers who ridiculed this theory, little realizing that it was in fact a discovery worthy of a new conspectus."

He was right. Nobody cared.

I think about Thops and I think about myself and I think about every person who ever figured out the trick only to discover that knowing the trick doesn't open the door, it gets the door bolted tighter, because the institution would rather lose the knowledge than lose control.

But I didn't come here to talk about me. I came here to talk about Morgott.

No. That's not true either. I came here to talk about devotion that outlives its object. About the mausoleum knights circling their empty lords. About Morgott defending a dead tree. About Finlay — Cleanrot Knight Finlay, who carried the unconscious Malenia across the entire continent, alone, from the rotting hellscape of Caelid to the Haligtree, a journey that would have taken weeks at minimum, through hostile territory, with a goddess of plague on her back. Finlay never wavered. Finlay's flesh was already putrefying from the rot. The Cleanrot Knights knew what serving Malenia meant — their bodies would decay, their armor would corrode, they would be consumed by the very thing they swore to protect — and they chose it anyway, and the acceptance of that fate made them the fiercest warriors in the Lands Between.

There's a vine that grows in Liurnia — I found it during one of my lean years, when I was sleeping in the ruins of a half-submerged village and eating whatever I could pull from the lake. This vine wraps itself around dead trees. Specifically dead trees. It can tell the difference. Something in the chemical signature of the bark — the absence of sap flow, maybe, or the particular mineral composition of wood that has stopped metabolizing — tells the vine "this one is empty, this one has no life of its own," and the vine grows toward it, embraces it, covers it so completely that from a distance you'd think the tree was alive. Green leaves. Flexible branches. The appearance of vitality. But pull back the vine and the trunk crumbles under your hand.

The vine is not parasitic, exactly. It's not feeding on the dead wood. It's using the structure. The height, the spread of branches, the access to sunlight that a tree-sized scaffold provides. The vine gets everything it needs — elevation, light, a framework for growth — and in return the dead tree gets to look alive for a while longer.

I sat in that ruined village with lake water lapping at my ankles and I stared at that vine-covered tree for a long time. Longer than was wise, given that the Cuckoo soldiers patrolled that stretch and they were not known for their mercy toward trespassers. But I couldn't stop looking at it. The collaboration between the living and the dead. The way both got something from the arrangement, even if what the dead thing got was only the illusion of —

Right. Right. The metaphor. I know.

Here's what I actually think, and I'm only going to say it once and then I'm going to go back to being furious, which is easier and requires less of me:

I think the mausoleum knights know their lords are empty. I think Morgott knew the Erdtree was dead. I think Finlay knew Malenia would bloom again and that the rot would win eventually and that the Haligtree was already failing. I think they all knew. And I think the knowing is what makes it sacred.

Not the faith. Not the hope. Not the expectation of reward or recognition or even survival. The knowing. The full, clear-eyed comprehension that the thing you are devoted to is broken, is gone, is a spectral projection of something that burned — and the choice to stay anyway. Because the alternative is to walk away, and walking away means admitting that the devotion was wasted, and admitting the devotion was wasted means the only thing you have left is yourself, and yourself is not enough. Yourself was never enough. You became enough only in the act of serving, and without the thing you served, you are just a person standing in a field with no direction and no purpose and no one calling your name.

I wouldn't know anything about that.

I sell potions. I use substitutes. I keep the spite engine running because spite is renewable and self-generating and it gets you out of bed in the morning when nothing else will. And if I sometimes find myself staring at a vine-covered dead tree in a flooded ruin and feeling something I don't have a scientific term for, something that sits in the chest like a stone and won't dissolve no matter how much bile I throw at it —

Well.

The nomadic merchants are still out there, you know. The survivors. Sitting at their little campfires, selling their wares, tiny gems in every color on their hats. They don't discuss what happened to their people. They just keep trading. They light their fires and they play their instruments and they offer you goods at reasonable prices and they do not, ever, in any conversation I've had with any of them, mention the clan that was buried alive beneath the world.

But their eyes. If you've been paying attention — and I have been paying attention for a very long time, because paying attention is the one skill exile can't take from you — their eyes are the eyes of people who know exactly what the Golden Order is and choose, every morning, to set up their stall anyway. To keep selling. To keep the caravan moving, even when the caravan is one donkey and a tattered tent and a collection of goods that nobody in a burning world particularly needs.

That's not faith. That's not hope. That's the other thing. The thing I don't have a word for. The thing the mausoleum knights have, circling endlessly. The thing Morgott had, standing at the gates. The thing I —

I sell potions. The cave mushrooms are adequate. The fireweed works fine.

And if anyone from Sangbelles is reading this — which you're not, because you stopped paying attention to me twenty years ago, which is exactly my point and always has been — I want you to know that the vine grows just as well on a dead tree as a living one. Better, maybe. The dead tree can't fight back.

Make of that what you will. I've got a client at sundown who needs a blood test and my best reagents are two weeks past optimal potency, so I'll be improvising, which is to say I'll be doing what I always do, which is to say I'll be alive out of sheer stubbornness in a world that keeps auditing its guest list and finding my name absent.

Morgott would understand. That's the worst part.

He would have understood completely.
