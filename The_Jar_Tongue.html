The Jar Tongue

by Oneila

The thing about the jars is that they talk.

Not figuratively. Not the way a ruin talks if you read it right, or the way a sword talks when you hold it and feel the balance point somebody chose. The living jars of the Lands Between open their lids and words come out.

I found the first one on a ridge above Stormhill. He was wedged between two rocks, rocking himself loose with a motion that involved his entire body — a controlled oscillation, left-right-left, each swing wider than the last, pottery scraping stone with a sound like a millwheel. His name, he told me, was Bairn. He was small. Roughly the height of my hip. His voice came from somewhere inside the vessel, muffled and resonant in the way that all speech inside a chamber is resonant, the consonants softened and the vowels deepened by the acoustics of his own body.

"You're a person," I said.

He stopped rocking. Considered this. "Parts of several," he said.

I sat down.

Here's what I knew about the jars before I met one. The living jars of the Lands Between are animate vessels — ceramic bodies containing organic remains. The remains are not random. The jars select for warriors. Strong fighters, skilled fighters, the recently dead whose bodies still carry whatever quality the jars can detect and we cannot name. The jars absorb the remains and become, in some functional sense, the people they contain. Not a resurrection. Something more like a digest.

That word kept coming back. Digest.

Bairn freed himself from the rocks while I was thinking. He did it with the patience of someone who has been stuck before and will be stuck again and does not consider either event remarkable. When he was loose he rolled — not walked, not shuffled, rolled — toward me with a momentum that suggested he weighed considerably more than his size would indicate.

"You're from outside," he said.

"What makes you think that?"

"You sat down."

I waited.

"People here don't sit near us. They fight us or they walk past. You sat down like you want to talk." He paused. The pause was physical — a slight backward tilt, his lid lifting a centimeter, then settling. "Nobody wants to talk."

I told him I was a linguist. He didn't know the word. I told him I studied how people speak. He said, "Oh. Like a Finger Reader."

Not like a Finger Reader. But I didn't correct him because the comparison told me something: in his world, the category of person-who-studies-communication was occupied by the Finger Readers, which meant communication itself was understood as a form of divination rather than a form of exchange. Language wasn't a tool for the jars. It was something that happened to them.

This turned out to be exactly right.

I traveled with Bairn for six days. He was headed south, toward a place he called the Aqueduct, where other jars gathered. He moved slowly — the rolling gait covered ground but required flat terrain, and Stormhill is not flat — and the pace gave me time to listen.

His speech was fractured. Not incoherent. Fractured the way a mosaic is fractured: individual pieces that are each smooth and finished, assembled into a pattern that makes sense from a distance but up close reveals the gaps between the tiles.

He would begin a sentence in one register — clear, childish, direct — and finish it in another. Mid-word sometimes. The shift was audible. The pitch would drop. The vocabulary would jump two literacy levels. A sentence that started "I don't like the cold" would end "— the thermal differential degrades adhesive integrity of the binding agent."

The first time it happened I stopped walking.

"What?" he said.

"You just changed."

"Changed what?"

"The way you talk. You started a sentence as yourself and finished it as someone who knows materials science."

He tilted. The backward-tilt thing. "That happens."

I asked who the materials scientist was. He said he didn't know. He said the remains inside him came from seven people and he didn't know all of their names. Two were soldiers. One was a craftsman — that was the materials science voice. One was a woman who sang. He knew she sang because sometimes he hummed melodies he had never learned, and the melodies had words in a language he didn't recognize, and the words had a grammatical structure that was unlike any language spoken in Limgrave.

I asked him to hum one.

He did. The melody was simple — five notes, repeated, with a variation on the third repetition that moved the tonal center up a half-step. The words, when he produced them, were in a dialect I couldn't immediately place. Coastal, maybe. The phonemic inventory included a lateral fricative that doesn't appear in any Limgrave dialect I've documented. Wherever this woman came from, it wasn't here.

The jar was a library. An involuntary, unsorted, partially corrupted library.

I spent the next four days running tests. Informal ones — I had no instruments except my ears and my notebook. I would ask Bairn questions designed to trigger specific registers: technical questions to draw out the craftsman, emotional questions to surface the singer, tactical questions to activate the soldiers. The switches were not instant. There was a latency. Sometimes thirty seconds, sometimes a full minute, during which Bairn would go quiet and his body would settle lower, the ceramic compressing slightly against the ground, as though the answer required physical effort — not effort of thought but effort of retrieval, the way a body strains when you ask it to reach a high shelf.

The soldiers were the easiest to trigger. Any mention of combat, defensive positioning, or blade maintenance would produce a shift in Bairn's posture — a forward lean, a squaring of the shoulders that a jar does not technically have but that the body somehow communicated anyway — and a voice that was deeper, clipped, imperative. The soldiers didn't discuss. They assessed. "Defensible terrain," one of them said through Bairn, looking at a ridge I had pointed to as a possible campsite. "Two approaches, both visible. Acceptable."

I tried to engage the soldier voice in conversation. It wasn't interested. Soldiers assess; they don't chat.

The craftsman emerged for different triggers. Material properties, structural questions, anything involving how a thing was made rather than how it was used. The craftsman's voice was measured, precise, fond of qualifications. "The binding agent is resinous," the craftsman said once, when I asked how the jar held together. "Partially organic. I believe — though I should note that my expertise is in metalwork, not ceramics — that the resin undergoes a slow crystallization over decades, which is why the older jars are harder and the younger ones crack."

Fascinating. Also terrifying, if you thought about it from the craftsman's perspective — a man whose professional knowledge survived his death and was now being deployed by a ceramic vessel to describe its own structural integrity. But I did not say this to Bairn. The jar did not seem troubled by the arrangement.

The singer was the hardest to reach.

She surfaced only twice in four days. The first time, I hummed the melody back to Bairn and he went very still — the absolute stillness of a jar that has stopped making its constant micro-adjustments, which is the jar equivalent of holding your breath. The woman's voice emerged. Not singing this time but speaking, in that coastal dialect, a sentence I partially translated as "the salt is in the wrong direction" or possibly "the tide has turned against the house."

The grammar was ambiguous. Coastal dialects often are. They develop in communities where the environment changes twice a day with the tides, and the language reflects that mutability — tense markers that indicate whether the tide is in or out, prepositions that shift meaning depending on the time of day. A sentence in a coastal dialect is never fully translatable without knowing when it was spoken. The singer's sentence, spoken through a jar in a landlocked hillside at midday, was orphaned from its temporal context. I could approximate its meaning. I could not fix it.

This is, incidentally, the fundamental problem of the jars. The people inside them were spoken in a context — a life, a body, a place. The jar preserves the words but not the context. What you get is signal without noise, which sounds like an improvement until you realize that noise is what tells you where the signal came from.

The second time the singer surfaced was at night, when Bairn was — I don't know what to call it. Sleeping seems wrong. Jars don't close their eyes. But there was a period of stillness each night where his lid would settle flat, his body would stop its minor adjustments and corrections, and the only sound was the faint tick of ceramic cooling in the night air. During one of these periods I heard humming. The five-note melody. Quiet. The singer, singing to no one, in a body that was not hers, in a place she had not chosen.

I wrote the melody down in notation. It didn't help.

On the fifth day we reached the Aqueduct and I met Alexander.

Alexander was large. The height of my chest, which for a jar is enormous. He had arms — small ones, vestigial-looking, protruding from the sides of the vessel — and a voice that was completely unified. No fracture. No register switching. No involuntary retrievals from undigested remains. Alexander spoke in one voice, and that voice was his.

I noticed this immediately. It was, given everything I had observed with Bairn, the most remarkable thing about him.

"You've integrated," I said.

"What a peculiar thing to say to someone you've just met."

"I've been traveling with Bairn."

"Ah." Alexander's lid — larger, heavier, sitting with the authority of a thing that has been fired at higher temperatures — tilted forward in a motion I was beginning to recognize as the jar equivalent of a nod. "Then you've heard the others."

"Seven of them."

"I contain considerably more than seven."

"How many?"

"I stopped counting at a certain point. It seemed rude." He paused. Alexander's pauses were different from Bairn's. Bairn's pauses were retrievals — the body searching for the right voice. Alexander's pauses were choices — a single mind selecting from a vast repertoire. "I will say that integration is not automatic. It requires effort. Intention. The remains must be — how to put this — composted."

The metaphor was agricultural, which surprised me.

"You digest them," I said.

"I incorporate them. Digestion implies that something is lost. Nothing is lost. But the boundaries between them dissolve. The soldier's reflexes and the craftsman's material knowledge and the singer's melodies — in Bairn, these are separate rooms in one house. In me, they are a single room with many windows."

"And the people they were?"

"Are me." He said this without hesitation. "They were themselves. They are me. Both statements are true. Language lacks a tense for this."

He was right. I tried to construct one in my notebook that evening — a tense that indicates a state of being that was once distributed across multiple subjects and is now unified in one. The closest analogue I could find was in the Langovin possessive-collective, which Sangbelles uses for institutional memory: a grammatical form that assigns ownership of a memory to a group rather than an individual. But even that wasn't right, because the Langovin form preserves the multiplicity. Alexander's form dissolved it.

The jars had invented a new relationship between self and other, and they had no word for it. They had no word for it because the people who had words — the scholars, the linguists, the Golden Order's taxonomists — had never asked a jar what it felt like to be one.

I stayed at the Aqueduct for three more days. There were eleven jars gathered there, ranging from Bairn's size to one enormous specimen who had been rolling the same circuit around the aqueduct's perimeter for what the others estimated was three years. The large one did not speak. The others said it used to but stopped. They said this the way you say someone moved away — matter-of-factly, without alarm, as though silence were a place you could go.

I documented the speech patterns of five jars. The data was consistent. The younger or less integrated jars — the ones with fewer remains, or more recent remains, or remains that resisted composting for whatever reason — showed the fracture pattern: multiple registers, involuntary switching, retrievals that surprised the jar as much as the listener. The older, larger, more integrated jars spoke in unified voices that drew from their components without being controlled by them.

The intermediate cases were the most interesting. One jar — medium-sized, four or five remains — had developed a stutter. Not a speech impediment. A negotiation. Two voices would try to answer simultaneously, and the jar would produce the first syllable of both responses overlapping, then pause, then choose one. The pause was the jar deciding which of its selves had more relevant expertise. It was, essentially, running an internal committee meeting every time someone asked it a question.

I thought about how the Foiskola faculty senate operates and decided the jar was more efficient.

Alexander gave me one more piece before I left. I asked him about the warrior-selection. Why warriors? Why not scholars, farmers, artists?

He rolled forward — the full-body lean that, in a jar, functions as stepping closer. "Who told you we select warriors?"

"Everyone."

"Everyone who has watched us from outside. Have you considered that what looks like selection is actually compatibility? Some remains integrate. Some do not. We take what we can hold. Warrior remains hold because warriors are already accustomed to losing themselves — in combat, in the unit, in the purpose of the fight. Their boundaries are thinner. A farmer who has never been anything but herself does not survive the integration. Not because she is weaker. Because she is more intact."

I wrote that down. I am still writing it down. A person whose identity is porous is easier to absorb than one whose identity is solid, and this has nothing to do with strength and everything to do with practice. Warriors practice dissolution. Scholars practice coherence. The jars are not selecting for combat skill. They are selecting for a prior willingness to stop being one thing and become part of something else.

The word Alexander used — and he was very specific about this, correcting me twice — was not "absorb" or "consume" or "digest." It was "welcome." The jar welcomes the remains. The remains, if they are compatible, welcome the jar.

Nobody had asked. In all the centuries that the living jars have rolled through the Lands Between, nobody with the tools to understand what was happening inside them had thought to sit down and ask. The Golden Order classified them as animated vessels, which is technically accurate and completely useless, the way calling a library a "building full of paper" is technically accurate.

I thought about that on my last night at the Aqueduct, sitting against a broken pillar while the jars settled into their cooling-silence. Eleven of them, arranged in a loose circle the way any group arranges itself at rest — not by plan but by the accumulated habits of proximity. Bairn was nearest to me. Alexander was in the center, which I suspect he always was.

The Golden Order's taxonomists had a system for everything. Omen. Misbegotten. Albinauric. Every non-standard form of life in the Lands Between had been classified, catalogued, and assigned a place in the hierarchy. The jars had been classified too: "animated vessels, sub-category: warrior collection, threat assessment: moderate, theological status: unclear."

Theological status: unclear. That was the interesting part. The Golden Order could not decide whether the jars had souls. If the remains inside them had souls, those souls should have returned to the Erdtree through the death cycle. But the remains were in the jars. The souls — if souls they were — were in the jars. The jars were, from a theological perspective, a leak in the system. Death that didn't flow back to the tree. Souls that stayed in the world instead of recycling through it.

No wonder nobody asked them questions. The answers might have been inconvenient. The answers might have required reclassifying "animated vessel" as "person," and the Golden Order had a long and well-documented history of preferring its classifications to its evidence.

I left the Aqueduct on the ninth day. Bairn walked me to the edge of the settlement — rolled, rather, with that patient lateral motion that covered ground at the cost of constant correction. At the boundary he stopped.

"Will you come back?" he asked.

Not the soldier's voice. Not the craftsman. Not the singer. Just Bairn. The youngest voice, the one that was still assembling itself from pieces, the one that didn't know yet which room it would live in or whether the rooms would dissolve.

I said I would. He tilted back, settled, and rolled toward the Aqueduct.

The singer hummed as he went. Five notes, repeated, with the variation on the third pass. The lateral fricative caught the morning air. Somewhere inside a ceramic body in a broken world, a woman from a coastal village I will never find was singing a song about the tide.

I have nine languages. I do not have a word for what the jars are.

I have a melody, though. And a notebook full of register shifts. And the specific sound of pottery on stone, which is the sound of someone who is several people trying to go in one direction, which is, if you think about it, not so different from the rest of us.
