The Conservator's Report

by Eitan

K.J. Parker

The trick to removing a consecration seal without destroying the underlying pigment is patience and a very thin blade. I mention this because I have neither, and the job needs doing by tomorrow.

Let me back up.

My name is Eudo, and I am—or was, before bureaucratic misfortune intervened—a conservator of wall paintings for the Archive of Public Memory in Leyndell. That title sounds grand until you learn what it actually entails, which is mostly scraping mold off plaster in damp vaults while officials argue about whether the images underneath are worth preserving. The answer, for the record, is always yes. Nobody ever listens.

The Archive occupies a series of underground chambers beneath what used to be the old Fundament of the Golden Order, back when the Golden Order had a fundament and not just a lingering sense of embarrassment. Now the building above us houses a tax office and a granary, and we operate on sufferance and a quarterly appropriation that would make a goatherd weep. I have four assistants, two of whom are competent, and a workshop that leaks in three places. We get by.

The work itself is good. I'll say that plainly, because people assume conservators are failed artists, and some of us are, but I was never an artist in the first place. I don't create images. I keep them from dying. There's a meaningful difference, and if you can't see it, you've probably never watched a fresco from the Age of the Crucible dissolve to powder because some amateur tried to fix it with egg wash and good intentions.

The materials are straightforward. Lime plaster—three parts sand to one part slaked lime, mixed wet, applied in thin coats. You paint into the wet plaster, which means the pigment bonds chemically as the lime carbonates. This is called buon fresco, and it lasts essentially forever, unless someone builds a drainage channel through it, which happens more often than you'd think. For repairs I use a secco technique—pigment mixed with lime water, applied over the dry surface—which is inferior but serviceable. The colors fade faster. The bond is weaker. But you can match the original palette if you're careful, and if you can get the pigments, which is increasingly difficult since the trade routes through Caelid became, shall we say, unreliable.

I had Egyptian blue, once. A whole jar. Lost it in the reorganization of '14, when they moved us to the lower vaults to make room for grain storage. Grain storage. In a facility designed for the preservation of irreplaceable cultural artifacts. But the Interim Council needed somewhere to put the barley, and barley, apparently, outranks the entire visual record of human civilization. I found the jar six months later, shattered, behind a row of sacks. The blue had mixed with rainwater and dried to a crust. Thirty years of supply contacts, gone.

I'm digressing. I do that. My second wife used to say I could make a straight road crooked just by walking it, which was unfair but not technically wrong.

So. The job.

Three days ago, a messenger arrived from the Interim Council—which is what we call the government now, since nobody wants to use the old titles and nobody can agree on new ones. The Interim Council governs by committee, which means nothing gets done quickly and everything gets done badly. The messenger was a young woman with the look of someone who'd been told this errand was beneath her and believed it. She handed me a sealed document and left without waiting for a response.

The document informed me that a wall painting had been discovered in a sealed chamber beneath the old Colosseum district, exposed by recent subsidence. Initial assessment suggested Crucible-period origin, which would make it roughly contemporary with the oldest surviving figurative art in the capital. The Interim Council wished it examined, documented, and—here was the interesting part—evaluated for potential removal or destruction.

Destruction. Of a Crucible-period fresco.

I read the document twice. Then a third time, because I was certain I'd missed a qualifying clause. Some official's idea of a joke, perhaps, or a copying error—"preservation" misread as "destruction" by a scribe with bad light and worse Latin. But no. The language was precise. The council wanted to know whether the painting depicted content that was "inconsistent with the public interest as presently understood," and if so, whether it could be removed without structural damage to the chamber, or, failing that, destroyed in situ.

I put the document down and went to make tea. When I came back, it still said the same thing.

Now, you need to understand something about the Crucible period to see why this made my hands cold. The Crucible was—how do I put this—the era before things got organized. Before the Golden Order decided what forms were acceptable and what forms were abomination. In the Crucible, life was promiscuous. People grew horns, tails, wings. Animals spoke, or we thought they did. The boundaries we now consider fundamental—human and beast, sacred and profane, beautiful and monstrous—didn't exist. They were drawn later, by people with theological opinions and access to soldiers.

The art of that period reflects this. Crucible-period paintings are full of chimeric figures: men with antlers, women with scales, children sprouting feathers from their shoulder blades. They are extraordinary. They are also, to the sensibility that prevailed under the Golden Order, profoundly heretical. For centuries, this art was plastered over, walled up, or chiseled away. The consecration seals I mentioned at the top—thick layers of blessed plaster applied over offending images—were the Golden Order's preferred method. You didn't destroy the painting. You buried it under a layer of sanctified lime, which theoretically purified the image beneath through proximity to holy material. A theological argument dressed up as conservation practice. Terrible for the art, obviously. The consecration plaster bonds to the original surface and introduces alkaline salts that migrate into the pigment layer. I've spent half my career undoing the damage.

The Golden Order is gone, of course. The Erdtree burned. Or rather—I should be precise—the golden projection of the Erdtree burned, since the actual tree was already dead and had been for some time, a fact that was technically common knowledge and practically unmentionable, rather like a prominent family's drinking problem. The point is, the theological justification for covering these paintings no longer exists. We've been uncovering them for the past decade, when funding permits, and every one has been a revelation. Literally. These are images of a world we've forgotten, made by people who lived in it, and every brush stroke is evidence that things were not always as we were told they were.

So when the Interim Council asked me to evaluate a newly discovered Crucible fresco for potential destruction, my first thought was: who on the council has an interest in keeping this particular image buried?

My second thought was: I'd better go look at it before someone else does.

The chamber was in the old Colosseum district, as advertised—a part of the city I rarely visit because the subsidence problem there is genuinely dangerous. The Colosseum was built in the Age of Dragons, or possibly earlier; the foundations go deep, deeper than anyone's mapped properly, and the recent earthquakes have opened gaps in the substrata that connect to tunnel systems nobody knew about. Three engineers have been lost in the past year. Not killed, necessarily. Just lost. They went down and didn't come back, and the recovery teams found nothing but their equipment, neatly arranged, as though someone had packed up their belongings after they left.

I took my assistant Farra, who is the more competent of the two competent ones. Farra is twenty-three, a former student at Raya Lucaria who left the Academy under circumstances she declines to discuss, and who turned up at the Archive one morning asking if we needed someone who could read Old Numen script. We did. She can also draw better than anyone I've ever employed, and she doesn't chatter, which in a colleague is worth more than talent.

The entrance to the chamber was through a crack in the basement floor of a derelict tanner's workshop. Someone had propped a ladder against the edge. The ladder was new.

"Someone's been down already," Farra said.

"Yes."

"The council's people?"

"Probably." I didn't mention the alternative, which was treasure hunters. The subsidence in this district has been a gift to looters. Every collapsed wall reveals a new pocket of untouched antiquity, and the Interim Council's guardsmen can't be everywhere at once, especially since half of them are former soldiers of Morgott's garrison who still aren't entirely clear on who they're working for now.

We climbed down. The ladder ended in a passage of dressed stone—old stone, fitted without mortar in the style I associate with pre-Erdtree construction. The blocks were enormous. Each one must have weighed as much as a cart horse. The precision of the joins was almost disturbing; I couldn't fit my thinnest blade between them, and my thinnest blade is very thin indeed.

The passage opened, after about forty paces, into a chamber.

I need to describe this carefully, because descriptions of ancient art tend to slide into rapture, and rapture is useless for documentation purposes.

The chamber was roughly circular, perhaps thirty feet in diameter, with a domed ceiling that rose to a peak about twenty feet above the floor. Every surface—walls, ceiling, the curved transition between them—was covered in painted plaster. The work was buon fresco, applied in the classic Crucible technique: lime plaster mixed with fine marble dust for luminosity, with pigments ground so fine they seemed to glow from within. The state of preservation was extraordinary. The sealed chamber had maintained a stable temperature and humidity for what I estimated was at least several thousand years. There was virtually no flaking, no salt damage, no biological growth. A few hairline cracks in the plaster where the underlying stone had shifted, but nothing that couldn't be stabilized with a lime injection.

The subject matter was—

Well. That was the problem, wasn't it?

The painting depicted a procession. It spiraled around the chamber walls and up toward the dome in a continuous frieze, the figures growing smaller as they ascended, creating a deliberate perspective effect that drew the eye upward. At the base, the figures were nearly life-size. At the top, they were the size of my hand. The composition was masterful—the kind of work that makes you want to sit down and simply stare for an hour, tracing the line of a single figure's gesture from its inception to its resolution, feeling the painter's hand move across the plaster, understanding the decisions being made in real time.

The procession was led by a tall figure wearing what appeared to be a crown of horns. Not a headdress. Not a symbolic ornament. Actual horns, growing from the skull, rendered with anatomical precision—the texture of the bone, the vascularization at the base, the way the skin puckered where horn met scalp. Behind this figure came others: men and women and things that were both and neither, some winged, some tailed, some bearing features I couldn't identify—protuberances and appendages that belonged to no taxonomy I knew. They carried objects. Vessels, tools, what might have been instruments. They were processing toward the dome's apex, where—

Where there was a tree.

Not the Erdtree. Not as we know it. This tree was darker. Its bark was textured with deep fissures, rendered in browns and iron reds and a black so absolute it seemed to absorb the lamplight. Its canopy was not golden but green—a living, leafy green, thick with foliage, and among the leaves were fruits, or seeds, or—I looked more closely—eyes. Dozens of eyes, open and watching, distributed through the canopy like ornaments on a festival tree. Beneath the tree's roots, painted in the segment of wall that curved behind the base of the dome, were figures lying down. Sleeping, or dead, or waiting. Their bodies were partially merged with the roots, as though the tree were growing through them, or from them.

"That's the Greattree," Farra said quietly.

"Yes."

"Before the Erdtree. Before the Golden Order. Before everything."

"I think so."

She was sketching already, her charcoal moving across the paper with the quick, certain strokes of someone who knows the image might not survive the week. I respected that instinct. I taught it to her.

"The horned figure at the front," she said without looking up. "That's not an Omen."

"No."

"That's a king. Or a priest. Look at the bearing, the position in the procession. The horns aren't a curse. They're a crown."

I know. I know. I had known it from the moment I saw the painting, and the knowledge sat in my stomach like cold lead, because Farra was right, and the Interim Council was right to be worried, though not for the reasons they probably thought.

Let me explain.

The Omen—the people born with horns, with tails, with the marks of the Crucible—have been persecuted for as long as anyone can remember. Under the Golden Order, they were sewn underground. Their horns were sawed off. They were treated as abominations, signs of impurity, proof of the line between the sacred and the profane. Morgott, the last king of Leyndell, was an Omen, and he loved the Golden Order despite its hatred of him, and the Golden Order never loved him back. His brother Mohg was an Omen too, and he made the opposite choice—embraced the blood, the darkness, built a kingdom of defilement underground. Two brothers, two responses to the same rejection. Both dead now.

The new government—the Interim Council—has officially declared the persecution of Omen a relic of the old regime. There are proclamations. There are commissions. There is a beautifully worded charter of recognition, drafted by a committee and signed by people who have never actually met an Omen. But the persecution hasn't stopped, not really, because you can't legislate away a revulsion that was bred into the culture over centuries, built into the architecture (there are still shackle-points in the undercrofts, still sluice channels designed to wash away the blood from the horn-sawing), woven into the prayers and the nursery rhymes and the reflexive flinch when someone sees a shadow that's shaped wrong.

And here was a painting that said: before all that, the horns were holy.

Not tolerated. Not accepted. Holy. The horned figure led the procession. The chimeric bodies were beautiful, glorified, processed toward the tree of life in a celebration of the very forms that the Golden Order spent centuries trying to eradicate.

This painting was proof that the Golden Order's foundational claim—that pure human form was the divine ideal and everything else was degeneration—was a lie. Not a misunderstanding. Not an oversimplification. A deliberate inversion of the original order. What was divine was declared monstrous. What was monstrous was declared pure.

Now. You might think the Interim Council, which has officially repudiated the Golden Order's policies, would want this painting preserved, documented, and displayed as evidence supporting their own stated position. You might think that.

You would be wrong, because politics is not about truth. It is about management.

The Interim Council includes, among its seventeen members, at least six who served the Golden Order in various capacities before the Shattering. Two are former Fundamentalists. One is a retired Crucible Knight, which you'd think would make him sympathetic to the painting's message, except that Crucible Knights under the Golden Order defined themselves by their loyalty to the regime, not their sympathy for its victims. There are also three representatives of the new Omen communities, who are the most vulnerable members of the council and therefore the most cautious. And there is Magistrate Vellhen, who controls the grain supply and therefore controls everything that matters.

Vellhen was the one who sent the messenger. I know this because the document bore his office's seal, not the council's. He is not an ideologue. He is an administrator. His concern is not theology but stability. And stability, in his view, requires a careful, managed transition from the old order to the new one—a transition in which the past is acknowledged in general terms but not examined in specific ones, because specific evidence has a way of making people angry, and angry people are difficult to administer.

A painting that says the Omen were once kings is the kind of specific evidence that could make a lot of people very angry indeed. Not just the old Fundamentalists. Not just the former horn-sawyers, who are still alive and still living in the city and would prefer not to be reminded of what they did. But also the Omen themselves—angry at what was taken from them, angry at the centuries of lies, angry in a way that the Interim Council's carefully worded charter of recognition cannot contain.

Vellhen doesn't want to destroy the painting because he's evil. He wants to destroy it because he's practical. Because he can see, quite clearly, that this image has the power to destabilize a fragile peace, and he has decided—or will decide, once he reads my report—that the peace is worth more than the painting.

And the terrible thing is, he might be right.

I sat in that chamber for a long time. Farra sketched. I examined the plaster, tested the adhesion, mapped the crack patterns, took notes on the pigment palette—all the things a conservator does when presented with a significant find. I was thorough. I was professional. My hands did not shake.

There was one section that troubled me, technically. On the eastern wall, about chest height, the original fresco had been partially overlaid with a later addition—not a consecration seal, nothing that crude, but a careful, skilled intervention in the secco technique. Someone, perhaps a century or two after the original, had painted over a portion of the procession. The later paint was applied directly to the dried original surface, which meant it could be removed without damaging the work beneath. I tested a corner with my softest brush and a drop of distilled water. The overlay softened immediately. Cheap lime wash, poorly bound. Whoever did this was in a hurry, or didn't care about permanence.

Beneath the overlay was a figure I hadn't expected.

It was human. Completely, ordinarily human. No horns, no wings, no tail. A young woman, plainly rendered, standing slightly apart from the chimeric procession, watching it pass. Her expression was—well, expressions in ancient frescoes are always a matter of interpretation, and conservators are supposed to describe, not interpret, so I'll say this: her mouth was level, her eyes were open, and her hands were at her sides with the palms turned outward. Whether that constitutes grief, acceptance, wonder, or simple observation is above my pay grade.

But someone, later, had covered her up. Not the horned king, not the chimeric celebrants, not the tree with its watching eyes. Those were left intact. The only figure deemed worth hiding was the lone, unadorned human.

I don't know what to make of that. Neither did Farra, who stopped sketching when she saw it and stood very still for about a minute, which is the longest I've ever known her to be motionless.

"They painted over the human," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

I didn't answer, because I didn't have one that wasn't pure speculation, and pure speculation is not in my professional remit. But I thought about it. I'm still thinking about it.

Here is what I think.

The original painting was a celebration of the Crucible—the age when all forms mingled and the boundaries hadn't been drawn yet. In that context, a purely human figure was the anomaly. The chimeric forms were the norm, the divine ideal, the blessed condition. And someone, later—not the Golden Order, not the horn-sawyers, someone in between, in that ambiguous period after the Crucible's glory faded but before the Golden Order's strictures hardened—someone looked at that one human figure and decided she was the problem. The single, unblended, unmingled human. The first sign of the separation that would eventually produce the Golden Order's cult of purity.

They didn't cover the horns. They covered the lack of them.

I finished my examination. I wrote up my notes in the standard format: description, condition assessment, materials analysis, structural concerns, recommendations. The recommendations section is where Vellhen will look first and probably last, so I took particular care with it.

I wrote that the painting was in exceptional condition. That it was of significant historical and artistic importance. That removal was technically feasible but would require a team of at least six skilled conservators working for a minimum of eight months, at a cost that would consume the Archive's budget for the next three years. That destruction in situ could be accomplished by several methods, the simplest being the application of a lime wash that would obscure the images while preserving the plaster substrate, allowing future recovery if circumstances changed.

I also wrote—and this is where I deviated from standard practice—a paragraph about the human figure. I described the overlay, its age, its composition, the ease with which it could be removed. I noted that the figure beneath was the only non-chimeric form in the entire composition, and that its concealment by a later hand suggested a complex reception history that merited further study.

Then I crossed that paragraph out.

Then I wrote it again on a separate sheet and gave it to Farra, who put it in her satchel without comment.

The report I will submit to Magistrate Vellhen tomorrow contains my professional assessment and three options: preserve in place with controlled public access, preserve in place with restricted access, or conceal with reversible lime wash. I did not include the option of destruction, because destruction is not a conservation technique, and I am a conservator, and the day I recommend the destruction of a Crucible-period masterpiece is the day I hand in my blades and take up goatherding.

He'll be furious. He may dismiss me. He may send someone else—someone with fewer qualms and a thicker brush. In which case the painting will be destroyed, and the horned king will disappear again, and the lone human woman will be buried under another layer of blessed lime, and the cycle will continue, as it always has: each age burying the evidence of the one before it, each new orthodoxy plastering over the heresy it replaced.

I could prevent this. I could recommend the lime wash myself—the reversible one, the gentle one, the one that preserves the possibility of future recovery. It would be a compromise. It would save my job and Farra's job and the Archive itself, which cannot operate without the council's funding. The painting would survive, hidden, waiting for a more courageous age to uncover it. That's not nothing. That's actually the argument for compromise, and it's a good one, and I've been making it to myself all evening.

But here's the thing about reversible concealment. I've spent my career removing consecration seals—those thick layers of blessed plaster the Golden Order applied over Crucible-period images three, four, five hundred years ago. Those seals were also supposed to be reversible. The priests who applied them certainly believed they were performing a gentle, provisional act—covering the images, not destroying them, preserving the possibility of future recovery. And maybe the first seal was gentle. But the second one, applied a century later when the first one cracked, was less careful. And the third, applied after a flood, was slapped on in haste with cheap materials. And the fourth was applied by someone who didn't even know there were paintings underneath, who was simply repairing a wall.

Four layers of reversible concealment, and what I find beneath them is a ruin. The alkaline salts have migrated. The pigments have bled. The figures are ghosts of themselves—legible if you know what you're looking for, but damaged beyond full recovery. Every gentle, provisional, reversible act of concealment was also an act of slow murder.

No seal is reversible. Not really. Not over time. Time is the agent that makes every temporary measure permanent, every compromise irreversible, every gentle act of burial into a final one. I know this. It is the one thing I know that isn't subject to political interpretation.

Farra has the description of the human figure. If I'm dismissed, she'll take it to the Academy. Raya Lucaria's new librarian is sympathetic. Not courageous, but sympathetic, which in this climate amounts to the same thing.

Tomorrow I'll deliver my report. It recommends full preservation with controlled public access. It does not recommend concealment. It does not mention destruction. It will be received by a practical man who will see it as an act of professional stubbornness by an aging conservator who values plaster over peace.

He won't be entirely wrong.

I am not a brave man. I have no talent for confrontation, no taste for martyrdom, and a deep and abiding fondness for my quarterly salary, which, inadequate as it is, pays for my workshop and my tea and the one good lamp I need for detail work. I am not making a stand. I am submitting a report, in the standard format, with the standard recommendations, and if the recommendations happen to be incompatible with what the magistrate wants, that is a consequence of the evidence, not of my character.

The horned king processes toward the tree. The human woman watches. The eyes in the canopy are open.

It's a very good painting. I intend to say so.
