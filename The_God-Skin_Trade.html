The God-Skin Trade

by Kontextmaschine

The Godskin Apostles wear robes made of god.

Not "blessed by" god, not "dedicated to" god, not "symbolically representing" god. Made of god. Flayed divine skin, tanned and stitched into vestments. They wear it the way a fur trapper wears beaver pelts — as proof of trade, as professional credential, as the finished product of an industry that requires a raw material supply chain and nobody ever asks where the beavers are coming from because the coat is right there and it's very warm.

So: which gods?

This is the question that should keep you up at night and doesn't, because the game drops "Godskin" as a compound noun and your brain files it under "creepy enemy faction" and moves on. But the compound noun is doing something extraordinary. It is telling you — flatly, without commentary, in the same register it uses for "Grafted Scion" and "Cleanrot Knight" — that there was a period in the history of the Lands Between when gods were being skinned with enough regularity that the people doing the skinning developed a group identity around it. A professional culture. Robes plural. Apostles plural. A Noble subclass with a distinct silhouette. You don't get institutional differentiation from one kill. You get it from an industry.

The Gloam-Eyed Queen ran this industry. Ran it well, by all accounts.

She was an Empyrean — same tier as Marika, same tier as Ranni, same potential to restructure reality. She wielded Destined Death through black flame, which is the Lands Between's way of saying she had access to the one force in the setting that makes death permanent. Not the recycling-through-the-Erdtree death that the Golden Order sells. Not the undeath that Those Who Live in Death experience. Actual death. The kind where you stop.

And she pointed it at gods.

The theological implications here are — look, I'm going to try to be precise about this because it matters. The Golden Order's entire legitimacy rests on the claim that the current divine arrangement is

the

divine arrangement. Marika is God. The Elden Ring is the law of nature. The Erdtree is the cosmic infrastructure. The Greater Will is the background radiation of reality. This has always been the case. This will always be the case. Deviation is heresy.

The Godskins are wearing the receipts that prove this is bullshit.

If the Gloam-Eyed Queen and her Apostles were killing gods and wearing their skins, then gods plural existed, gods plural could die, and the divine order Marika imposed was not a revelation of cosmic truth but a

survivor's

arrangement — the theology of whoever was left standing after the god-skin trade dried up. Marika didn't discover she was God. Marika was the last god nobody managed to flay.

(Or: Marika was the god who figured out how to make flaying impossible, by sealing away Destined Death inside Maliketh and removing permanent death from the setting entirely. Which reframes the sealing of Destined Death from "divine mercy" to "monopolist removing the competition's tooling." But that's a different post.)

The Godskins survive into the present as — what, exactly? Relics. Holdovers. A dispersed religious community organized around a sacrament they can no longer perform because the prerequisite — killable gods — has been removed from the system. They still wear the robes. The robes still work, in the sense that Godskin magic is real and potent and the black flame still burns. But the robes are old. The skins are old. No new gods have been flayed in a very long time because Marika made flaying gods structurally impossible, and the Godskins are left performing the rituals of an industry that has been shut down by regulatory capture.

They're the equivalent of a guild hall where the craft has been outlawed but the journeymen still show up every morning and polish their tools.

What gets me is the Noble/Apostle split. Two distinct body types, two distinct fighting styles, two ranks in what is clearly a hierarchical organization. The Nobles are fat — distended, rolling, their stolen skin stretched over frames that seem designed to maximize surface area. The Apostles are thin — elongated, serpentine, their skin tight and functional. This isn't random monster design. This is institutional stratification expressed through body morphology. The Nobles consumed. The Apostles served. One class was defined by accumulation, the other by devotion. You see the same split in every priestly hierarchy that has ever existed — the bishops who eat well and the monks who don't, and somehow both are serving the same god.

The black flame itself is fascinating because it's a fossil fuel. I mean that almost literally. It's the residual energy of Destined Death — a cosmic force that was active in a previous era, was sealed away, and now persists only in degraded form, powering the incantations of a remnant population who remember when the supply was abundant. The Godskins are burning the last reserves of a depleted resource and calling it worship. Their fire is weaker than it was. Their god is gone. Their queen was defeated by Maliketh in a war that nobody writes about because the winner got to decide what counts as history.

And here's the thing I keep circling back to: the Godskins are not wrong. Their theology — "gods can die, gods should die, the divine order is not permanent" — is empirically correct. The Elden Ring shatters. Marika breaks. The Erdtree burns. Every single claim the Golden Order made about the permanence and inevitability of the current arrangement turns out to be false, and the people who were saying "this isn't permanent, we've seen gods die before, we're wearing them" were right the entire time.

Being right didn't help them. Being right almost never does.

The Godskins haunt divine towers and isolated chapels. They attack anyone who approaches. They don't recruit, don't proselytize, don't build. They are a religion that has been proven correct by history and has absolutely no idea what to do with that validation because their entire institutional identity was built around opposition to an order that no longer exists. They were the resistance. The thing they were resisting fell. They're still resisting.

I think about the skins themselves — the actual material, the leather. How old is it? How many generations of Apostle have worn the same robe? Is there a preservation protocol? Do they repair the skins when they tear, and if so, with what — more god-skin? Regular leather? At what percentage of replacement material does a god-skin robe stop being a god-skin robe? This is the Ship of Theseus except the ship is a coat made of a dead deity and the question is whether sanctity inheres in the material or the institution that maintains it.

The answer, obviously, is the institution. It's always the institution. The skin is a credential. The credential outlasts the skin.
