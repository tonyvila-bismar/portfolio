The Recusant's Ledger

by Oneila

In Limgrave I am a pilgrim. Roughspun cloak, walking staff, the particular shuffle of someone whose feet hurt and whose faith doesn't. The soldiers at the checkpoint wave me through. Pilgrims are not worth searching. They carry nothing of value except conviction, and conviction is not taxable.

In Liurnia I am a scholar. Academy robes — stolen, not earned, but the Cuckoo soldiers at the lakeside garrison cannot tell the difference between a genuine sorcerer and a woman wearing the right clothes with the right posture. The posture is the hard part. Academy graduates carry their heads at a specific angle. Chin slightly elevated. Eyes focused on the middle distance. The angle communicates: I am thinking about something more important than you. I practiced it for three weeks in a mirror before my first Liurnia contract.

In the Altus Plateau I am a merchant. Orvath's papers, purchased from his estate after the Shattering for a price that would make you wince. Orvath was a pelt-buyer for the Great Caravan who crossed the Sea of Fog eleven times. His papers grant passage through every checkpoint in the heartland. Nobody asks why a dead man's papers are being carried by a woman half his age, because nobody looks at papers closely enough to notice the discrepancy between the name and the face. They look at the seal.

In the capital I am nobody. Leyndell is too broken for identities. The ash covers everything. The yellow wax on the doors has cracked. You walk through Leyndell the way you walk through a graveyard — quietly, with your hands visible, hoping the things that live here don't notice you.

My name is Veshka. My real name, the one I was born with, before any of this. I am a Recusant of Volcano Manor, and I kill people for a living.

The ledger is Tanith's invention. Every contract recorded. Target, location, method, date, payment. The payment is always the same — continued residence at the Manor, continued protection from the Two Fingers' assassins, continued access to the serpent's blessing, which is a way of saying: you get to stay alive, and in exchange you take lives, and the bookkeeping is immaculate.

Tanith does not call them assassinations. She calls them "invitations to a world unburdened by false guidance." The targets are always Tarnished who serve the Two Fingers — champions following grace toward the Erdtree, doing exactly what the system was designed to make them do. We kill them because Rykard has decided that the system itself is the enemy, and the people who participate in it are participants, and participants are legitimate targets.

I have completed nineteen contracts. The ledger records each one in Tanith's elegant handwriting — she writes the entries herself, because the record is the ritual, and the ritual is the legitimacy, and legitimacy matters even when you're running an assassination guild out of a volcano.

Especially when you're running an assassination guild out of a volcano.

The first contract was in Limgrave. A Tarnished named Gaven, freshly arrived from across the Sea of Fog, still blinking at the golden light of grace. He had been assigned a Finger Maiden. He had a sword he didn't know how to use and armor that didn't fit and the look of a man who had been dead recently and wasn't sure the resurrection had taken.

I killed him at a Site of Grace. He was resting. His maiden was gathering rowa fruit nearby. I used a dagger — clean, fast, from behind. He didn't see me. He didn't feel it. The maiden came back and found his body and screamed, and the scream was the part I hadn't prepared for.

I reported back to the Manor. Tanith wrote in the ledger. "Gaven of the Badlands. Limgrave, southern checkpoint. Blade. Third day of the new moon."

She asked me how I felt.

I told her I felt like a pilgrim. Because that was what I had been wearing at the time, and I had discovered something about myself that I have not been able to un-discover: I feel like whoever I'm dressed as. The cloak is not a disguise. It is a self. When I wear the pilgrim's roughspun, I shuffle. I hurt. I believe. When I take it off, the belief comes off with it, and what's underneath is — less. Quieter. A woman with a dagger and a ledger entry and no particular feelings about either.

Tanith looked at me for a long time. She had been a dancer in a foreign land before Rykard found her. She understood performance. She understood the gap between the self that performs and the self that watches.

"Good," she said. "The ones who feel nothing are the ones who last."

The Liurnia contract was harder. Not the killing — the killing is never the hard part, which is itself a thing I should probably think about more carefully than I do. The hard part was the three weeks I spent at the Academy checkpoint waiting for my target.

His name was Castellan. A Tarnished scholar, one of the rare ones who came to the Lands Between not to fight but to study. He had been corresponding with the Academy about glintstone resonance theory. He believed — sincerely, from what I could gather by reading his letters, which I stole from the Academy's mail system during my second week — that understanding the nature of glintstone would reveal something fundamental about the relationship between the stars and consciousness.

He was probably right. The primeval current scholars had been chasing the same question, and the ones who caught it were absorbed into graven masses, but the question itself was legitimate.

I killed him on the bridge between the academy gate and the mainland. He was carrying three notebooks full of observations. I read them afterward, which was not part of the contract and not something I reported to Tanith. The observations were precise, elegant, and useless to me. I do not understand glintstone theory. I understand how to walk like a scholar, how to angle my chin, how to make the Cuckoo soldiers see the robe instead of the woman.

The notebooks are in my quarters at the Manor. I don't know why I kept them. Bernahl — the knight whose maiden threw herself into the fire, the man who teaches Ashes of War with the steady hands of someone who has decided to see nothing, hear nothing, doubt nothing — Bernahl saw the notebooks on my shelf and said: "Souvenirs are a bad habit."

He was right. I kept them anyway.

By the seventh contract I had developed a system. Each region required a different self. The selves had names, histories, mannerisms. The pilgrim was Maren — a widow from the Weeping Peninsula, devout, arthritic in the left hand, walked with a slight limp that I maintained for hours because consistency is the difference between a disguise and a character. The scholar was Tessaly — a name I borrowed from an actual scholar I'd met in Leyndell, because real names carry real weight and a stolen name fits better than an invented one. The merchant was Orvath, gender and all, because the papers said Orvath and the guards saw the papers.

The system worked. I moved through the Lands Between like water through a filter, changing shape to match each opening, and the targets died, and the ledger filled, and Tanith's handwriting never wavered.

But the selves accumulated.

I don't mean they haunted me. I mean they persisted. Maren's limp appeared when I was tired, even when I wasn't wearing the roughspun. Tessaly's chin-angle crept into my posture at the Manor, which confused Diallos, who thought I was looking down on him, which I was, but not because of the angle. Orvath's trade vocabulary — the specific words a pelt-buyer uses when assessing quality, words I had memorized from his journals — surfaced in conversations where they didn't belong.

I was collecting selves the way Castellan collected glintstone observations. Each one precise. Each one useful. Each one, I was beginning to suspect, more real than the woman who wore them.

The thirteenth contract was in the Mountaintops of the Giants. A Tarnished named Rialle who had made it further than most — past the capital, past the burning, into the frozen wastes where the Fire Monks keep their vigil and the last Giant tends his cursed flame.

I could not be a pilgrim in the Mountaintops. There is nothing to be a pilgrim to. I could not be a scholar — the Academy has no interest in the frozen north. I could not be a merchant — no trade routes survive above the snow line.

I went as myself.

This was the hardest contract, and not because of the cold, though the cold was extraordinary. It was hard because I had no self to wear. I walked through the Mountaintops as Veshka, which meant I walked through the Mountaintops as the absence that lives underneath the costumes, the quiet woman with the dagger, and I discovered that the quiet woman does not know how to walk.

Not physically. Physically I'm fine. I mean the walk that communicates identity — the shuffle that says pilgrim, the glide that says scholar, the heavy-footed confidence that says merchant with papers. Veshka has no walk. Veshka moves the way water moves when there is no container — formlessly, finding the lowest point, pooling in whatever depression presents itself.

Rialle was camped at the base of the Forge. She was alone. Her maiden had died three days earlier — natural causes, which in the Mountaintops means exposure. Rialle was sitting by a fire she had built from the wood of her maiden's walking staff, and she was staring at the flame, and the flame was ordinary flame, not the ruin-flame of the Giants, and the staring was the staring of a woman who had walked across an entire continent and lost the person who was supposed to burn at the end of it.

I sat down across the fire from her. She looked up. She did not look alarmed. She was too tired to be alarmed, and I was too empty to be threatening.

"Recusant?" she asked.

"Yes."

"You're here to kill me."

"Yes."

She nodded. She looked at the fire again.

"My maiden's name was Coreth," she said. "She was forty-one. She had survived three previous Tarnished. She knew the terrain between Limgrave and the Altus Plateau better than anyone alive. She called the rot-dogs 'barkers' because she said the theological name was too many syllables for something that was trying to eat you."

"I didn't ask."

"I know you didn't ask. I'm telling you because someone should know her name before this is over, and you're the only one here."

I killed her after she finished talking. She did not resist. The ledger entry reads: "Rialle of the Western Reaches. Mountaintops, Forge approach. Blade. Second week of frost." Tanith's handwriting is the same as always.

Coreth's name is not in the ledger. Coreth is not a target. Coreth is a dead maiden whose walking staff was burned by a woman who no longer needed it, and whose name now lives in the head of the woman who killed the woman who burned it, and this is a chain of inheritance that no institution designed and no ledger tracks and no theology accounts for.

I remember the names of all their maidens. I don't remember the names of most of the targets. This is, I think, a problem.

The nineteenth contract is current. The target is in the Consecrated Snowfield, north of the Haligtree. I have been walking for two weeks. I am wearing nothing — no costume, no identity, no self. Just the clothes I own and the dagger I carry and the ledger in my pack that records everything except the things that matter.

Patches told me before I left that the Snowfield is a whiteout — visibility near zero, no landmarks, no grace sites, no map. "You'll get lost," he said. "Everyone gets lost. The snow makes everything the same."

"I won't get lost."

"You will. And when you do, you'll start talking to yourself, because the snow takes everything else away and talking is how you prove you exist."

"I don't need to prove I exist."

"Everyone needs to prove they exist. The question is who you prove it to."

He was being uncharacteristically philosophical. Patches is a coward and a thief and a liar, and every word he says is calculated for advantage, and the advantage of being philosophical with a woman about to walk into a whiteout is unclear to me. Unless the advantage is: if she dies, she dies thinking well of him, and thinking well of Patches costs nothing and changes nothing.

I am in the Snowfield now. The snow is exactly as described. White. Featureless. The wind carries ice crystals that sound, against the cloth of my hood, like someone whispering a language I almost recognize.

I have been walking for three days without seeing another living thing. The target is ahead. Or behind. Or nowhere. The Snowfield does not have directions. It has conditions.

Patches was right. I am talking to myself. Not out loud — I'm not that far gone. But inside, the selves are having a conversation. Maren says pray. Tessaly says observe. Orvath says calculate the cost. Veshka says nothing, because Veshka is the one listening to the others, and what the others are saying is: you are not one person. You are a ledger. You are a record of all the selves you have worn and all the people you have killed and all the maidens whose names you remember and all the targets whose names you don't.

The snow continues. The target is somewhere. The dagger is in my belt. The ledger is in my pack.

I will find the target. I will complete the contract. I will return to the Manor. Tanith will write the entry. The handwriting will not waver.

And I will take off whatever I am wearing and look for the woman underneath and the question — the question that has been growing since the first contract, since Gaven's maiden screamed, since Castellan's notebooks, since Rialle said her maiden's name into the fire — the question is whether there is anyone there.

Whether the woman underneath the costumes is a woman or a space. Whether Veshka is a person or a gap between performances.

I don't know.

The snow doesn't care.
