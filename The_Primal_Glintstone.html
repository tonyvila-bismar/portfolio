The Primal Glintstone

by Caato Valentis

A primal glintstone is a sorcerer's soul.

I want you to hold that sentence in your hand for a moment before we proceed, because it is doing something that most sentences about magic do not do, which is telling the truth without decoration. Not a sorcerer's power. Not a sorcerer's essence, which is a word people use when they mean "soul" but are embarrassed by the directness. The soul. The irreducible thing. Compressed into crystal, which is to say compressed into a form that can be held, studied, transplanted, and — this is the part that matters — used as raw material.

The Academy of Raya Lucaria was, for most of its institutional life, a school dedicated to the study of glintstone sorcery, which was itself the study of starlight crystallized into mineral form. The stars radiated. The radiation struck the earth. The earth held it. Sorcerers learned to extract the held light and redirect it, which is a polite way of describing the fundamental act of glintstone magic: taking something cosmic and making it do what you want.

This was respectable.

The primeval current was not.

The primeval current was the recognition — arrived at independently by at least three scholars across different centuries of the Academy's history, which tells you it was not a breakthrough but an inevitability, a conclusion the material was always going to force on anyone who studied it long enough — that the glintstone in the earth and the glintstone that forms inside a sorcerer's body when they practice magic intensively enough are the same substance. The stars made the stone. The stone made the sorcerer. The sorcerer, over decades of channeling stellar energy, began to crystallize from the inside, and the crystal that grew in them was as much a piece of the cosmos as the glintstone in the mines.

The primeval current scholars looked at this and asked: what happens if we use the sorcerer-stone the same way we use the earth-stone?

The Academy expelled them. I want to be precise about the sequence. The Academy did not expel them for asking. The Academy expelled them for answering.

Azur and Lusat were the names that survived. There were others — the names did not survive because the Academy was thorough in its erasures, and an institution that controls a library controls a history, and a history without names is a history without people, which is a landscape without landmarks, which is a place you cannot navigate back to even if you wanted to.

Azur beheld the primeval glintstone and saw something he described, in the single fragment of his writing that escaped the Academy's corrections, as "the current that precedes the stone." Not the star. Not the light. Not the crystal. The thing before all of those — the impulse, the original motion, the first vibration that would eventually slow down enough to become light and then slow down further to become matter and then slow down further still to become a woman standing in a basement laboratory watching her own hands turn blue.

Lusat looked somewhere else. Lusat looked at the dying moments of a star cluster — not metaphorically, not through analogy, but directly, through a glintstone array that functioned as a telescope of a kind the Academy had not sanctioned and would not have approved, because the array's lens was a primal glintstone, which is to say its lens was a person — and what Lusat saw broke him.

The fragments do not say what he saw. They say only that afterward he could not stop seeing it, that his eyes continued to perceive the star cluster's final collapse superimposed on everything else, that he walked through the Academy's corridors with the death of ten thousand suns playing across his visual field like a transparency laid over the world of the living.

Both of them ended up with their bodies encased in crystal. Their own crystal. Grown from the inside, the primeval current that they had tapped into flowing through them and solidifying as it went, until what remained was not a corpse but a formation — geological, beautiful, and aware.

I have seen formations like this. Not in the Lands Between.

In the Trident's geological archive there is a crystal mass recovered from a planet in the Kavepap system whose dominant species had discovered, through an entirely different mechanism, the same principle: that consciousness and mineral structure are not as far apart as biology would prefer, and that the distance between them can be closed, and that closing it is not death but also not life but something the language has not yet agreed to name.

The Kavepap sealed theirs in a vault and studied them for six hundred years and learned nothing, because the formations were not communicating and the scientists could not determine whether this was because the formations had nothing to say or because the scientists lacked the apparatus to hear it. I suspect the latter. I suspect it strongly. But suspicion is not knowledge, and the Kavepap, who are better empiricists than I will ever be, would tell you that until you can measure the signal, the signal does not exist, and they would be wrong, but they would be wrong in a way that is at least honest about its limitations.

Sellen was not Azur. Sellen was not Lusat. This distinction matters because the story of Sellen is not a story about genius — it is a story about inheritance, which is a different thing entirely, and confusing the two is a mistake I have watched scholars make for a century and a half and it never stops irritating me.

Azur and Lusat discovered. Sellen received.

She received their work through whatever channels survive an institutional purge — the copied pages, the oral traditions, the student who memorized a proof before the original was burned and carried it in her head for forty years and taught it to one other student who taught it to Sellen. The primeval current arrived in Sellen's hands the way most dangerous knowledge arrives: reduced, fragmented, stripped of the cautions that the original discoverers had learned through personal catastrophe. Azur knew what the current cost because the current was costing him his body in real time. Sellen knew what Azur had found. She did not know what Azur had lost, because the loss was in the chapters the Academy had burned.

She was brilliant. I do not say this lightly. The accounts describe a mind that could hold a glintstone theorem in three dimensions, rotate it, find the flaw, and correct it while lecturing on an unrelated topic. She taught at the Academy. She was expelled from the Academy.

The charge was practicing the primeval current, which was true, but the expulsion was not for the practice — it was for the implication. If sorcerer-stone and earth-stone are the same substance, then sorcerers are not channelers of cosmic power. They are cosmic power. The distinction between the magician and the material collapses, and when it collapses, the Academy's entire institutional architecture — which depends on sorcerers being scholars who study an external phenomenon — collapses with it.

The Academy did not expel Sellen for being dangerous. The Academy expelled Sellen for being right in a way that made the Academy unnecessary.

After her expulsion she continued. This is the sentence that should give you pause, because "continued" is doing work here that a less honest narrator would distribute across three paragraphs of motivation and psychological nuance. She continued. She found Azur, crystallized and silent in his grotto. She found Lusat, encased and watching the death of stars that had been dead for millennia. She looked at what the primeval current had done to its two greatest practitioners and she continued.

I have known artists like this. I have watched them walk into the theater where the last director hanged himself from the flies and begin blocking the first scene. The building is not a warning. The building is a challenge. The predecessor's failure is not evidence that the project is impossible — it is evidence that the predecessor was not equal to it, and the artist standing in the doorway believes, with the perfect serene confidence that is indistinguishable from madness until the final act determines which one it was, that she is.

Sellen asked a Tarnished to help her. Specifically, she asked a Tarnished to cut the primal glintstone from her body — her soul, compressed into crystal, the seed of everything she was — and transplant it into a different body. A puppet body. One of Seluvis's discarded mannequins, a shell emptied of its original occupant by a man whose relationship to consent I will not dignify with analysis because the analysis would require me to take his methodology seriously and I refuse.

She put her soul in a stolen body and went to the Academy.

The puppet body is the detail I cannot stop turning over. Not the ambition. Not the primeval current. The puppet. Sellen's plan required her to abandon her own flesh — the body that had spent decades crystallizing, that had become partly mineral, that was itself evidence of the very principle she was trying to prove — and install herself in a container that had been manufactured for someone else's purposes. She became a tenant in a body that had been designed as a prison.

There is a word for what this is but the word depends on which tradition you ask. In the theatrical vocabulary I was raised on, it is an understudy stepping into a role that was built for a different performer and discovering that the costume does not fit but the blocking is already set and the audience is already seated and the only choice is to perform. In the vocabulary of the Codex — and I reach for this reluctantly because I am not a theologian but I am not stupid either — it is the problem of the vessel: can the container change the contents, and if so, who is speaking when the transplanted soul opens its borrowed mouth?

She went to the Academy and she succeeded and she failed and the succession was the failure.

Sellen gathered the primal glintstones of Azur and Lusat. She brought them to the Academy. She performed — and "performed" is my word, not the sources' word, but it is the right word — she performed the act that would restore the primeval current to the center of the Academy's study. She proved the thesis. Sorcerer-stone and earth-stone are the same substance. The sorcerer is the material. The current is real.

And the current consumed her.

She became a graven mass. A cluster of fused bodies — not corpses, not crystals, but the thing between, the thing the language will not name, faces pressed together at angles that suggest a geometry the skull was not designed to accommodate. The graven masses haunt the Academy. They are the institution's nightmare made architectural: clusters of sorcerers who pushed too far and were pulled too far and ended up fused into something that the corridors of the school must now navigate around, something the students walk past on their way to lecture halls where they study the stars at a safe and institutional distance.

I have stood in front of a graven mass. Not Sellen's. A different one, older, from a different century of the Academy's long and selective memory. I stood in front of it for twenty minutes because I am the kind of creature who stands in front of things for twenty minutes, and what I saw was this: the faces were calm. Not peaceful — calm is not peaceful, calm is the absence of a particular kind of effort, the face that remains when the muscles that perform expression stop performing — and the eyes, in the faces that still had eyes, were open, and they were looking at something, and what they were looking at was not in the room.

The conventional reading is horror. The Academy would prefer you read it as horror, because horror is a deterrent, and a deterrent is a tool of institutional self-preservation, and the Academy has always been better at self-preservation than at scholarship. Look at the graven mass. See what happens when you go too far. Stay within the curriculum. Study the stars from the approved distance. Do not ask what the stars are made of. Do not ask what you are made of. Do not notice that the questions are the same question.

I do not read it as horror.

I read it as the final scene of a play that the director could not end. The production was magnificent and the production was unsustainable and the production consumed the company and the company is still on stage, frozen, the lights still on, the audience long gone, and if you listen — and I am a very old creature and I have been listening for a very long time — there is still something happening in the cluster, some vibration, some resonance, some current that did not stop flowing when the bodies stopped being separate, and whether that current is consciousness or memory or simply the mechanical persistence of a magical process that no longer has anyone to turn it off, I cannot tell you, and I have been thinking about it for longer than most people are alive.

Sellen's graven mass is in the Grand Library. The Academy's most forbidden product sits in the Academy's most important room, and the students walk past it, and the institution that expelled her absorbed her, and the absorption is final in a way the expulsion never was.

She proved the thesis. She became the proof.

This is the part where a lesser narrator would draw the moral — the Icarus analogy, the Prometheus comparison, the well-worn grooves of a story about ambition punished. I will not draw the moral because the moral is a lie. Sellen was not punished for her ambition. Sellen was completed by her ambition. The primeval current does not destroy its practitioners. It finishes them. The process Azur began in his body, the process Lusat began in his, the process Sellen chose — it is a process of becoming, and the becoming has a shape, and the shape is a graven mass, and the graven mass is not a failure.

It is the final form of a sorcerer who refused to maintain the polite fiction that the magician and the material are different things.

The horror is not that Sellen became a graven mass.

The horror is that she was right.

The sorcerer is the stone. The stone is the star. The star is the current that precedes the stone. And the current does not care whether its vessel was born or stolen or emptied of its previous tenant by a man with a collection of puppets in his basement, because the current is older than bodies and will outlast them, and what it wants — if wanting is a thing that a cosmic process can do, and I am less certain than I used to be that it cannot — what it wants is to be known, and being known requires a knower, and the knower is consumed in the knowing, and this is not tragedy.

It is the cost of admission.

I stood in front of that older graven mass for twenty minutes and I saw the faces and I saw the calm and I saw the eyes looking at something that was not in the room, and I thought: they are looking at the current. They are looking at the thing before the stone, the thing before the light, the thing before the first vibration slowed down enough to become matter. They found it. They are still finding it. The finding did not stop when they did.

I walked out of the Academy into the rain of the Liurnian lakeshore and the rain hit the water and the water held the rain the way amber holds a fly and I thought about Sellen and I thought about the puppet body and I thought about the woman who put her soul in a borrowed container and went to prove a theorem and became the theorem, and I could not decide — I still cannot decide, and I have been considering it for some time — whether what she did was the most honest act of scholarship I have ever encountered or the most elaborate suicide.

The distinction may not matter. The current does not distinguish.

The faces are calm.
