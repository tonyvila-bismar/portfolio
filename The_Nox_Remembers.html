The Nox Remembers

by Oneila

INSCRIPTION, SOUTH GATE OF NOKRON, UNDATED

We were not always underground.

This is recorded here so that no generation of the Nox forgets it: we lived beneath the sky. We saw the stars. The stars were real. They were not the lights we have made — not the cold blue flames in our vaulted ceilings, not the glintstone lamps that mimic constellations we remember from paintings of paintings of paintings. The stars were vast and far and they did not care about us, and their not-caring was the most beautiful thing in the world, because it meant the universe was larger than our problems.

The Greater Will took the sky from us. We were driven below for a crime the records name differently depending on who is writing: rebellion, heresy, ambition, curiosity. The Fingerslayer Blade is cited. The artificial stars are cited. The desire to understand what the stars were made of — whether the light that fell from them was the same substance as glintstone, whether consciousness was stellar in origin, whether the Greater Will was one god among many or the only one — is cited.

We asked questions. We were buried for it.

Let this gate remind every Nox who passes through it: the sky was real. The punishment was real. The questions were worth asking.

FRAGMENT, NOKSTELLA ARCHIVE, ATTRIBUTED TO SWORDSTRESS-ARCHIVIST YUELLE

The false sky took eleven generations to build.

I have the construction records. Sixty-two volumes, most water-damaged, some deliberately defaced by later administrations who found the engineering details theologically inconvenient. The first generation designed the vault. They had seen the real sky. They remembered the distances. They calculated the angles of starlight at the latitude of Nokron and reproduced them in cold flame and glintstone suspension, and the result was — wrong.

Not mechanically wrong. Emotionally wrong. The stars were in the right positions but they were too close. The vault is high — three hundred feet at its apex — but three hundred feet is not infinity, and the eye knows the difference. The first generation's sky looked like a painting. Beautiful. Dead.

The second generation adjusted. They introduced atmospheric scattering — a fine particulate suspension in the upper vault that softened the point-sources into something that approximated the twinkle of real starlight seen through real atmosphere. Better. Still wrong. The stars did not move.

The real sky turned. The constellations wheeled. The seasons were written in stellar motion. The Nox sky was frozen — a snapshot of one night, the night before the banishment, preserved in crystal and cold flame.

The third generation added rotation. A mechanical system — gears, cables, counterweights — that moved the glintstone lamps in arcs that mimicked the apparent motion of the real sky over a twenty-four-hour cycle.

The system required constant maintenance. Eleven technicians per shift, three shifts per day, seven days per week. The maintenance schedule is in Volume 38.

The fourth generation replaced the mechanical system with a magical one — a self-sustaining enchantment that drew on the ambient magical field of the underground rivers. This reduced the maintenance crew from thirty-three to four.

The enchantment lasted nine hundred years before it needed recalibration.

The fifth generation added new stars. Not reproductions of real stars — new ones. Stars that had never existed in the sky above. They were memorials. Each new star represented a Nox who had died defending the cities, or who had contributed something essential to the civilization's survival. The astronomers protested that the false sky was diverging from the real one. The council overruled them. "We are not reproducing the sky," the council minutes read. "We are building one."

By the sixth generation, no living Nox had seen the real sky. The false sky was the only sky. The distinction between reproduction and original had dissolved — not because the Nox forgot there was a real sky, but because the real sky was inaccessible and the false sky was here and the emotional work of looking up and feeling small and feeling wonder and feeling the particular loneliness of a conscious being under an indifferent cosmos — that work was being done by the false sky as effectively as it had ever been done by the real one.

The seventh generation stopped calling it the false sky. They called it the sky.

PERSONAL LETTER, FOUND IN SWORDSTRESS QUARTERS, NOKRON, UNSIGNED

Mother —

I have been assigned to the outer perimeter. The Ancestral Followers are restless again — they blow their horns in the Siofra galleries and the sound carries through the stone and the younger swordstresses cannot sleep. I can sleep. I have been here long enough that the horns are just weather.

You asked me once why I chose the sword over the archive. You were an archivist. Your mother was an archivist. The family expectation was clear.

I chose the sword because the sword is simple and the archive is not, and I wanted something I could hold in my hand that would do one thing when I swung it.

The archive holds everything. Every record of every generation since the banishment. Every construction log for the sky. Every census of every city. Every letter like this one, which will be filed and indexed and stored in a vault beneath a vault beneath a city beneath the world, and someday someone will read it and know that a swordstress loved her mother and could not say so in person because the perimeter rotation is eighteen months and the roads between Nokron and Nokstella are not safe.

I look up at the sky every night. I know it is not real. I know the stars are glintstone and cold flame and the memorial additions of six generations of people who needed the sky to mean something more than astronomy. I know the rotation mechanism hums if you listen for it — a low vibration, like a building breathing, like the heartbeat of an engineering project that outlived its engineers.

I look up and I feel what you are supposed to feel when you look at stars. Smallness. Wonder. The sense that the universe is large and I am not and this is fine.

Is it real if it works?

The Greater Will buried us for asking questions about the sky. We built a new sky and kept asking. The questions have not changed. The sky has. The sky is ours now — made by us, maintained by us, amended by us, and if it is a lesser sky than the one we lost, it is a lesser sky that we chose, and choosing is something the Greater Will did not think we would do.

The Fingerslayer Blade is in the reliquary at Nokron. I have seen it. It is exactly what its name says — a blade that can kill the Fingers, the Greater Will's intermediaries, the trembling appendages through which divine policy is administered. We made it because we could. We made it because the capacity to kill god's instruments is the capacity to refuse god's instructions, and refusal is the only prayer the Nox have left.

I don't know if we will ever use it. I don't know if anyone will come who could carry it to the surface and drive it into the Two Fingers and break the connection between the Greater Will and the Lands Between. I don't know if that would free us or destroy us or make no difference at all.

I know we built it. I know we built the sky. I know we built the cities and the archives and the enchantments and the swords and the dark moon rituals that call to something older and colder than the Greater Will, something that lives in the spaces between stars — the real stars, the ones we can't see, the ones that are still up there burning whether we're allowed to look at them or not.

We are the Nox. We live in the dark. We built our own light.

That is not defeat. That is an answer.

Your daughter

INSCRIPTION, NORTH GATE OF NOKSTELLA, UNDATED

The Lord of Night will come. The stars will move again — not the ones we built but the ones we remember. The age will turn. The dark moon will rise. And the Nox will look up at a sky they did not make and feel what their ancestors felt, and it will be the same feeling, because the sky was never the point.

The looking up was the point.

We have not stopped.
