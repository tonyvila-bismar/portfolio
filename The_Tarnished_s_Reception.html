The Tarnished's Reception

by Paarfi

It has been observed by historians of greater reputation than ourselves — though we hasten to add that this observation, like so many of the observations made by historians of greater reputation, was made first by historians of lesser reputation who were not credited, a subject upon which we could dilate at considerable length were we not constrained by the demands of the narrative which, we assure the reader, is proceeding — that the quality of a civilization may be judged by the courtesy it extends to strangers.

If this is so, and we believe it is, then the Roundtable Hold presents a puzzle of no small complexity, for it is a place where the courtesy is impeccable and the civilization is in ruins.

But let us begin, as the historian's craft demands, with the particulars.

The Tarnished who concerns us arrived at the Roundtable Hold on a Tuesday. We are aware that the Lands Between does not, strictly speaking, observe the same calendrical conventions as those regions with which the reader may be more familiar, but we have taken the liberty of converting the local temporal notation into a form more readily digestible, and we assure the reader that the conversion is accurate to within what might be called, without undue exaggeration, a reasonable margin. It was a Tuesday. The Tarnished arrived. These facts are not in dispute, though the reader may question whether the day of the week is relevant to the narrative that follows, to which we respond: it is not, but the historian records what is known, and what is known includes the day.

Her name was Veradis. She had been resurrected approximately three weeks prior in a region of Limgrave that is chiefly notable for its population of soldiers who are, in the common parlance, "not quite dead," a description which, as the reader will appreciate, applies to a rather large number of persons in the Lands Between and is therefore of limited diagnostic utility. She had fought her way through these soldiers with a determination that we would characterize as admirable were we in the habit of characterizing things, which we are, and therefore: admirable.

She carried a sword of no particular distinction, a shield that had been dented in a manner suggesting intimate acquaintance with a rather large creature's foot, and an expression of bewilderment that, in our experience, is the natural condition of anyone who has recently died, been resurrected, and been told to go become a god without further instruction.

She arrived at the Roundtable Hold by the mechanism that all Tarnished arrive at the Roundtable Hold, which is to say, suddenly and without adequate preparation.

We shall not describe the mechanism. It has been described by better historians than ourselves — there, we have said it, and the reader may note the concession, though we trust that the reader also notes that the concession was delivered with a grace that suggests it cost us very little — and we see no purpose in rehearsing what is already well established. Veradis was elsewhere. Then she was at the Hold. The transition was, by all accounts, disorienting.

The Roundtable Hold is a building that exists in a location that is not, properly speaking, a location. It is adjacent to the Lands Between in the way that a footnote is adjacent to the text it annotates: connected, dependent, and occupying a space that is technically part of the work while being clearly separate from it. The Hold contains a large circular table, several smaller rooms of varying utility, a forge, a library of sorts, a balcony from which one may observe the table without being observed, and a pair of Two Fingers.

The Two Fingers are — and here we must beg the reader's patience, for the subject is delicate and our own understanding, though considerably more extensive than the average, is not without its limits — the Two Fingers are appendages. Large ones. They protrude from a surface. They twitch. They are understood to communicate the will of the Greater Will, a cosmic entity whose name, we cannot help but observe, suggests a certain poverty of imagination on the part of whoever named it, though we would not dream of criticizing a being of such presumably vast intellect on the basis of its nomenclature alone.

The Two Fingers communicate by twitching.

We shall let that stand without elaboration, as we feel the reader is capable of appreciating the implications without our assistance, and because elaboration would require us to address certain theological questions that are, frankly, above our station, though not — we hasten to add — above our interest.

Veradis, then, arrived at the Roundtable Hold, found herself in a building she had not intended to visit, surrounded by persons she had not been introduced to, in possession of a sword that needed sharpening and a mandate from a pair of twitching fingers that she had not yet had the pleasure of meeting.

It was at this point that she encountered Sir Gideon Ofnir, the All-Knowing.

We must pause here to describe Sir Gideon, for he is the sort of person who demands description in the way that a portrait demands a frame: not because the portrait is incomplete without it, but because the frame announces the category of thing you are looking at. Sir Gideon Ofnir was seated at a desk surrounded by books, maps, documents, and the specific quality of purposeful disarray that characterizes a person who wishes you to understand that they are very busy and very important without being so gauche as to say so directly. He wore armor of considerable quality. His helm was distinctive — one might say dramatic, if one were feeling generous, and ridiculous, if one were not. He looked up as Veradis approached, and his expression communicated, with the efficiency of long practice, the following: I see you. I have seen many like you. I will help you. I will also judge you. These are not contradictory.

"You are Tarnished," said Gideon.

"I am," said Veradis.

"And newly arrived."

"I am that as well."

"And you seek, I presume, to become Elden Lord."

"I have been given to understand," said Veradis, with a caution that we find entirely appropriate to the circumstances, "that this is the general expectation."

"The general expectation," said Gideon, "is a phrase that does a great deal of work in that sentence."

"Does it?"

"It does. For the expectation is not general. It is specific. The Two Fingers have designated you, as they have designated all Tarnished who find their way to this Hold, as a candidate for the throne. This is not a suggestion. It is not an invitation. It is a designation. Do you understand the distinction?"

"I confess," said Veradis, "that the distinction between a designation and an invitation is not one I have had occasion to consider, having been dead until recently."

Sir Gideon regarded her for a moment with an expression that we shall characterize, with the historian's customary precision, as complex.

"The recently dead," he said, "are often the most honest. It is a quality that does not last. Sit down."

Veradis sat.

What followed was a conversation that lasted, by our estimation, the better part of an hour, during which Sir Gideon Ofnir explained to Veradis the nature of the Shattering, the disposition of the demigods, the geography of the Lands Between, the function of the sites of grace, the purpose of the Roundtable Hold, and the identities and capabilities of the other Tarnished who had taken up residence there. He did so with thoroughness, with clarity, and with the unmistakable air of a man who has delivered this briefing many times and has refined it to the point where it functions less as information and more as performance.

Veradis listened. She asked questions. The questions were good. We know this because Sir Gideon answered them, and Sir Gideon, as the reader will appreciate, is not a man who answers questions he considers beneath him. He answers them, certainly — he is courteous in his way — but his answers to poor questions carry a specific weight of condescension that is absent from his answers to good ones, and this weight was absent from his responses to Veradis.

She asked about the demigods. He told her. She asked about the Great Runes. He told her. She asked about the Erdtree. He told her, and his telling, we cannot help but observe, had about it the quality of a man describing a cathedral he once loved and now suspects is empty — the facts were precise, the reverence was genuine, and the conclusion was conspicuously absent.

She asked about the other Tarnished.

"There are," said Gideon, "a number of Tarnished who have been designated and called back. Some are here, in this Hold. Some are elsewhere, pursuing their own paths. Some are dead, though as you have personally experienced, this is a less permanent condition than it once was."

"How many?"

"More than you would expect. Fewer than there should be."

"That is not a number."

"No," said Gideon, with what might have been the faintest coloration of amusement, though we would not stake our reputation upon this interpretation. "It is not."

We pause here to observe — and we trust that the reader will forgive the interruption, though we note that the reader has, by this point in our narrative, had ample opportunity to accustom themselves to such interruptions and should therefore experience this one with a degree of equanimity that approaches, if it does not quite achieve, actual patience — we pause to observe that the game of questions, as it is played in the Roundtable Hold, differs in one significant respect from the game as it is played in the courts and salons with which we are more familiar.

In a court, the game is played for pleasure. Both parties know the answer. The delight is in the delay. The information, when it finally arrives, is a formality — a bow at the end of a dance whose steps were the point.

In the Roundtable Hold, the game is played for survival. Neither party knows the answer. The delay is not pleasurable. It is diagnostic. Each question reveals what the asker does not know, and in a building full of people competing to become a god, what you do not know is a vulnerability you have just displayed to a man who collects vulnerabilities the way other men collect stamps.

Sir Gideon Ofnir, the All-Knowing, collected everything.

Veradis, to her credit — and we say this with the full weight of historical judgment that such a statement implies — understood this. She asked two more questions and then stopped. Not because she had run out of questions. Because she had recognized the exchange rate.

She asked about the Two Fingers.

He paused.

"The Two Fingers," he said, "are the intermediaries of the Greater Will."

"Yes," said Veradis. "You mentioned. But what do they want?"

"They want you to become Elden Lord."

"That is what they have designated me to do. I asked what they want."

Sir Gideon Ofnir, the All-Knowing, was silent for a period that we shall describe as meaningful.

"That," he said at length, "is the question I have been asking for a very long time. I would not recommend expecting an answer."

It is at this juncture that we must introduce the reader to the other occupants of the Roundtable Hold, for Veradis's education did not end with Sir Gideon, and the Hold, though not large, contains a population whose variety is, in our considered opinion, rather poorly served by the word "eclectic," though we shall use it for want of a better alternative and trust the reader to supply the additional connotations from context.

There was Hewg, the weaponsmith, who was chained to his forge and who sharpened blades with the mechanical devotion of a man who has forgotten why he does what he does but has not forgotten how. Veradis brought him her sword. He examined it without comment, improved it without flourish, and returned it without looking at her face. She thanked him. He said: "A weapon has no need of thanks, and nor do I."

This struck Veradis, and it strikes us, as the saddest thing anyone in the Hold had said, though the competition, as the reader will discover, was not inconsiderable.

We feel compelled to note — and we recognize that this note constitutes a digression within what is already, by certain standards, a narrative not entirely innocent of digression — that the chains binding Hewg to his forge were of a craftsmanship that Hewg himself could not have improved upon. We mention this because the detail seems to us to possess a significance that transcends the merely decorative: that whoever chained the smith took the trouble to chain him well, with work worthy of the man it bound.

Whether this constitutes respect or cruelty is a question we leave to the reader, as the historian is not equipped, and should not pretend to be equipped, to adjudicate matters of the heart, however much certain unnamed colleagues may presume to do so in their own publications, which we have read, and which would have benefited from a similar restraint.

There was Roderika, whom we have not yet mentioned and whom we now introduce with the observation that she was seated on the floor near Hewg's forge, and that her posture — knees drawn up, arms around them, chin resting on the uppermost knee — conveyed a quality of grief so contained that it had become architectural. She had lost her companions. All of them.

She had been gifted with the ability to tune spirit ashes, which is to say, to take the crystallized remnants of the dead and make them useful, which is to say, to transform grief into a weapon, which is to say — but here we find that the thing we wish to say has outrun our capacity to say it, and we will therefore move on, and trust that the reader understands why.

There was Corhyn, a scholar of incantations, who was earnest and reverent and whose faith in the Golden Order was so complete that it had the quality of architecture: a structure built so solidly that the cracks in its foundation were invisible to the person standing on top of it. He offered to teach Veradis the fundamentals of holy incantation. She accepted, not because she intended to use them, but because his offer was the first thing in the Hold that had been extended without conditions.

There was D, the hunter, who sat in a corner with his hounds and who spoke rarely and with the careful precision of a man who has learned that words, once spoken, cannot be retrieved, and who has had occasion to regret several that escaped him. He nodded at Veradis when she passed. She nodded back. This constituted, by the standards of the Roundtable Hold, a warm and forthcoming introduction.

And there was Fia, the Deathbed Companion, who sat on a bed in a small room off the main hall and who offered, with a gentleness that was neither performative nor predatory, to hold Veradis in her arms.

Veradis, who had been dead three weeks ago and who had not been held by anyone since before her death and who was, though she would not have described it in these terms, profoundly and quietly terrified, said yes.

We shall not describe what passed between them, for some moments are better served by the historian's restraint than by his eloquence, and we are, despite what certain unnamed critics have suggested in reviews that we do not dignify with responses (though we note their existence, and the existence of their authors, and the notable absence of significant scholarly output from said authors, with what we trust is an appropriate degree of professional detachment) — we are capable of restraint.

Veradis emerged some time later. She did not speak of it. She went to the balcony that overlooks the great table and she stood there for a time, looking down at the Hold's occupants going about their business — the scholar reading, the smith forging, the knight studying his maps, the hunter sitting with his dogs — and she understood, with the clarity that sometimes arrives unbidden and unwelcome, that every person in this building was waiting.

They were not waiting for her. They were not waiting for each other. They were waiting for the world to be repaired, and they had each, in their own fashion, concluded that their contribution to the repair was to remain in this building and continue doing what they had always done, and that this was either faith or habit and that the distinction between the two had long since ceased to matter.

Veradis put her hand on the railing. The stone was warm. The Hold, for all its strangeness, was warm. Grace touched this place, even here, even in a building that existed in a footnote to reality, and the warmth of grace was the warmth of a promise that someone, somewhere, was paying attention.

She went downstairs. She sharpened her sword — Hewg did not look up, but the blade, when he returned it, was better than it had been, which was better than it had been when she first arrived, which was better than it had been when she pulled it from a dead soldier's hand in Limgrave three weeks ago. She walked to the grace point and felt it pull her northeast, toward something she could not see and did not understand and had been designated to fix.

She went.

We wish her well. The historian is not, strictly speaking, permitted to express preferences regarding the outcomes of the events he chronicles, but we are, as the reader has perhaps observed, not strict, and we wish her well, and we trust that this small departure from professional convention will be forgiven by all but the most unreasonable of our critics, whom we do not, as we have established, dignify with responses.
