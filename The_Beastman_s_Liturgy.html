The Beastman's Liturgy

by Oneila

I.

The storm has not moved in four thousand years.

I want you to understand what that means for a liturgy. A liturgy is a set of words performed in sequence, and performance requires time, and time in Farum Azula does not function the way it functions elsewhere. The temple is mid-collapse. The stones are falling. They have been falling since before the Erdtree grew. They will be falling when the Erdtree is forgotten. The collapse is not an event. It is a condition.

The beastmen sing inside it.

I came to Farum Azula because I was told the oldest liturgical tradition in the Lands Between survived here — older than the Golden Order's hymns, older than the Carian moon-chants, older than the fire prayers of the Giants. The dragon cult of the ancient age, preserved in amber. Preserved in storm.

I am a linguist. I study how words change when the people who speak them are cut off from everyone else. Island populations. Mountain villages. Underground civilizations. Linguistic drift is my subject, and drift requires isolation, and there is no isolation in the Lands Between more complete than Farum Azula.

The beastmen have been alone with their god for four millennia. Their god left. They kept singing.

II.

Ghurrath is the senior clergyman. He is seven feet tall and his face is the face of something between a man and a lion, with a jaw that could crack stone and eyes the color of old amber. He wears robes that were white once. The storm has turned them grey.

He does not speak any language I recognize.

This was the first problem. I had prepared for the dragon cult's liturgical tongue — a ceremonial register derived from ancient draconic, preserved in fragments on the lightning incantation scrolls that occasionally wash up in the Lands Between. I had studied those fragments for three years. I could read the invocations. I could parse the grammatical structures. I could identify the phonemic inventory.

What Ghurrath spoke was not that language. It was descended from it, the way a river delta is descended from a mountain spring — recognizably connected, topographically transformed. Four thousand years of isolation had done to the beastmen's speech what four thousand years of isolation does to any language: it had drifted. Vowels had shifted. Consonants had merged. The grammar had simplified in some dimensions and elaborated in others. Words I recognized from the scrolls had acquired meanings I could not have predicted.

The word for "lightning," for instance. On the scrolls it is kethrac — a straightforward noun, the thing that comes from the sky. In Ghurrath's speech it had become something like gethrai, and it no longer meant lightning. It meant the-sound-a-stone-makes-when-it-has-been-falling-for-so-long-that-the-falling-has-become-a-kind-of-singing.

They had not invented a new word. They had eroded an old one until it fit their experience. Lightning does not strike in Farum Azula. The storm holds it. The stones fall. The falling makes a sound. The sound needed a name. The closest word was "lightning" and over four thousand years the word had walked from its original meaning to a new one, step by imperceptible step, the way a glacier walks.

I spent three weeks learning to hear Ghurrath before I could begin to understand him.

III.

The liturgy is performed seven times per day. Or seven times per something — "day" does not have a stable referent in a place where the sun does not move.

The beastmen measure time by the collapse. Specific stones fall at specific intervals. A keystone in the eastern arcade drops every — I want to say "four hours" but that's a translation, not a measurement. Every keystone-drop, the liturgy is performed. The keystone has been dropping and resetting for millennia. It falls. It returns to its position. It falls again. The beastmen have calibrated their entire temporal framework to the rhythm of a building destroying and rebuilding itself.

The liturgy itself takes approximately forty minutes. Seven performers. Ghurrath leads. Six others respond. The form is antiphonal — call and response, leader and chorus, a structure so ancient it predates writing in most cultures I've studied.

The words are — I need to be careful here.

The words are not comprehensible. Not to the beastmen who speak them. Not to Ghurrath. Not, after three months of study, to me. The liturgical register is a third language — neither the ancient draconic of the scrolls nor the evolved vernacular the beastmen use for daily speech. It is a fossil. A phonetic sequence preserved by repetition across four thousand years, its meaning lost somewhere in the first millennium, its sounds maintained by the same mechanism that maintains any ritual: you learn it from the person before you, who learned it from the person before them, and nobody stops to ask what it means because the performance is the meaning.

I have heard this before. I have heard this in every tradition I've studied. The Erdtree burial rites contain phrases in a language that predates the Golden Order by centuries. The Carian moon-chants include passages that Rennala herself could not translate. Every liturgy outlives its language.

But the beastmen's case is different, because the beastmen know their liturgy is opaque. They know they are singing words they do not understand. And they do it anyway. Seven times per keystone-drop. Forty minutes per performance. Every performer note-perfect, because the transmission chain — four thousand years of teacher to student, mouth to ear — has maintained the phonetic sequence with a fidelity that I can verify against the scroll fragments.

The sounds have not drifted. The daily language drifted. The liturgy did not. They held the ritual words in place while letting their own speech evolve around it.

That is extraordinary. That is a feat of cultural conservation that I have never encountered at this scale. The beastmen cannot explain to you what they are singing. But they can sing it exactly the way it was sung when Placidusax still sat on his throne.

IV.

Placidusax. The Dragonlord.

He is still here.

Not in the way the Erdtree is still here — a spectral projection, a ghost of former glory. Placidusax is physically present, sitting in his crumbling throne room at the center of the storm, waiting for a god who fled. He has been waiting since before the Erdtree. He will wait until the storm ends, which it won't.

The beastmen do not worship Placidusax. This was my second misunderstanding. I assumed the liturgy was directed at the Dragonlord, that the dragon cult was a cult of the dragon. It is not. The liturgy is directed at the absent god — the one who fled, the one Placidusax waits for, the one whose name is present in the liturgy but untranslatable because the concept it refers to does not exist in any language I speak.

The closest I can get is: the-thing-that-was-here-before-the-thing-that-is-here-now-and-that-will-be-here-after-the-thing-that-is-here-now-is-gone. A temporal concept. Not a being. Not a personality. A quality of precedence. The god is the principle of having-been-first.

Placidusax served this principle. The beastmen serve Placidusax's service. The liturgy is not addressed to a being. It is addressed to a relationship — the relationship between the Dragonlord and the thing he waits for.

When I explained this interpretation to Ghurrath, he was quiet for a long time. The storm howled. A stone fell from the eastern arcade and returned to its position.

Then he said — and I am translating here from a language that has spent four thousand years developing vocabulary for exactly this kind of theological distinction — "You are almost correct. The liturgy is not addressed to the god. It is not addressed to the Dragonlord. It is addressed to the waiting."

The waiting itself. As an entity. As something that has existed so long it has acquired its own presence, its own weight, its own claim on worship.

I asked him: "Is the waiting the same as the god?"

He considered this. His amber eyes did not blink. Beastmen blink less frequently than humans, which gives their silences an intensity that is partly biological and partly — I think — cultivated.

"The god left," he said. "The waiting stayed. If the god returns, the waiting ends. If the waiting ends, what have we been doing?"

"Preserving the liturgy."

"The liturgy preserves the waiting. If the waiting ends, the liturgy is — " He used a word I had not heard before. It sounded like the word for "lightning" but with a different terminal vowel. I asked him to repeat it.

"Gethrae," he said. Not gethrai — the-sound-a-stone-makes-falling. Gethrae. Different vowel. Different word.

"What does that mean?"

"The sound a stone makes when it reaches the ground."

I understood. Gethrai: the sound of perpetual falling — the liturgy in action, the worship continuing, the waiting sustained. Gethrae: the sound of impact. The end. The stone finally landing. The collapse completing. The storm resolving into weather.

The liturgy is gethrai. If the god returns, it becomes gethrae.

Two words separated by one vowel and four thousand years of theological precision.

V.

I attended a hundred and twelve performances of the liturgy during my stay at Farum Azula. I transcribed each one phonetically. I compared the transcriptions.

They were identical. Not similar. Identical. A hundred and twelve performances by seven performers in a language none of them understood, and every consonant, every vowel, every pause, every breath was in the same place.

I asked the youngest performer — a beastman named Threnn who was perhaps twelve years old, though age is difficult to assess in a place where time stutters — how he had learned the liturgy.

"Ghurrath taught me."

"How did he teach you?"

"He sang it. I sang it back. He corrected me."

"How many times?"

"Until it was right."

"How did you know when it was right?"

Threnn looked at me with the expression of someone who has been asked how they know when water is wet. "It sounds right," he said. "It fits."

"Fits what?"

"The stones."

I thought he meant the stones of the temple. He did not. He meant the falling stones — the perpetual collapse, the rhythm of Farum Azula's destruction and reconstruction. The liturgy was calibrated to the architecture. Not metaphorically. Acoustically. The resonant frequencies of the chant matched the frequencies produced by the falling stones, and the performers adjusted their pitch and timing to maintain the match as the stones fell and returned and fell again.

The liturgy was a duet between the beastmen and their crumbling temple.

That was why the sounds had not drifted. The stones were the tuning fork. As long as the collapse continued — as long as the storm held the building in its perpetual state of falling — the liturgy had an external reference point. The daily language could drift because it was calibrated to nothing but itself. The liturgy held because it was calibrated to the building.

Remove the building and the liturgy would drift within a generation.

The building is the text.

VI.

I left Farum Azula after four months. Getting there had required navigating a tornado and bribing a wyvern. Getting out required the same tornado and a different wyvern. I do not recommend the journey.

I brought back sixty pages of phonetic transcription, a partial grammar of the beastmen's evolved vernacular, and a theological framework that would give the Golden Order's Fundamentalists a collective seizure — a religion that worships waiting itself, that has maintained its liturgical precision not through scripture or doctrine but through architectural resonance, that considers the absence of its god to be the foundational condition of worship rather than a crisis to be resolved.

The Fundamentalists worship an Order they believe is perfect and eternal. The beastmen worship a gap. The Fundamentalists build their theology on the premise that their god is present, active, and governing. The beastmen build theirs on the premise that their god is gone, that the gone-ness is the sacred thing, and that the proper response to divine absence is not despair but maintenance.

They maintain. That is the word. They maintain the liturgy the way you maintain a building — not because it is perfect but because it is there, and the alternative is letting it fall, and they have been watching things fall for four thousand years and they know what falling looks like and they have decided, with the full weight of four millennia of theological reflection, that falling is not the worst thing.

The worst thing is landing.

The worst thing is gethrae.

VII.

I published nothing. I wrote my notes in a cipher of my own invention and stored them in a trunk in my quarters in Leyndell. When the Shattering came, I packed the trunk and left the city before the burning. The trunk is in a cellar in the hinterlands. The notes are intact.

I did not publish because I could not solve the translation problem. Everything I have told you is a translation, and every translation is a betrayal, and the specific betrayal involved in translating the beastmen's liturgy is this: the words are sounds. The sounds are calibrated to falling stones. The falling stones are calibrated to a storm that has not moved in four thousand years. To translate the liturgy is to remove it from its architecture, and to remove it from its architecture is to remove the only thing that has kept it stable, and to remove the thing that kept it stable is to ensure that it drifts, and drift is death for a liturgy that is already incomprehensible to its performers.

I could give you the sounds. I could give you the phonetic transcription, precise to the breath. But the sounds without the stones are gethrae — the final impact, the collapse completed, the waiting ended not by the god's return but by the linguist's notebook.

Some things should not be translated. Some things can only be heard in the place where they are sung.

I am not a woman who believes in the sacred. I am a linguist. I study the mechanics of meaning. But I stood in Farum Azula and listened to seven beastmen sing words they did not understand in a language that no longer exists inside a building that has been falling for four thousand years, and the sound they made — the sound of the singing and the stones together, the duet between the living and the collapsing — was the most coherent expression of faith I have ever encountered.

Not because it was beautiful. Because it was precise. Because it had been maintained, sound by sound, vowel by vowel, across a span of time that exceeds the entire history of the Golden Order, by creatures who could not explain what they were doing but could do it perfectly, because the building taught them, and the building never forgot.

Ghurrath is still there. The storm still holds. The stones still fall. The liturgy continues.

Gethrai.

The sound of something that has not yet landed.
