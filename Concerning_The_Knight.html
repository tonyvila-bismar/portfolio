Concerning The Knight

by Paarfi

CONCERNING THE KNIGHT OF THE FOURTH LANCERS, AND THE CHOICE THAT WAS OFFERED

We begin, as the careful historian must, with the weather.

It was raining on the morning in question — a cold, persistent, thoroughly disreputable rain of the sort that falls upon the Stormhill garrison in the month that the locals call, with characteristic Limgrave poetry, "the month when everything is wet."

The garrison itself, which we have had occasion to describe elsewhere and which we do not propose to describe again in its entirety, being mindful of the reader's patience and the god Brevity — to whom we hereby offer our customary devotion — consisted of a stone tower of no particular architectural distinction, a courtyard of somewhat more distinction owing to the wildflowers that grew between the paving stones, and a stable that was, by the universal and apparently uncontested judgment of the horses quartered therein, the worst stable in the Altus Plateau.

In this garrison, on this morning, in this rain, there stood a man.

He was not a tall man, being perhaps five feet and eight inches in height, which is not short by any reasonable standard but which seemed shorter than it was, owing to the breadth of his shoulders and the particular way in which he carried his weight — forward, on the balls of his feet, as though at any moment he might be required to move in a direction not yet determined.

He wore the armor of the Fourth Crucible Lancers, which is to say armor that had been repaired so many times that the original metalwork was present only in the philosophical sense, the way one might say that a river in its tenth century is the same river that flowed in its first. He carried a sword. The sword was not remarkable. The man was.

His name was Vyrthen, and he had served in the Fourth Crucible Lancers for twenty-two years, and he had horns.

We feel we must pause here — though we have only just begun, and the reader may reasonably object that pausing after three paragraphs suggests a certain lack of commitment to forward progress — to address a matter of considerable historiographic importance. The horns.

In the period of which we write, horns were a contested subject. We do not mean that people argued about them, though they did. We mean that the very fact of their existence on a human body had, within the space of a single generation, been reclassified from the category of the sacred to the category of the shameful, and that this reclassification had been accomplished not through philosophical debate, not through theological disputation, not through the slow accumulation of evidence and counterevidence that characterizes the honest pursuit of truth, but through a vote taken in a single session of the First Erdtree Synod, which lasted forty-five minutes, and which was, by the unanimous testimony of those present, concluded in time for lunch.

We bring this up because the historian who does not address the reclassification at the outset will find it impossible to explain what follows. The reader who does not understand that Vyrthen's horns were, on the day he was born, considered a mark of divine favor, and were, on the morning of which we write, considered a deformity requiring surgical correction, cannot understand why Vyrthen did what he did.

And we confess that understanding why Vyrthen did what he did is, in fact, the entire reason we are telling this story, rather than any of the other stories we might have told, several of which involve more swordfighting and at least one of which involves a scandalous affair between a Crucible priestess and a tax collector, which we mention only to demonstrate that we are not unaware of what the public prefers and that our decision to tell this particular story is, therefore, a deliberate choice.

But we digress. We have been accused of this before, sometimes justly.

Vyrthen's horns curved forward from the temples and terminated in blunt points approximately two inches above his brow line. They were dark at the base — the color of old walnut — and lighter at the tips, where the keratin was newer. They had been growing for forty-one years, which was the entirety of Vyrthen's life, and in that time they had been oiled, polished, blessed by three separate Crucible priests, and, on one occasion that Vyrthen preferred not to discuss, decorated with small bells for a festival that had gone, in his professional estimation, rather poorly.

He was standing in the rain because he had been summoned.

The summons had arrived the previous evening, carried by a courier who wore the white and gold of the Erdtree Communion — which is to say, of the new order — and who had the expression of a person tasked with delivering documents to places he would rather not be. We do not blame the courier. The Fourth Crucible Lancers had a reputation, and the reputation was, in the main, that they were still Crucible Lancers, and that the dissolution order that had reached every other garrison in the Altus Plateau had, through some combination of administrative oversight, geographic inconvenience, and the simple fact that no one wanted to be the person to deliver it, not yet reached Stormhill.

Until now.

The courier handed Vyrthen the document. Vyrthen accepted it. The courier departed with visible relief, and Vyrthen carried the document into the garrison and read it by lamplight while the rain continued its commentary on the proceedings.

The document said this, in the ornate official language that characterized all communications from the Office of Erdtree Communion: that the Crucible Knights were dissolved. That the incantations channeling Crucible aspects were henceforth classified as prohibited. That all serving knights were offered a choice between voluntary aspect suppression and honorable discharge to the hinterlands. That the choice was to be made within seven days.

Vyrthen read the document twice. He then folded it with care, placed it in the breast pocket of his coat, and went to bed. He slept well, by his own account, which we have no reason to doubt, though we acknowledge that a man's account of his own sleep is perhaps the least reliable form of testimony available to the historian.

The next morning — the rainy morning, the morning on which we have agreed to begin this account — a representative of the Erdtree Communion arrived at the garrison to oversee the choice.

The representative was a woman of middle years named Magistra Endelin, and she arrived on a horse that was, if anything, more miserable about the rain than she was. She wore the formal vestments of the Communion's administrative branch: white robes with gold embroidery, a chain of office bearing the sigil of the Erdtree, and a wide-brimmed hat that had been designed for ceremony rather than weather and was performing neither function well.

We feel it is important to note that Magistra Endelin was, by every account we have been able to locate, a thoroughly decent person. She did not relish her assignment. She had processed seven garrisons before Stormhill and had found the work distressing, a fact she confided to her assistant in a letter that survived, somewhat improbably, in the archives of the Communion's southeastern district office, where we found it pressed between two pages of a requisition form for candles.

We mention this because the reader may be tempted to cast Magistra Endelin as a villain, and we wish to resist that temptation on the reader's behalf. She was not a villain. She was a functionary performing a function, and the function was monstrous, and she knew it, and she did it anyway. We leave the moral implications of this to the reader, having discovered long ago that the historian who offers moral judgments loses the reader's trust precisely when it is most needed.

Magistra Endelin dismounted, handed the reins of her horse to a soldier who looked as though she had not expected to be handed reins, and asked to be taken to the commander.

She was informed that the commander was absent. This was true. Commander Vyrien had departed for the hinterlands three days earlier, having made her choice in advance of the official proceedings, and having left behind a brief note addressed to no one in particular that said, in its entirety: "The Horse."

We confess that we do not understand this note. We have consulted nine scholars, four linguists, and a woman in Liurnia who claimed to specialize in equine metaphor, and we are no closer to understanding it than when we began. We include it because the historian's obligation is to the record, not to comprehension.

In the commander's absence, the senior officer was Vyrthen. And so it was Vyrthen who met Magistra Endelin in the courtyard, in the rain, with his horns glistening.

We have promised the reader — have we not? Let us check. Yes, we believe we have, at least implicitly — that we would explain why Vyrthen did what he did. We have not yet reached the doing, but we must first describe the conversation, because the conversation is where the doing began.

Magistra Endelin looked at Vyrthen. Vyrthen looked at Magistra Endelin. The rain continued.

"You are," said the Magistra, "the acting commander?"

"I am."

"Then you have read the dissolution order?"

"I have."

"And you understand its terms?"

"I do."

We note with interest — and we hope the reader will forgive us for noting it, as it seems to us a matter of some consequence — that Vyrthen's responses to these preliminary questions were, by the standards of formal military address in the Altus Plateau jurisdictions, shockingly brief.

A proper exchange of this nature would have involved, at minimum, an acknowledgment of the Magistra's authority, a confirmation of the receipt of the document, a secondary confirmation that the document had been read in its entirety and not merely skimmed, and a ritualized expression of gratitude for the Communion's administrative diligence in processing the dissolution in an orderly fashion. Vyrthen omitted all of this. He answered as though the questions were questions, rather than rituals. It was, in its way, the rudest thing he could have done.

Magistra Endelin noticed. She was, as we have established, an intelligent and experienced administrator, and she recognized the omission for what it was. She chose — and this, too, seems to us significant — to proceed as though nothing unusual had occurred.

"The terms of the dissolution provide two options," she said. "Voluntary aspect suppression, which would be performed by a licensed chirurgeon-liturgist at the Communion Annex in Leyndell, or honorable discharge to the hinterlands, effective immediately. You may take up to seven days to—"

"No," said Vyrthen.

We must pause again. We must.

Because the word "no" is, in context, remarkable. The document offered two options. Vyrthen did not choose the first option. He did not choose the second option. He did not ask for clarification, or for time, or for the seven days to which he was entitled. He said "no," which was not one of the available responses, and he said it while standing in the rain with his horns glistening, and the word landed in the courtyard like a stone dropped into a well.

Magistra Endelin, to her considerable credit, did not immediately respond. She regarded Vyrthen for a period of time that we estimate, based on the testimony of the soldier still holding the horse's reins, to have been approximately fifteen seconds. Then she said:

"I do not believe I understand."

"I believe you do," said Vyrthen.

"You decline both options?"

"I decline the choice."

"The choice is not—" She paused. She appeared to reconsider her phrasing. "The dissolution order is not a request, Knight. It is an order issued by the Office of Erdtree Communion with the full authority of the Golden Order."

"Yes," said Vyrthen. "I understand what it is."

"Then—"

"I am a Crucible Knight. The Crucible Knights have served the Erdtree since before the Erdtree was called the Erdtree. The incantations I channel are not techniques I learned. They are expressions of the primordial life that flows through my body and through the body of every aspected being in the Lands Between. You are asking me to choose between having my horns cut off and walking into exile. These are not choices. A choice requires at least one option that does not constitute the annihilation of what I am."

This is the longest speech Vyrthen is recorded to have made in his entire military career. We have searched the garrison records — such as survive — and have found nothing longer than a six-word tactical directive issued during a skirmish with bandits on the North Limgrave road, which reads, in its entirety: "Flank left. Kill the tall one."

It is our belief — and the reader is free to disagree, as the reader is always free to disagree, though we would ask that the disagreement be conducted in a spirit of scholarly courtesy — that Vyrthen had been composing this speech for some time. Perhaps since the first rumors of the reclassification. Perhaps since the word iltavirtaan — which we are told meant, in the old liturgical tongue, something like "the shapes the tree dreamed before it woke" — was replaced in the official lexicon by the word vexation. Vyrthen was not a man of many words. But the words he had were chosen with the kind of care that suggests long preparation and deep conviction.

Magistra Endelin listened. We believe she listened with genuine attention, because what she said next was not what the administrative protocol required her to say. The protocol required her to restate the terms, note the refusal, and escalate to enforcement. She did not do this. She said:

"I am aware of what the Crucible was."

And Vyrthen said: "Was."

And that, the reader will understand, was the entire argument in a single word. The Magistra had used the past tense. Vyrthen had noticed. The Crucible was. The Golden Order is. The tense did the work that a hundred pages of theological disputation could not have done, and it did it in a single syllable, and both of them heard it, and neither of them said anything for a time that the soldier holding the horse estimated at approximately thirty seconds, though she admitted under later questioning that she was not counting, and that she was primarily concerned with the horse, which had begun to sneeze.

We have nearly reached the end of this account, and we find that we have, despite our best intentions and our customary devotion to the aforementioned god of Brevity, consumed rather more of the reader's time than we had originally planned. We apologize for this. We do not, however, apologize for the story itself, which seems to us, in its small and wet and undramatic way, to be a story worth telling.

What happened next is quickly told. Magistra Endelin recorded Vyrthen's refusal in the official log, using the language "Subject declined to select from available options." She noted the date, the weather, and the names of the witnesses. She did not record Vyrthen's speech. We have the speech only because the soldier holding the horse — whose name, if the reader will indulge us in one final digression, was Lancer Orvenne, and who was twenty-three years old and had no Crucible aspects and had joined the Fourth Lancers because they were the only garrison that would take someone with her disciplinary record — wrote it down from memory that evening in a letter to her sister.

Vyrthen was not arrested. This surprised us when we first encountered the record, and we suspect it may surprise the reader. The dissolution order contained provisions for non-compliance, and those provisions were not gentle. But Magistra Endelin had, as we have noted, processed seven garrisons before Stormhill, and in those seven garrisons she had overseen the removal of horns from men and women who wept, and the departure into exile of men and women who did not, and in none of those garrisons had anyone said "no."

She told Vyrthen that she would report his refusal to the district authority. She told him that the district authority would, in all likelihood, send a enforcement detail. She told him that the enforcement detail would not offer a choice.

Vyrthen thanked her for her candor. This, too, was a breach of protocol. A soldier does not thank a Communion official for anything; gratitude flows upward in the hierarchy, not laterally, and certainly not from a Crucible Knight whose classification had, as of the dissolution order, been reclassified from "sanctioned military" to "aspected civilian, pending processing." But Vyrthen thanked her, and the Magistra accepted the thanks, and neither of them commented on the impropriety.

And then Vyrthen walked out of the garrison.

He did not take the road to the hinterlands. He did not present himself for aspect suppression. He walked north, into the rain, toward the cliffs that overlook the Sea of Fog, and the last that anyone at the Stormhill garrison saw of him was his silhouette against the grey sky — broad-shouldered, slightly forward on the balls of his feet, the curve of his horns dark against the clouds.

Lancer Orvenne, in her letter, described the horns as "like a crown."

We do not know what became of Vyrthen. The enforcement detail arrived eight days later and found an empty garrison. Every Lancer had gone — some to the hinterlands, some to the suppression clinics, some, like Vyrthen, into directions that the records do not specify. The wildflowers still grew between the paving stones. The horses were still in the stable, and were, if anything, more discontented than before.

We have searched for Vyrthen in the records of the hinterland settlements, in the census of Omen-classified persons, in the military archives of every subsequent conflict. He does not appear. He walked north and was not seen again. The rain closed behind him.

We are aware that this is not a satisfying conclusion. The reader has been patient — more patient than we deserve, though precisely as patient as we have come to expect — and the reader deserves resolution. We have none to offer. History does not always conclude. Sometimes a man walks into the rain and does not come back, and the historian is left with a courtyard, a horse, a soldier's letter, and the stubborn, foolish, magnificent fact that someone, when offered a choice between two forms of annihilation, chose instead to say a word that was not on the list.

We have nothing more to add.

Though we note, for the record, that it was still raining.
