The Root

by Pellegrin

Thessaly told me to bring rope, a lantern, chalk, and nothing else.

I brought a small knife. I did not tell her about the knife. The chalk was for marking the walls so I could find my way back. The rope was for the descent into the second chamber, where the root shaft narrowed to a gap that required lowering yourself by your arms. The lantern was obvious. The knife was mine.

I was seventeen. I had been a temple acolyte for two years, which was long enough to learn the Invocation of the Unformed and short enough that I still believed it.

The liturgist's name was Orrath. She had gone into the root system beneath the Temple of the Unformed four days earlier, carrying her own rope and lantern and chalk, and she had not come back. This was not, Thessaly explained, unusual. Orrath studied the deep roots. She sometimes stayed below for two or three days, sleeping in the second chamber, eating dried meat and writing observations in a journal she kept wrapped in oilskin. She had done this perhaps forty times in the years Thessaly had known her.

But four days was longer than any previous descent.

Thessaly could not go herself. She was Senior Liturgist. She had the Festival to prepare. She had forty-seven participants to classify and a garrison commander who needed the census tallied and a faction within the priesthood that was making increasingly pointed arguments about which aspects were properly sacred and which were — the word they used was "residual," which Thessaly said was the most dangerous word she had heard in thirty years of service, and she had heard some dangerous words.

She needed someone expendable.

She did not say this. She said she needed someone young and strong and small enough to fit through the gap in the second chamber. But I understood. In any institution, the person sent into the dark place is the person whose absence would cause the least disruption. I had been an acolyte for two years. My absence would cause none.

The entrance to the root system was in the temple's cellar. Not a dramatic opening — a crack in the foundation wall, roughly three feet wide and five feet tall, through which a persistent draft carried a smell I could not identify. Not rot. Not mineral dampness. Something organic and warm, like the breath of a large animal sleeping in a confined space.

The chalk marks from Orrath's previous descents were visible on the walls. White lines, each one numbered. She had been methodical. I followed them.

The first chamber was unremarkable. Natural stone, shaped by water over centuries, with root material threading through the ceiling in thick braids. The roots were pale — not the gold of the Erdtree's upper structure but a gray-white, luminous in the lantern light, with a texture that reminded me of tendon. They pulsed. I want to be precise about this: they pulsed. A slow, rhythmic contraction, perhaps once every ten seconds, visible as a slight thinning along the length of the root followed by a return to its resting diameter.

I had seen roots before. Every acolyte has. The temple courtyard had exposed root structures that the liturgists used for teaching. Those roots were still. These were not.

I followed the chalk marks to the gap.

The gap was exactly as Thessaly had described: a narrowing of the shaft where two rock formations nearly met, leaving a vertical slot perhaps eighteen inches wide and eight feet deep. The rope was already in place — Orrath's, anchored to a root above with a knot I recognized from training. I tested it. It held. I lowered myself through.

The second chamber was different.

The roots here were not threading through stone. They were the structure. The walls, the ceiling, the floor — all root material, densely interwoven, forming a space roughly twenty feet across and ten feet high. The pale gray-white had darkened to something closer to amber. The pulsing was faster here — every six or seven seconds — and synchronized. Every root in the chamber contracted at the same moment, then relaxed at the same moment, producing an effect that I can only describe as breathing.

The chamber was breathing.

I stood in it and felt the floor move beneath me. Not dramatically. A gentle rise and fall, perhaps a quarter inch of displacement, timed to the pulse. I was standing on something alive in a way I had not been prepared for.

Orrath's camp was against the far wall. A bedroll, a pack, an oilskin journal, a lantern with no oil remaining. The dried meat was untouched. The chalk was set neatly beside the journal, aligned with the edge of the bedroll, in the particular way of a person who keeps her tools organized even when she is alone in the dark.

She was not there.

I opened the journal.

The first entries were what I expected. Measurements. Root diameters, contraction rates, temperature readings, mineral samples catalogued by location and depth. Orrath wrote in a small, precise hand that reminded me of Thessaly's — they had trained in the same cohort, I would learn later, and shared the same penmanship instructor, which is the kind of detail that matters only after someone is gone.

The measurements continued for three days. Then they changed.

On the fourth day, the entries were different. Not in content — she was still recording root diameters and contraction rates. But the numbers were wrong. Not wrong in the sense of measurement error. Wrong in the sense of impossible. Root diameters of forty centimeters in a chamber where the largest root I could see was perhaps twelve. Contraction rates of once per second in a chamber where the rate was clearly six or seven seconds. Temperatures fifteen degrees higher than the air around me.

She was measuring something I could not see.

I read further. The handwriting did not deteriorate. This is the detail that stayed with me afterward, when I was trying to explain what I had found to Thessaly, who listened without interrupting and then asked me to say it again, slowly. The handwriting did not deteriorate. A person losing their mind produces shaky letters, inconsistent spacing, words trailing off mid-sentence. Orrath's hand remained precise. Her cataloguing remained methodical. She was recording measurements with the same rigor she had always used.

The measurements were simply of a different place.

On the morning of the fourth day — she noted the time, though I do not know how she determined morning without daylight — she had written a single sentence that was not a measurement. It said: "The roots below the second chamber continue for at least another sixty meters. The network is not a structure. It is an organ."

Below the sentence she had drawn a diagram. A cross-section of the root network, extending downward from the second chamber into depths she had apparently explored while I was still learning the Invocation in the courtyard above. The diagram showed the roots converging as they descended, braiding together, thickening, until at the lowest point they merged into a single mass she had labeled, in her precise hand, "the confluence."

There was a gap in the floor of the second chamber. I had not noticed it because a root had grown across it, partially obscuring the opening. The root was warm to the touch. I moved it aside — it yielded, like living muscle — and looked down.

The shaft continued.

I should have gone back. Thessaly had asked me to find Orrath, and Orrath was not in the second chamber, and the reasonable conclusion was that she had gone deeper, and the reasonable action was to report this and let someone more qualified make the next decision.

But I was seventeen, and the shaft continued, and the roots were breathing, and I had the knife I had not told Thessaly about, and I wanted to know.

I descended.

The third chamber was not a chamber. It was a space defined entirely by root material — no stone visible, no mineral substrate, just the interwoven network of pale amber roots forming walls and floor and ceiling in continuous organic architecture. The pulsing here was once every three seconds. The temperature was noticeably warmer. The air had a quality I did not have vocabulary for — thick, charged, the way air feels before a storm, except the charge was not electrical. It was biological. My skin registered it as a pressure.

I noticed my hands first. The hair on the backs of my hands was standing upright, the way it does in cold, except I was not cold. I was warm. The lantern flame burned steady — no draft — but the light seemed to catch differently on the root surfaces, as though the amber material was refracting it in ways stone would not. The shadows moved when the roots contracted. The whole space shifted slightly with each pulse, the way the inside of a lung shifts when you breathe.

I became aware that I could hear the pulse. Not with my ears. With my sternum. A low, subsonic rhythm that I felt as a vibration in my chest cavity, timed perfectly to the visible contractions. My heartbeat, which I had not been aware of until that moment, was syncing to it. I do not know whether this was physiological or psychological. I know it happened.

Orrath was sitting against the far wall.

She was alive. Her eyes were open. She was looking at me with an expression I have spent years trying to describe and have never found the right words for. It was not fear or confusion or pain. It was the expression of someone who is seeing something clearly for the first time and is trying to decide whether to tell you about it.

"You're the acolyte," she said. Her voice was normal. Calm. Measured.

"Thessaly sent me."

"I expected she would." She looked at the root wall behind me. "You came through the gap."

"Yes."

"Did you read the journal?"

"Yes."

She nodded. "The measurements are accurate. I need you to understand that. The numbers I recorded on the fourth day are not errors. They are measurements of the root network at a depth you cannot see from the second chamber. The network continues below us for at least another hundred meters. I have not been able to determine its full extent."

"Why didn't you come back?"

She looked at me. The expression again — clarity trying to decide whether to speak.

"Because the roots are not a conduit," she said. "The temple teaches that the roots carry the Crucible's influence outward from the tree, the way blood vessels carry blood from the heart. The influence originates at the tree and diminishes with distance. This is the model."

I nodded. This was what every acolyte learned.

"The model is wrong. The roots are not carrying influence outward. The roots are the influence. The network itself — the interwoven structure, the synchronized contractions, the convergence at depth — the network is performing the mingling. Not transmitting it. Performing it. The Crucible is not an event that happened in the past. It is a process that is happening now, continuously, in the root network, and what we see on the surface — the horns, the tails, the aspects the Litany classifies — are the process's visible effects, the way waves on the surface of water are the visible effects of currents below."

She paused.

"I stayed because I could feel it," she said. "Not the temperature. Not the pulse. The process. At this depth, the field is strong enough that a human body begins to — I don't have the right word. Participate. The boundary between my body and the network became less distinct. Not physically. I am still myself. My hand is still my hand. But I could feel the roots the way I feel my own circulation. As though they were an extension of something I had always had but never noticed."

I stared at her.

"The Invocation of the Unformed," she said. "We ask the assembled to remember that they are fragments of the original unity. We say this as theology. We mean it as metaphor."

She placed her palm flat against the root wall. The roots around her hand contracted, then relaxed, and for a moment — I am not embellishing, I am reporting what I saw — the contraction pattern spread outward from her hand in concentric waves, as though her touch had become the pulse's origin.

"It is not a metaphor," she said.

I sat down. Not because I chose to. Because my legs made a decision without consulting the rest of me.

We sat in the third chamber for what I later calculated was approximately six hours, though it felt like less. Orrath explained what she had observed. The root network was not a distribution system. It was a living organ — the Crucible's organ — performing a continuous process of biological interchange at a scale the temple had never suspected. The aspects manifesting on the surface were not gifts bestowed by a historical event. They were symptoms of an ongoing process, the way a fever is a symptom of an immune response. The Crucible was alive. It was working. And what it was working on was everything.

She showed me the lower reaches of the third chamber, where the roots braided into structures she called galleries — long, curving tunnels of interwoven root material, each one pulsing at a slightly different rate, each one warmer than the last. In the deepest gallery she had mapped, the roots had begun to produce something she could not classify. Not mineral. Not vegetable. A substance that grew in thin sheets between the root braids, translucent and faintly luminous, warm to the touch.

She had collected samples. Under magnification — she carried a hand lens — the sheets showed a structure that resembled neither plant tissue nor stone. The cells, if they were cells, were irregular, overlapping, as though multiple growth patterns had been superimposed on the same substrate. She said it reminded her of the tissue samples the temple took from aspected individuals during classification — the same quality of multiplicity, the same refusal to resolve into a single type.

The Crucible's mingling, she believed, was not limited to the flesh of living creatures. It was happening in the roots themselves. The network was mingling with itself — rock with root, root with something neither, producing forms that the Litany had no category for because the Litany assumed the Crucible's work was finished.

The Crucible's work was not finished. It had never stopped.

I asked her what "everything" meant.

She said she did not know yet. She said she needed more time.

I climbed back up through the gap and the first chamber and the cellar entrance and stood in the temple courtyard in the late afternoon light and looked at the Erdtree sapling growing in the center of the courtyard and understood that its roots went down into something I had been standing inside, something that was breathing, and that I would never look at the tree the same way again.

I told Thessaly. She listened. She asked me to say it again.

Then she asked me to bring Orrath more dried meat and a fresh lantern, and to tell no one what I had seen.

I did both. The meat was easy. The silence was harder. I have kept it for eleven years, which is most of my adult life, and I am breaking it now because Orrath is dead — she died last winter, of a respiratory infection she contracted during a surface visit, which is the kind of death that should not be possible for someone who spent her life touching the source of all life, but the Crucible does not choose its steel and it does not choose its departures — and because the root network beneath the Temple of the Unformed is still breathing, and someone should know.

Thessaly retired three years ago. She never spoke of what I told her, not to me, not to anyone I know of. But she changed the Festival. The year after my descent, she added a question to the classification ceremony — not the standard "What does the form teach you?" but a second question, asked privately, after the public presentation: "When did it begin?" She wanted to know whether the participants could identify the moment their aspect started manifesting. Most could not. A few described a sensation that preceded the visible change — a warmth, a pressure, a feeling of being touched from the inside. Thessaly recorded these accounts in a separate journal she kept locked in her quarters.

I have that journal now. I keep it with Orrath's.

The process continues. I take measurements now, in Orrath's place, with her instruments, in her journal, in her hand — which is also Thessaly's hand, because they had the same penmanship instructor.

The roots pulse. The chamber breathes. The Crucible is not history.

I go down every week now. I have mapped the third chamber completely and begun mapping the galleries below. The translucent sheets Orrath discovered are spreading — slowly, a few centimeters per month, but measurably. They are warmer than the surrounding root material. Under the hand lens, the cellular structure is becoming more complex with each visit, as though the tissue is differentiating in real time, working toward a form that does not yet exist.

I do not know what it is becoming. Neither did Orrath. Her last journal entry, written three days before the infection that killed her, reads: "The confluence is producing new substrate. Classification impossible with available instruments. Recommend long-term observation. Patience."

Patience. She spent nine years in the dark, measuring something the temple refused to name, recording data no one requested, and her final recommendation was patience.

I am trying. Some weeks it is considerably harder than others.

It is happening now, beneath your feet, whether the temple acknowledges it or not.
