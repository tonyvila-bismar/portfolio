Concerning the Seamster, and How His Survival Was Determined by a Remark

It is not our usual practice to devote the resources of a full historical account to the life of a seamster. We acknowledge this at the outset because our critics — and we have them, oh, we have them in numbers that would do credit to a more controversial historian, a historian who had perhaps taken positions on the Great Succession or the disposition of the Orb, rather than one who has merely attempted, with such diligence and precision as his admittedly limited faculties permit, to record the lives of persons whom posterity might otherwise forget — because our critics will observe that a seamster is not a general, is not a sorcerer, is not a queen.

He is not, in short, the sort of subject upon whom a historian of reputation ought to expend his ink.

To which we reply: the reader will, we trust, permit us the latitude to determine for ourselves upon whom our ink is best expended. The seamster in question is called Boc, and his story is, in our considered judgment, worth every drop.

Boc was a demi-human.

We pause here to observe that the term "demi-human" is, in the parlance of the Lands Between, a classification of such comprehensive imprecision that it encompasses beings as various as the beastmen of Farum Azula, the silver-furred wolves of the Consecrated Snowfield, and the diminutive, large-eyed creatures who inhabit the coastal caves of Limgrave and who, when not being chased from their homes by soldiers in the service of one lordling or another, occupy themselves with tasks of modest domestic utility. Boc belonged to this last category. He was small, hairless in the manner of his kind, possessed of features that the fashion of the Golden Order considered unbeautiful, and he carried a sewing needle.

The needle was his prized possession. We mention this because in a world of greatswords and sacred spears and weapons forged from the remembrance of dead gods, a sewing needle is an object of such determined modesty that it borders on the defiant. Boc did not carry it for protection. He carried it because he was a seamster, and a seamster carries a needle, and the carrying was, for Boc, an act of identity so fundamental that to lose the needle would have been, in some sense we do not pretend to define with philosophical rigor, to lose himself.

His fellows bullied him. We state this plainly because the plain statement is the most terrible. They bullied him and they transformed him into a tree.

We will not linger on the mechanism of this transformation, which involved a magic native to the demi-human species and which was, by the standards of the Lands Between, unremarkable — a petty cruelty of the sort that occurs daily in every society we have studied and which no society has succeeded in preventing, not through law, not through custom, not through the most elaborately constructed codes of courtesy, a subject upon which we could dilate at considerable length were we not conscious of the god Brevity, whom all historians ought to worship. We cannot say too little on this subject.

Boc was a tree. A Tarnished freed him. The Tarnished, whose name the records do not preserve and whose motives we therefore cannot assess, struck the tree with sufficient force to break the enchantment, and Boc emerged, bewildered, grateful, and still holding his needle.

We have attempted, with what thoroughness the available sources permit, to determine how long Boc existed in arboreal form. The records are, we regret to say, silent on this point, which is characteristic of records pertaining to demi-humans in the Lands Between — a civilization that maintained meticulous census data for its human population, elaborate genealogies for its nobility, and comprehensive breeding records for its livestock, but which regarded demi-humans as occupying a category so ambiguous that even the census-takers, who are not generally a philosophical breed, found themselves unable to determine whether to count them under "persons" or "fauna."

(We have elsewhere noted, in our account of the Enumerator of Leyndell, the difficulties inherent in classifying persons whom the prevailing theology has elected to regard as something less than persons. We do not wish to repeat ourselves, but we observe, with some asperity, that a civilization capable of building the Erdtree and maintaining a divine communication apparatus spanning multiple planes of existence could not, apparently, devise a census form with a category for "demi-human who mends clothes.")

Boc emerged from the tree. He followed the Tarnished. This requires no explanation for anyone who has observed the behavior of a creature recently rescued from torment, but we shall provide one regardless, because it is our nature, and because the nature of gratitude is a subject insufficiently examined by historians who prefer to chronicle the gratitude of nations rather than of seamsters.

Boc was grateful. He expressed his gratitude in the only currency he possessed, which was his craft. He altered the Tarnished's armor.

Let us pause to appreciate this, for we find that too many of our readers — and we say this with the affection of a man who has spent his career attempting to educate a public that does not always wish to be educated — too many of our readers will pass over this moment without recognizing what it contains. A demi-human seamster, freshly liberated from arboreal imprisonment, offers his services to a warrior of such formidable capacity that the warrior could, with a single blow, return him to his previous botanical condition. The service offered is not intelligence, not sorcery, not the revelation of some hidden path or secret treasure. The service is tailoring. "Your cloak sits badly across the shoulders," one imagines him saying, although we do not have the precise words. "I can fix it."

We have known greater acts of courage. We have not known many.

The reader may wonder what manner of alterations a demi-human seamster might perform upon the armor of a Tarnished warrior, and we are pleased to satisfy this curiosity, for the subject of martial tailoring is one upon which we have conducted, if we may say so, rather more research than our peers, most of whom regard the relationship between a soldier and his garments as beneath historical notice, a view we consider shortsighted in the extreme, given that an army marches as much on its stitching as on its stomach.

Boc could reset a shoulder seam to accommodate the altered posture of a swordsman who favored his right side. He could take in the waist of a gambeson without compromising the layered padding that absorbed the force of a blow. He could repair the leather strapping of a greave in a manner that distributed weight across the shin rather than concentrating it at the knee, which is a correction so subtle that most warriors would not notice it consciously but would find, after a day's march, that they were less tired than they expected, and would attribute this to good fortune rather than good tailoring, which is, we observe, the fate of all excellent craft: to be so well executed that it becomes invisible.

His stitching was even. His eye for proportion was exact. He could look at a breastplate that had been battered through three regions of a shattered world and see, beneath the dents and the bloodstains and the general air of catastrophic neglect, the garment it was supposed to be, and he could bring it closer to that ideal with thread and patience and the absolute attention of a creature for whom this work was the only proof that he existed.

He was, however, unhappy with his appearance.

We arrive now at the crux of the matter, and we ask the reader's patience, for the crux is small and easily missed, and we have not come this far — through the enchantment and the liberation and the grateful tailoring and the years of practiced craft — to fumble the arrival.

Boc believed himself ugly. We do not say he was ugly, for the standards of beauty in the Lands Between were established by a theological regime that considered horns an abomination and golden hair a sign of divine favor, and we place limited confidence in any aesthetic framework that requires a god to underwrite it. But Boc believed it, and belief, as any historian can attest, is more durable than fact, and considerably more dangerous.

We have, in the course of our researches, encountered no fewer than fourteen distinct definitions of beauty in the historical literature of the Lands Between, ranging from the Golden Order's insistence that beauty is conformity to divine template (a position we find both internally consistent and morally repulsive) to the Crucible-era understanding that beauty is the visible expression of the primordial mingling (a position we find both aesthetically appealing and functionally extinct).

The demi-humans had, so far as we can determine, their own aesthetic traditions, but these were not recorded by the civilizations that shared their territory, because civilizations do not, as a rule, record the aesthetic traditions of peoples they have classified as vermin. We note this with the calm of a scholar who has grown accustomed to discovering that the most interesting chapters of history are precisely the ones that no one wrote down.

Boc believed himself ugly by the only standard available to him, which was the standard of the civilization that had classified his entire species as something less than persons. He judged himself by the rules of a game he had never been invited to play. This is, we submit, the cruelest possible arrangement, and we have studied many cruel arrangements.

He learned of Rennala's rebirth.

The Queen of the Full Moon, cloistered in the Grand Library of Raya Lucaria, cradling the amber egg her departed husband had left like a stone in her shoe and a knife in her heart — Rennala could remake a person. The rebirth was imperfect. The students she transformed emerged formless, diminished, not entirely themselves. But the promise — the word "beautiful" applied to the result — the promise was sufficient.

Boc wished to be reborn beautiful.

Here the story branches, and we must follow both paths, for the branching is the story's meaning.

In one path, the Tarnished permits it. Boc goes to Rennala. Boc is reborn. The rebirth strips him of everything he was — his skill, his memory, his identity, his needle — and leaves behind a creature that may indeed be beautiful by the standards of the Golden Order's aesthetic framework, but which is not Boc, has never been Boc, and will never again be Boc, because the process that made him beautiful also made him no one.

He becomes one of Rennala's sweetings. A formless thing on the library floor. The needle falls from hands that no longer remember holding it.

We will not pretend that this outcome does not distress us. We are a historian, not a stoic, and if our predecessors in the profession have maintained a convention of dispassionate remove when narrating the destruction of persons in their care, we consider this convention to be, with the greatest respect to our predecessors, cowardice of the most genteel variety. Boc was a seamster. He had a needle. The needle meant something to him that we, who have never been transformed into a tree by our colleagues, cannot fully comprehend but can, with effort, approximate, and the approximation is sufficient to make his loss vivid.

In the other path — and we confess that our voice falters here, not from scholarly uncertainty but from an emotion we do not customarily permit to interrupt our prose, though we find, on this occasion, that the emotion has arrived without consulting us and shows no inclination to depart — in the other path, the Tarnished speaks.

The Tarnished says: "You are beautiful."

That is all. Three words. No theological framework. No divine endorsement. No cosmic restructuring of the aesthetic principles governing the Lands Between. A warrior, who has killed demigods and shattered the pretensions of an entire ecclesiastical order, stops and says to a demi-human seamster holding a needle: you are beautiful.

Boc lives.

He lives because someone said so. Not because it was true by any standard that the Golden Order would recognize, not because his features suddenly conformed to the template that the Erdtree's theology considered acceptable, but because someone whose opinion he valued looked at him and told him, without qualification or circumlocution (and we note the irony of a historian who has built his career upon circumlocution praising its absence), told him that he was enough.

The needle stays in his hand. The skill remains. The identity — the small, stubborn, hard-won identity of a creature who carries a sewing needle in a world of sacred swords — the identity persists.

We are aware that this is not the sort of event that typically commands the attention of historians. No armies clashed. No thrones changed hands. No gods were slain or elevated or consumed by their own ambitions. A seamster was told he was beautiful by a person who meant it, and the seamster believed it, and the belief was sufficient to prevent him from destroying himself in pursuit of a beauty that would have cost him everything he was.

We have been asked — by a colleague whose name we shall charitably withhold, though we note that his own historical work has been confined to a three-volume account of grain tariffs in the western provinces, a contribution to the discipline that we are certain posterity will treat with precisely the reverence it deserves — we have been asked why we devote our attention to such small matters when the Lands Between offers no shortage of large ones.

We reply with a question of our own: what is a historian for?

If the answer is "to record the deeds of the great," then we concede the point. Boc's deeds were not great. His alterations to the Tarnished's armor will not appear in any chronicle of the war. His needle will not be preserved in any reliquary. His name will be forgotten by everyone except, we hope, the readers of this account, who will carry it as far as their memories permit, which is, we acknowledge, not far, but farther than nothing, and farther than nothing is the entire distance between a life recorded and a life lost.

If the answer is "to preserve what would otherwise be forgotten," then we submit that no subject in the Lands Between is more urgently in need of preservation than the life of a demi-human seamster who was turned into a tree, freed by a stranger, and saved from self-destruction by a compliment.

We have chronicled wars. We have chronicled the fall of dynasties, the machinations of sorcerers, the slow degradation of divine institutions whose foundations were rotten from the first stone. We have done so with, we flatter ourselves, a certain competence, and if our critics disagree, we invite them, as always, to produce superior work, a challenge they have thus far declined to accept.

But we have not chronicled anything, in all our years of labor, that strikes us as more important than this: that a seamster lived because a warrior said three words, and meant them.

The reader will perhaps forgive us if we do not elaborate further. Some subjects are best served by brevity, and we find, to our considerable surprise, that we have at last identified one.
