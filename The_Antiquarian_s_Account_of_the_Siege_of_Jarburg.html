The Antiquarian's Account of the Siege of Jarburg

by Paarfi

Paarfi of Roundwood

It has been observed, by those whose profession it is to observe such things and then to inform the rest of us of their observations at a length which would try the patience of the Erdtree itself—were that venerable organism still in a condition to experience patience, or indeed any sensation whatsoever—that the writing of history is, in its essentials, an act of selection. One chooses what to include. One chooses, with rather more difficulty and considerably less gratitude from one's readers, what to exclude. And it is in the narrow and perilous passage between these two choices that the historian earns, or fails to earn, the bread upon which the continuation of the historian's existence, and therefore of the historian's history, depends.

We mention this not to solicit sympathy—though we would not refuse it, should it be offered, as our creditors have proven markedly unsympathetic of late, a matter we shall not dwell upon except to note that a historian who has been evicted from his study is unlikely to produce work of the quality his readers have come to expect, and that those readers might therefore consider the historian's housing situation a matter of personal concern—but rather to explain why, from the vast and blood-soaked tapestry of the Late Interregnal Period, which is to say that interval between the Shattering of the Elden Ring and the ascension of the new Lord whose identity and choices we shall discuss in a subsequent volume, we have chosen to relate the events surrounding a settlement so modest that many of our fellow historians have not troubled to learn its name.

The settlement was called Jarburg.

We can hear the objection already—indeed, we have heard it, from a certain colleague whose name we shall not dignify by recording, though we will note that his most recent publication contained no fewer than eleven factual errors regarding the chronology of the Carian succession, errors which we catalogued in a letter to his publisher that has, to date, received no reply. The objection is this: "Why Jarburg? What consequence can attach to a village of living jars, perched on a cliff-shelf in eastern Liurnia, accessible only by a series of precarious drops that would discourage any visitor of sound mind and functioning knees?"

To which we respond: it is precisely because the place was small, and its inhabitants humble, and its role in the grand movements of empires apparently negligible, that the events which transpired there during the sixty-third day of the Reddening—that period when the influence of the Lord of Blood's emissaries extended even into regions one might have supposed beneath their notice—illuminate the character of the age with a clarity that no account of demigods and their decaying ambitions can match.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves, a fault we have been accused of before, though never by anyone whose own work demonstrated a superior command of narrative sequence. Let us instead begin where a historian ought to begin, which is to say, with the place, and the people, and the precise quality of the light on the morning in question.

Jarburg occupied a series of natural terraces on the western cliffs of the Liurnian ravine, below the plateau where the Carian study hall stood in its state of scholarly decomposition, and above the mist-shrouded waters that collected in the basin far below. The terraces had been cultivated—or, to speak with greater precision, had cultivated themselves, for the jars who resided there possessed a relationship with the soil that the present author, despite extensive research, cannot claim to fully understand. Flowers grew in an abundance that seemed, to the human eye, almost aggressive. Lilies, daisies, and certain bulbous golden blooms whose Liurnian name translates roughly as "sunpuddles" crowded every available surface, growing between the paving stones, climbing the remnants of low walls, and in one case sprouting from a crack in the rim of a jar-person named Tumbkin, who regarded the flower as a hat and was not wrong to do so.

The jars themselves—and here we must pause to address a matter of terminology that has caused the present author considerable difficulty, for the word "jar" in common usage suggests an object, a vessel, a thing into which one puts other things, and while this is not, strictly speaking, inaccurate as a description of the living jars, it is woefully incomplete, rather in the manner that describing the Erdtree as "a plant" is not inaccurate but might be considered to omit certain relevant details—the jars, we say, were beings of clay and will, ambulatory, conversant, and possessed of a communal disposition that would put many human settlements to shame. They ranged in size from the very small, no larger than a man's fist, to the considerable, reaching perhaps to the waist of an adult human. They had arms, of a sort. They did not have legs in the conventional sense but managed locomotion through means that the natural philosophers of Raya Lucaria had debated for generations without reaching consensus, a fact which should surprise no one familiar with that institution's capacity for generating debate in inverse proportion to its capacity for generating conclusions.

The largest and most articulate of Jarburg's residents at the time of our account was a jar known as Jar-Bairn, and it is with this personage—for we shall use the term, and defend its use against any who would deny personhood to a being capable of grief, loyalty, and the accurate assessment of another's character—that our history is principally concerned.

On the morning of the sixty-third day of the Reddening, which is the date established by the Tarnished Archaeologist's reconstruction and which we see no reason to dispute, a woman came to Jarburg.

She came by the path, which is to say she came by falling, for the path to Jarburg was not, properly speaking, a path at all but rather a series of ledges, tombstones, and architectural projections down which a visitor was obliged to drop with increasing commitment and decreasing certainty about the condition of their ankles upon arrival. That the woman accomplished this descent without injury—or at least without injury sufficient to prevent her from walking—spoke to either considerable athleticism or considerable desperation, and as we shall see, she possessed both in adequate supply.

Her name was Caelwin, and she was a Tarnished of no particular renown.

We say "no particular renown" and mean it precisely, for in an age when every second wanderer in a tattered cloak claimed some grand purpose, some guiding grace, some destiny involving the Elden Ring and the reforging of a world's fundamental parameters, Caelwin claimed nothing of the sort. She had been, before the call of grace restored her to the Lands Between, a soldier in some foreign war whose name she had forgotten, fighting for a cause she could not recall, in a country that may or may not have existed by the time of her return. She had died there—of this she was certain, as the scar that crossed her throat from left to right was not the sort of wound one survived through medical intervention.

Grace had returned to her, as it had returned to all the Tarnished, manifesting as a faint golden luminescence at the periphery of her vision that pointed, with cheerful insistence, in directions she did not wish to go. She had followed it for a time. She had stopped following it some weeks prior to her arrival in Jarburg, having concluded—through a process of reasoning she did not share with the present author's sources and which we shall not, therefore, presume to reconstruct—that the guidance of grace was less a benevolent invitation than a summons to participate in a competition she had no interest in winning.

What she wanted, on the morning she fell into Jarburg, was breakfast.

"I don't suppose," she said, addressing the first jar she encountered—a small one, no larger than a cooking pot, which was sitting at the edge of a flower bed with the contented stillness of a creature that has no appointments—"that you have anything to eat?"

The jar, which did not speak—not all of them did, a fact that certain of our colleagues have failed to appreciate in their accounts, treating the living jars as a monolithic society when in truth they exhibited as much variation in temperament and capacity as any human village—simply rocked slightly in the wind and said nothing.

"I see," said Caelwin. "Well. That is a clear answer, and I thank you for it."

She continued along the terrace, stepping carefully between the sunpuddles, until she came upon Jar-Bairn.

Now, Jar-Bairn was at this time experiencing what we might charitably describe as a period of civic distress. The village's previous protector—a figure known to us only as the Tarnished warrior, whose identity has been the subject of much speculation that we shall not contribute to here, as speculation is the enemy of scholarship, a position we hold with absolute firmness despite what our aforementioned colleague has characterized as our "habitual willingness to speculate at length about matters for which not a shred of evidence exists," a characterization so unjust that we can only assume it was motivated by personal malice—the village's previous protector, we say, had departed some weeks before, and in the interval, Jarburg had received visitors of a less welcome sort.

These were the emissaries of Mohg, Lord of Blood.

The reader may be surprised to learn that the Lord of Blood's influence extended to so remote and seemingly valueless a location as Jarburg. We confess that we ourselves were surprised, until our research revealed the reason, which was this: the living jars contained, within their clay bodies, the remains of warriors. Champions. Persons of exceptional vitality whose physical remnants, compressed and amalgamated within the jars' interiors, constituted a source of power that was—to a faction whose entire theology was built upon the sanctification of blood and the extraction of vital essence—irresistible.

They wanted the jars for what was inside them.

"Oh," said Jar-Bairn, when Caelwin introduced herself—or rather, when Caelwin sat down on a low wall near the jar and said nothing for approximately ten minutes, which Jar-Bairn interpreted, correctly, as an introduction of the most courteous sort, for jars do not rush conversation any more than flowers rush blooming. "Oh, you're a Tarnished, then?"

"I am told so," said Caelwin.

"And you've come to Jarburg?"

"It appears that way."

"On purpose?"

"I will confess," said Caelwin, "that my arrival involved more gravity than intention."

"Gravity," repeated Jar-Bairn.

"I fell."

"Ah." Jar-Bairn considered this. "Most do."

There followed a conversation of the sort that our readers have, we hope, come to expect from the present author's reconstructions—which is to say, a conversation in which the two parties circled the subject of importance with the elaborate courtesy of duelists assessing each other's reach before the first engagement. We shall reproduce it as faithfully as our sources permit, noting only that the dialogue of living jars presents particular challenges to the historian, as their speech was often accompanied by a faint ceramic resonance that inflected their words with harmonics no written transcription can capture.

"You are troubled," said Caelwin.

"How, you perceive this?"

"I do."

"And from what do you derive this perception?"

"From the fact that you have not moved since I arrived, and that the flowers nearest to you are wilting, which suggests you have been sitting in the same spot long enough for your shadow to kill them."

Jar-Bairn looked down. This was true. Three sunpuddles had gone brown at his base.

"I have been thinking," he said.

"About?"

"About what potentate means."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Potentate. It is a word that was given to me. I was told I might become one. The potentate of Jarburg. But I do not know what it means to be a potentate when the village one is potentate of is being—" He stopped. His clay body made a sound that the present author's source described as "the noise a bell makes when it is cracked," and which we interpret, with some confidence, as the jar equivalent of a stifled sob.

"Being what?" said Caelwin.

"Broken," said Jar-Bairn. "They come at night. In red. They take the little ones. They take them and they—"

He stopped again.

The reader will forgive us if we do not immediately describe what the emissaries of the Lord of Blood did with the jars they took. The historian's duty is to truth, but truth, like a blade, benefits from being drawn at the correct moment rather than brandished indiscriminately at every opportunity. We shall return to this matter presently. For now, let it suffice to say that Caelwin understood, from the quality of Jar-Bairn's silence, what words would have conveyed less efficiently.

"How many?" she asked.

"Three. In the past fortnight. Tumbkin. Sprig. And the small one who sat by the eastern wall, who never spoke but who liked to be near the blue flowers. That one had no name. We were going to give a name. We had not decided yet."

Caelwin said nothing for a long time.

We must interrupt our narrative here—and we are aware that we have already interrupted it several times, for which we offer no apology, as interruption is the historian's prerogative and indeed obligation when context demands it—to observe that the decision Caelwin made in the next few moments was not, by any reasonable standard, a rational one. She was a single Tarnished of no particular power. She carried a straight sword of unremarkable provenance and a wooden shield whose surface was so scarred by prior use that its original heraldry—if it had ever possessed heraldry—was indecipherable. She had no patron, no maiden, no great rune, and no plan. She was hungry. She had been, by her own admission, fleeing the very notion of purpose.

And yet.

"When do they come?" she said.

"How, you wish to know—"

"When they come. Yes."

"But why do you—"

"Jar-Bairn."

"Yes?"

"When do they come?"

"At night. When the mist rises from the basin. There are three of them. They wear masks. White masks."

Caelwin nodded. She stood. She looked at the terraces of Jarburg—the flowers, the low walls, the narrow paths between the garden beds—with the assessing gaze of a person who is performing calculations that have nothing to do with horticulture.

"The path down," she said. "The one I fell along. Is there another way in?"

"There is a passage from the ravine below. Through the old aqueduct. It is flooded in places, but a person who did not mind wet boots could traverse it."

"And the emissaries—they come from below?"

"They do."

"Through the aqueduct?"

"I believe so. I have not—" Jar-Bairn's voice did the cracked-bell thing again. "I have not been brave enough to look."

Caelwin sat back down on the wall. She removed her boots—which were, we note, already wet, a detail that may or may not be significant—and began to attend to a blister on her left heel with the unselfconscious practicality of a person for whom blisters are a more immediate concern than eschatology.

"I will stay tonight," she said.

"How, you will—"

"Stay. Tonight. Here."

"But the emissaries—"

"Yes."

"They are servants of the Lord of Blood."

"So you have said."

"And you are—forgive me—you are one person."

"This is also true."

"With one sword."

"I have counted it, and I concur."

"And you propose to—"

"I propose nothing. I intend to sit here, in your very pleasant village, among your very pleasant flowers, and if persons arrive with the intention of breaking your people, I intend to make that intention difficult to execute."

Jar-Bairn was quiet for a moment.

"You are mad," he said, with something that the present author's source described as wonder.

"Almost certainly," said Caelwin. "Now. About breakfast."

We shall not dwell upon the hours that followed, as they were largely occupied by the consumption of boiled tubers—for Jarburg, despite its agrarian appearance, produced no food fit for human consumption, and Caelwin was reduced to foraging in a manner that the present author finds more appropriate to a treatise on survival botany than to a work of narrative history—and by a series of conversations between Caelwin and the various jars of the village, conversations which, while charming in themselves, contribute little to the advancement of our account.

We shall note only this: Caelwin spent the afternoon walking the perimeter of Jarburg with the attention of a woman who has defended positions before. She noted the width of the aqueduct entrance. She noted the placement of certain large stones that might, with effort, be repositioned. She noted that the terrace directly above the aqueduct's outlet was home to a collection of what the jars called "Alexander's children"—small warrior jars, none larger than a fist, who had been fashioned in imitation of the great Iron Fist Alexander and who possessed, despite their diminutive size, an enthusiasm for violence that their creator would have approved of.

"Can they throw themselves?" Caelwin asked Jar-Bairn.

"How, throw—"

"Throw. As in, propel. Launch. Project through the air at speed."

"They are soldjars," said Jar-Bairn, with a note of pride that was unmistakable despite the ceramic resonance. "They can do anything Alexander could do, if on a smaller scale."

"And when a soldjar breaks—"

"Oh, it is a grand thing. You should see it. A flash, a bang, and then—" He stopped. "You are thinking something."

"I am thinking several things."

"You are thinking of using Alexander's children as weapons."

"I would never use a person as a weapon without their consent."

Jar-Bairn considered this.

"I will ask them," he said. And then, after a pause: "They will say yes. They have been wanting someone to ask."

The reader may wonder—and the present author confesses to having wondered himself, during the long nights of research that attended the preparation of this account, nights made longer by the inadequacy of the tallow candles that are all a historian of limited means can afford, a circumstance we mention not to elicit pity but merely to establish the conditions under which scholarship is, in this degraded age, produced—the reader may wonder, we say, why the emissaries of the Lord of Blood troubled themselves with Jarburg at all. Three jars in a fortnight is not a harvest that would satisfy an ambition as vast as Mohg's. The Lord of Blood sought to raise a god. He required oceans of vitality, not teaspoons.

The answer, as nearly as we have been able to determine it, is this: the emissaries were not acting on Mohg's direct orders. They were freelancers. Initiates of the Sanguine Church whose zeal exceeded their station and whose understanding of their lord's theology was, at best, approximate. They collected blood and vital essence not because they had been commanded to, but because collection was, for them, a form of worship—the way a certain type of cleric collects alms not for the poor but for the pleasure of the collecting.

They were, in other words, the most dangerous kind of fanatic: the kind that acts without instruction, that improvises devotion, that finds in the silence of a distant lord permission to do whatever they wished.

There were three of them, as Jar-Bairn had said. They wore the white masks associated with the war surgeons of Mohg's service—masks that were, according to the item descriptions that have come down to us, "splattered with the lifeblood of their victims," a detail that the masks' original designer had intended as a record of service and that the emissaries had adopted as an aesthetic choice. They carried surgical implements modified for combat: curved blades, hooked probes, instruments designed not to kill but to open, to extract, to separate the contents from the container.

They arrived at Jarburg on the sixty-third night of the Reddening, as the mist rose from the basin below, filtering through the aqueduct and filling the lower terraces with a white haze that turned the flowers into ghosts of themselves.

Now we come to the matter we have been approaching with what we hope the reader will recognize as deliberate and purposeful restraint, for we are not one of those historians who believes that the depiction of violence is improved by haste. We hold, on the contrary, that violence—like love, like grief, like the preparation of a properly seasoned fish broth—deserves the historian's full and careful attention, and that to rush through it is to betray both the victim and the witness.

The first emissary came through the aqueduct with the confidence of a man who has done this before and expects no resistance. He emerged into the lower terrace, straightened, and took two steps toward the nearest cluster of small jars.

He did not take a third.

The stone that struck him was not large, but it had been dropped from a sufficient height—the terrace above, to be precise, where Caelwin had spent the better part of an hour positioning a loose piece of masonry at the lip of the ledge—that its impact upon the emissary's shoulder produced a sound that our source described, with admirable economy, as "wet."

The emissary went down. He did not go down quietly.

The second and third emissaries, who had been following at an interval of perhaps ten paces, responded with the coordinated efficiency of persons trained in violence. The second drew his curved blade and pressed forward. The third dropped back to the aqueduct entrance and began scanning the terraces above with the practiced vigilance of a lookout.

It was the lookout who saw Caelwin first. She was standing on the terrace above, silhouetted against the Liurnian sky, which at that hour was the color of old silver. She held her straight sword in her right hand. Her shield was on her left arm. She was not, we must note, an imposing figure. She was of middling height, lean in the way that people who have not eaten well for a long time are lean, and her armor—such as it was—consisted of a leather jerkin over a linen shirt, neither of which would have troubled a determined blade for more than an instant.

"You are trespassing," she said.

The third emissary looked at her. The mask made his expression unreadable, but the present author believes, based on what followed, that the expression was amusement.

"How, trespassing?" he said. "In a village of jars?"

"In a village," said Caelwin. "Yes."

"And you are its protector?"

"Tonight I am."

"One woman. With one sword."

"We have established this."

"Against three servants of the Lord of Blood."

"Two. Your friend with the crushed shoulder will not be participating."

The emissary glanced back. The first man was attempting to rise and failing, his left arm hanging at an angle that the anatomists of Raya Lucaria would have found instructive. The second emissary had not stopped advancing. He was on the lower terrace now, moving between the flower beds, his curved blade held low.

"Two, then," the third emissary conceded. "The numbers remain unfavorable."

"For you, yes," said Caelwin. "The village has also been consulted."

The emissary did not understand this statement. He would, very shortly.

What happened next occurred with a speed that our narrative voice is, we acknowledge, poorly suited to convey. We have observed elsewhere in this work that the historian's natural tempo is the adagio, and that events which transpire in seconds require a certain compression of our customary method. We shall do our best.

The second emissary, having reached the center of the lower terrace, found his footing suddenly uncertain. This was because the ground beneath his boots had come alive—or rather, the things he had taken for decorative stones arrayed among the flower beds were not stones at all but soldjars, Alexander's children, and they had been waiting with the coiled patience of their progenitor for someone to step within range.

They did not attack in the conventional sense. They detonated.

The first soldjar struck the emissary's right boot and burst with a crack that echoed off the ravine walls. The flash was, by several accounts, remarkably bright for so small a vessel. The emissary staggered. A second soldjar launched itself at his knee. A third struck his hip. Each impact was accompanied by a detonation of compressed fire, the warrior-essence within the jars converting itself, in the moment of shattering, from potential to kinetic energy in the most literal and spectacular fashion.

The emissary fell. He was not dead—the soldjars were small, and their individual force was limited—but he was burned, disoriented, and on the ground, which is where Caelwin wanted him.

She had not been idle during this. While the soldjars engaged the second emissary, she had dropped from the upper terrace to the lower—a drop of perhaps eight feet, which she absorbed with the practiced roll of a woman who has, as we noted earlier, a history of falling into Jarburg—and closed the distance to the third emissary, who found, to his considerable and expressed displeasure, that he was now fighting a woman with a straight sword in a space so narrow that his longer curved blade was more hindrance than help.

The third emissary was the better fighter. This was evident from the first engagement. His technique was precise, economical, and informed by a tradition of surgical violence that prioritized the opening of flesh over the destruction of bone. He cut Caelwin twice in the first thirty seconds—once on the forearm, once across the ribs—and both cuts were shallow, deliberate, designed to bleed rather than to kill.

He was, in other words, practicing his vocation. He was treating Caelwin as a subject rather than an opponent. This was, as it turned out, a miscalculation.

Caelwin was not the better fighter. She was, however, the angrier one.

She took the third cut—this one across the thigh, deeper than the others—and did not retreat. She stepped inside the emissary's reach, and drove the pommel of her straight sword into the white mask with a force that cracked the ceramic and, from the sound that followed, the face beneath it.

The emissary staggered back. Caelwin followed. She struck again—this time with the edge, a descending cut that caught the emissary's weapon arm at the elbow and produced results that we shall describe only by noting that the curved blade clattered to the stones still held by a hand that was no longer attached to anything.

The third emissary sat down heavily among the sunpuddles.

"You are bleeding," he observed.

"Yes," said Caelwin.

"Considerably."

"I am aware."

"You should attend to that."

"After," said Caelwin.

"After what?"

"After you leave."

"We will leave," he said.

"And we will not return."

"That," said Caelwin, "would be wise."

We note, with the historian's obligation to completeness, that they did not return.

What followed was a period of approximately six weeks during which Caelwin remained in Jarburg.

Jar-Bairn asked her about it on the fourth day.

"You are still here," he said.

"I am."

"I thought you intended to leave."

"I did intend to leave."

"And yet—"

"And yet I have not left."

"May I ask why?"

"I have been thinking," she said, "about what potentate means."

"And I believe it means this: a potentate is a person who stays."

"Not a person who conquers. Not a person who collects great runes, or sits upon a throne, or reforges the laws of the world according to their preference. A potentate is a person who is present. Who remains. Who, when the jars are frightened, does not leave."

"That is not," said Jar-Bairn carefully, "the definition I have encountered in any of the texts available to me."

"The texts available to you were written by people who had never been to Jarburg."

"This is true."

"Well, then."

"Potentate," he said, trying the word again, as one tries on a garment to assess its fit.

"Potentate," agreed Caelwin.

She stayed six weeks. When she left—and she did leave, for she was, after all, Tarnished, and the Lands Between does not permit its chosen instruments to rest for long in pleasant villages among flowers—she left the straight sword behind, driven into the earth at the entrance to the aqueduct, where it served both as a marker and as a warning, and where, for all the present author knows, it stands yet.

Jar-Bairn did become potentate of Jarburg. What is less documented is the speech he gave upon assuming the title, a speech that consisted of exactly four words, delivered to the assembled jars of the village on a morning of gold and mist:

"I will not leave."

We are told that the soldjars, upon hearing these words, detonated spontaneously in celebration, producing a display of light and sound that was visible from the plateau above, where a passing Carian scholar noted it in her journal as "fireworks from the jar-village; cause unknown."

The cause was known. It was joy.

But we hold—and we will defend this position against any critic, named or unnamed, published or unpublished, solvent or otherwise—that the significance of a historical event is not determined solely by its scale. A woman who falls into a village of jars and decides to stay is not a footnote. She is a thesis.

The thesis is this: that in a world where gods betray their shadows, where mothers pit their children against each other, where the greatest devotion goes unrewarded and the deepest love unreciprocated, it is possible—not guaranteed, not common, but possible—for a person of no particular renown to choose, freely and without cosmic mandate, to protect something small and fragile and alive.

This, the present author submits, is worth recording.

Even if no one publishes the supplementary volume.
