The Fitting

by Tess Jyanssen

The boy had grown a second horn overnight, which meant Vesna's measurements from yesterday were useless.

She stood in the workshop doorway with her calipers in one hand and a cup of something hot and resinous in the other. (Not tea, technically. The bark of the erdtree sapling outside the western forge produced a brew that tasted like pine tar mixed with ambition, and every armorer in the district drank it because it kept your hands from shaking during close-tolerance work.) She looked at the horn.

It had come in at a slight leftward cant, maybe twelve degrees off the sagittal line. Unusual. Most secondary cranial aspects grew symmetrically. The primary horn, the one she'd already measured and templated and begun shaping a channel for in the helm's crown plate, curved backward and slightly to the right. Classic Crucible aspect geometry: logarithmic spiral, growth rings visible if you knew where to look, surface texture somewhere between polished antler and river stone.

The new horn was different. Shorter, thicker, blunted at the tip in a way that meant it was still growing. It had split the skin above his left temple sometime after midnight. The blood had dried in a line down his jaw. He hadn't cleaned it.

He was fourteen years old. He'd been selected for the Order of the Crucible six days ago. He was trying very hard to look like the kind of person who did not notice when bone erupted from his skull.

Vesna noticed. Vesna noticed everything. This was why she was the best fitter in the district. It was also why she slept badly.

"Sit," she said.

He sat. The stool creaked. He was bigger than he'd been three weeks ago when the temple assessors first brought him in for typing. His shoulders had broadened by nearly two inches (she could see it in the way his training tunic pulled across the back, the seam allowance eaten up) and his hands had thickened at the knuckle in a way that suggested caudal development within the year. Tail, probably. The hand thickening usually preceded it by four to six weeks. She'd fitted enough squires to know the sequence.

"When did the second one come in?"

"Dawn prayer." He paused. "It hurt."

"They do."

She set down the cup and picked up the sketchbook. The page she'd been working from was already obsolete. The cranial template she'd drawn (a beautiful piece of work, if she said so herself, and she said so frequently, to anyone who would listen) showed a single-horn channel with reinforced lateral bracing and a hinge point at the occipital ridge. Standard Crucible Knight fitting. She'd done maybe three hundred of them.

A second horn at twelve degrees off-axis meant starting over.

Not the whole helm. Just the crown plate and the left temporal reinforcement and the interior padding architecture and the ventilation channels and the weight distribution across the gorget attachment points. So: the whole helm.

"I need to measure the new one," Vesna said. "Hold still. If you move I will charge you for the calipers I break."

He held still. She measured.

Here is the thing about fitting armor to a Crucible Knight that no one who hasn't done it understands: the body is not finished. A knight's aspects manifest over years, sometimes decades. The first signs appear in adolescence and the full expression doesn't stabilize until well into middle age. Some never stabilize.

Vesna had fitted a knight named Ordovis who sprouted a new aspect every three to five years for his entire career. Started with horns, developed tail at twenty-two, grew patches of dense interlocking scales across his shoulders and upper back at thirty, and then at forty-one began producing something along his spine that Vesna could only describe as the beginning of wings.

(She had described it exactly that way to the temple scholars and they'd looked at her like she'd said something profound. She had not said something profound. She had said what it looked like. The scholars then spent four months writing a treatise about the theological implications of wing-aspect manifestation in mature knights, and Vesna spent four months figuring out how to build a backplate that could open along pre-scored lines when the wings decided to actually arrive. Her contribution to the world was more useful. She was certain of this.)

The problem is tolerances. It is always tolerances.

A static body, you fit to the body. You measure, you shape, you hammer, you fit again. The steel learns the person. Every decent armorer knows this.

A Crucible body is what it is becoming. You are not fitting armor to a person. You are fitting armor to a trajectory. The measurements you take today are wrong by next season. The horn that's twelve degrees off-axis now might correct toward center as it lengthens, or it might cant further, or it might branch. Branching is rare but Vesna had seen it twice, and both times it happened inside a helm that was not designed for it, and both times the result was a knight with a cracked helm and a headache that lasted a week and a fitter who had to explain to the temple quartermaster why she needed to start over.

So you build in flex. You score the steel along growth lines so it can open without cracking. You use riveted plates instead of solid forgings wherever an aspect might emerge. You leave expansion channels packed with compressed felted erdtree bark (cheap, mildly sacred, and therefore subsidized by the temple, which was the best combination of qualities any material could have). You design every piece of the armor to fail gracefully when the body inside it changes.

This is engineering. This is the most interesting engineering problem in the Lands Between and Vesna would fight anyone who said otherwise.

The boy's name was Siluren. He was from a village in the eastern lowlands where his family raised goats and grew a legume that Vesna had never heard of until Siluren described it with the passionate specificity of someone who had spent his entire childhood shelling it. (She liked him for this. A person who could talk about legumes the way she talked about steel was a person who paid attention to the world.) He'd manifested his first horn at twelve. His parents brought him to the district temple. The assessors typed him as probable dual-horn with caudal potential and rated his aspect density at seven out of ten, which was high. High enough that the Order wanted him.

The Order always wanted the high-density ones. More aspects meant more incantation channels. A knight who could manifest both tail sweep and horn charge in combat was worth three knights who could only do one.

The temple scholars talked about this in terms of divine favor and proximity to the primordial wellspring of life. Vesna talked about it in terms of fitting logistics: a high-density knight needed more flexible armor, more frequent refittings, and a significantly larger budget. Aspect-rated armor cost four times what standard plate cost. For a knight like Ordovis, the lifetime fitting expense exceeded the cost of his horse, his weapons, his lodging, and his food combined.

She had made this argument to the temple quartermaster once. The quartermaster had responded that the body was sacred and that one does not economize on the housing of the divine. Vesna had responded that one absolutely does economize on the housing of the divine if the alternative is the divine's armor splitting open during a border engagement because the budget didn't cover a second refitting this year. The quartermaster had not invited her to subsequent budget meetings.

She won the argument anyway. She always won. Not because she was charming (she was not charming) but because the alternative to funding her work was dead knights, and dead knights meant the Order had to recruit and train replacements and then those replacements needed armor and then Vesna was right back where she started except now she was fitting two sets instead of one.

Economics always won. Theology was just the story economics told about itself.

She thought about Siluren's parents sometimes, though she had never met them. She thought about them because she had met a hundred parents exactly like them: lowland farmers, herders, craftspeople, people who understood the material world in the specific and useful way that Vesna respected most, who had watched their child begin to change and felt a complicated mixture of terror and pride that they would never fully sort out. The temple assessors came. They measured the horn, graded the density, and issued a classification number. Then they took the child.

The parents did not get a say. The Order's charter was older than any kingdom currently standing, and its claim on high-density manifestants was absolute. You could refuse. But refusing meant your child grew up outside the Order's protection, and an unprotected manifestant was a target for every village that feared the Crucible's strangeness and every bandit who knew that a fresh horn, properly harvested, could be ground into a powder that the apothecaries in the western cities bought for more than its weight in silver.

Vesna did not judge the parents who surrendered their children. She judged the system that made surrender the rational choice. Then she went back to work, because the system was not going to dismantle itself on account of her disapproval, and in the meantime the children still needed armor that fit.

Siluren sat very still while she measured the new horn. She wrote down the numbers. She sketched the growth angle. She calculated the stress distributions and the material requirements and the hours and the cost, and she was good at this, and she knew she was good at this, and knowing did not make it easier. It made it more interesting. Those are different things.

"The new channel will add weight to the left side," she told him. "Maybe a quarter-pound. I'll compensate in the right temporal plate but you'll feel the difference for a week. If it gives you headaches, come back."

"It already gives me headaches."

"The horn or the helmet?"

"Both."

She almost smiled. "Welcome to the Order."

He left. Her next appointment was already waiting.

Knight-Commander Ysilde had been manifesting for thirty-one years. She was Vesna's longest-running client, her most expensive project, and the closest thing to a masterwork that Vesna would ever produce. Ysilde's primary aspects were cranial: three horns, two curved and one straight, in a configuration that the temple scholars called the trident formation and that Vesna called a fitting nightmare. Her dorsal ridge ran from the base of her skull to her mid-back, dense as turtle shell and harder than anything Vesna could produce in a forge.

Secondary aspects: caudal (a tail, thick and muscular, with a bony club at its tip that had been adding mass for four years) and integumentary (golden-brown scale across her shoulders and forearms, with new patches along her ribs since the last fitting eight months ago).

Ysilde's current armor was Vesna's sixth set for her. The fifth had lasted two years before the dorsal ridge outgrew the backplate's expansion channels. The fourth had lasted three. The third had been catastrophically destroyed when Ysilde's tail club developed faster than projected and punched through the rear tasset during a training exercise. The tasset was a loss. The training dummy was a loss. The training dummy's mounting post, which was sunk three feet into packed earth, was also a loss.

Vesna had been impressed despite herself.

"Left pauldron's binding," Ysilde said, by way of greeting.

"Which strap?"

"The upper. Where the new scale came in."

Vesna walked around her. The scale growth along Ysilde's left shoulder had advanced maybe a centimeter since the last fitting. Not much in absolute terms. But scale was dense, and dense meant the padding compressed differently, and compressed differently meant the strap tension changed, and changed strap tension meant the pauldron sat wrong, and a pauldron that sat wrong meant a knight who couldn't raise her sword arm above sixty degrees without the plate catching on the gorget.

Tolerances. Always tolerances. The scholars wrote papers about the theology of bodily transformation. Vesna kept people alive by noticing that a strap was a quarter-inch too tight.

She unbolted the pauldron. She examined the padding. She measured the scale.

"You've been training in this."

"Obviously."

"With the binding."

"The binding started ten days ago. I have obligations."

"Your obligations include not destroying a six-thousand-mark suit of armor because you were too proud to come in for a strap adjustment."

Ysilde looked at her. Fifty-three years old, six feet tall without the horns, and she had killed more border raiders than any other knight in the district. She also had the slightly sheepish expression of a person who knew Vesna was right.

"The left greave is also rubbing," Ysilde said. "Since you're already angry."

Vesna was not angry. Vesna was delighted. This was her favorite part: the detective work, the cascade of small failures traced back to a single root cause, the satisfaction of understanding a body better than its owner did. She removed the greave. The new scale on Ysilde's shin had grown in a slightly convex pattern, pushing the padding outward by maybe two millimeters.

Two millimeters. That was the margin between comfortable and agonizing when you were wearing steel against your skin for twelve hours a day.

She refitted both pieces in an hour. New padding cut from her stock. New strap holes punched at adjusted intervals. The pauldron went back on and Ysilde raised her arm above her head, rotated it, drew an imaginary sword strike. No catch.

"Good," Ysilde said. Which was as close to a compliment as Ysilde got.

Vesna watched her walk out of the workshop. The tail swung behind her in a slow counter-rhythm to her stride, the bone club at its tip catching the light. Thirty-one years of manifesting. Three horns, a dorsal ridge, a tail, and scale coverage approaching forty percent. Ysilde's body had been becoming something else for longer than Siluren had been alive.

And it was beautiful.

Vesna did not use that word carelessly. She preferred "elegant" for things that worked well and "impressive" for things that were large and "interesting" for things she was still making up her mind about. But Ysilde in full aspect, in armor that Vesna had shaped to honor every curve and plate and spine of that transformed body, was beautiful. The armor did not hide what Ysilde was. It declared it.

That was the job. Not containment. Declaration.

The workshop was quiet after that. Morning light through the western window, positioned to illuminate the workbench without casting shadows from the roof beams. (Vesna had positioned it herself when she took over from old Margrave. Margrave was a gifted smith and a terrible architect.) The erdtree sapling outside was doing its golden thing. Pollen drifted.

Somewhere in the district a smith was already hammering: the rhythm was a draw, not a forge, which meant wire. You could tell. The draw rhythm was faster and more regular, three quick strikes and a pull. Forge rhythm was irregular, responsive, the smith following the metal's lead. Vesna could identify every smith in the district by their hammer rhythm. This was not a useful skill. She was proud of it anyway.

She sat with the sketchbook.

She looked at the sketch of Siluren's asymmetric horns and thought about what they would be in twenty years. The asymmetry that looked awkward on a fourteen-year-old would become a sweeping crown. The tail would add counterbalance. The scaling, if it came (she was guessing based on the skin texture behind his ears, which was just beginning to show the stippling) would give him armor he grew himself, under the armor she made him.

She would fit him eight, maybe ten times over those twenty years. Each fitting a conversation between his body and her steel. Each piece of armor a record of who he was at that moment: the angle of the horn, the length of the tail, the density of the scale. You could lay them out in sequence and read a life.

Old Margrave had told her this when she was an apprentice. She had thought it was sentimental.

She did not think it was sentimental anymore.

Here is what Vesna thought about, on mornings when the light was good and the bark tea was still warm:

She thought about how the Crucible made people who were more than one thing. How the sacred and the bestial were not opposites but layers of the same material. How a boy could grow bone from his skull and that bone could be divine.

She thought about the engineering problem, which was the same as the theological problem, which was the same as the political problem: what do you do with a body that will not stay one shape? Do you fight it? Do you contain it? Or do you build something flexible enough to hold it while it becomes whatever it is becoming?

Vesna built armor. That was her answer.

She picked up the calipers. She started the new template. The left temporal plate needed to be wider by an inch and a quarter, scored at thirty and sixty degrees to allow for branching, with a secondary channel that could be opened or sealed depending on whether the horn corrected or deviated further.

She worked.

Outside, the erdtree sapling dropped a golden seed onto the windowsill. Small and bright, the size of a fingernail. Vesna looked at it for a moment. She put it in her pocket. Those were worth something, technically, to the right buyer.

She had never sold one. She did not intend to start.

Some things you kept because they were beautiful and because the world made them without being asked.

She went back to work.
