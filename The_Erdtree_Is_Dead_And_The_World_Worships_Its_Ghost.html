The Erdtree Is Dead And The World Worships Its Ghost

by Kontextmaschine

The Erdtree Is Dead and the World Worships Its Ghost

Kontextmaschine

So someone's going around saying the Lands Between is a medieval fantasy world and — no. Stop. It's not. It's not even close. The Lands Between is a colonial occupation that has been running so long the colonizers forgot they're colonizers, which if you've spent any time at all studying American territorial governance or the British Raj or honestly the postwar occupation of Japan is the most realistic thing in the entire game. The Greater Will sent a golden star carrying the Elden Beast to a land that already had civilizations, already had gods, already had a functioning cosmological order — the ancient dragons had an Elden Lord, Placidusax is literally still sitting in his crumbling temple waiting for a god that left — and what the Erdtree did was what every imperial project does: it replaced the local operating system, declared the previous one barbaric, and then claimed it had been there forever.

And the thing people don't sit with long enough is that it worked. It worked the way all these projects work — not through some clean genocide but through the slower process of making the old way illegible. The Crucible is the perfect example. The Crucible was the original condition of life in the Lands Between, this primordial mingling where people had horns and tails and wings and all of it was considered divine, and the Golden Order's entire move was to reclassify those features as impurity. That's not theology, that's an ethnosuppressionist campaign with ecclesiastical characteristics. You're born with horns? Congratulations, you're an Omen now, you live underground, and if you're lucky they just saw the horns off instead of killing you. Morgott and Mohg are the two possible responses to this and the game is smart enough to show you both — Morgott who loves the system that despises him (and protects it with total devotion that is never once acknowledged or rewarded, he dies and the narration literally says "he was never loved") and Mohg who says fuck it, if I'm defiled then I'll build a kingdom out of defilement, which also doesn't work but at least has the dignity of honesty.

The Misbegotten are the same thing from the labor side. Crucible traits — wings, bestial features — once divine, now your ticket to being a beast of burden. They revolt at Castle Morne and the game frames this as a crisis to be resolved and then quietly, through item descriptions, makes clear these beings were enslaved because the new theological order needed a permanent underclass. The revolt isn't the aberration. The slavery is.

What kills me — and this is where the game is actually doing something more sophisticated than most actual historiography I've encountered — is the Greattree. Because the Erdtree isn't even the original tree. There was a Greattree before it, connected to the underground root system where the Siofra and Ainsel rivers meet, and the catacombs throughout the Lands Between aren't Erdtree funerary complexes, they're Greattree funerary complexes, they predate the whole Golden Order. The new religion didn't just conquer the old one — it literally grew on top of it, using the same root infrastructure, the same sacred architecture, the same emotional need for a cycle of death and rebirth, and just rebranded the whole thing gold. If you've studied how Christianity absorbed Roman temple sites or how the Mormons repurposed Masonic ritual or — honestly, the most direct parallel is how the postwar Japanese constitution was written by Americans but presented as an organic expression of Japanese democratic aspiration — same root system, new canopy, everyone agrees not to look down.

And then the canopy dies and nobody notices. That's the part that makes the Lands Between genuinely unsettling as a setting rather than just an interesting one. The Erdtree the player sees is a spectral projection. The real tree burned. There's ash choking the streets of Leyndell, there's wax seals on the doors of citizens trying to keep the ash out, the golden seeds come from "illusory trees." The first Godfrey you fight is a golden phantom conjured at the peak of his power before his tarnishing — a hologram of past glory, which, like, if you want to talk about what it felt like to visit the Washington Mall in 2006 and see the Lincoln Memorial while knowing what was happening in Iraq, there's your metaphor. The institution's physical grandeur is a ghost projected over the ruin. Everyone performs reverence. The reverence is structurally load-bearing. If you stop performing it the whole thing collapses, so you don't stop.

Gideon Ofnir is the character who sees this most clearly and it breaks him. He studied everything — the Two Fingers, the Erdtree, the histories, the demigods — and his conclusion is that the system is irreparable. "The all-knowing knows. No one can become Elden Lord." He fights you not to win the throne but to prevent anyone from sitting on it, because he has determined that the throne itself is rotten and that anyone who takes it is just starting the cycle again. This is the doomer intellectual problem — guy who does all the reading, understands the structural critique perfectly, and then decides the correct response is to make sure nothing changes because change just produces a new version of the same failure. (The Robert McNamara trajectory, basically — total information, total paralysis, except Gideon goes one step further into active sabotage.)

The endings are where the game gets genuinely interesting about this because they're all different answers to the same question: what do you do with a broken colonial infrastructure? Goldmask's answer is remove the gods and keep the system — pure abstract law, the fickleness removed, which the Tarnished Archaeologist correctly identifies as fascism (or at minimum a kind of cosmic technocracy where the irregularities that make life livable are engineered out). Fia's answer is accept that death is broken and build a new order around the brokenness — let Those Who Live in Death have a place, stop pretending the clean cycle still works. The Dung Eater's answer is the most horrifying and also the most egalitarian: make everyone an Omen, spread the curse universally, and the category of "impure" ceases to exist because there's no "pure" left to define it against. Which is monstrous but is also — I mean, if you take seriously the idea that the Omen's oppression is the foundational crime of the Golden Order, then universalizing the condition does solve the problem, in the same way that universal suffrage solved the problem of who gets to vote by eliminating the category of people who don't.

Ranni's answer is the only one that actually breaks the cycle: take the Elden Ring and leave. Remove it from the Lands Between entirely. Go into the cold dark of outer space where no Outer God can reach it. Her ending is liberation through distance — not fixing the system but removing the thing that makes the system possible. She explicitly warns her consort about the solitude. She's not promising a better world. She's promising no world at all, at least not one governed by these rules. Which — and I say this as someone who spent a lot of time studying the Japanese postwar constitutional debate — sometimes the most radical thing you can do is not write a new constitution. Sometimes it's taking the pen away.

The thing the game never lets you do is go back. There's no option to restore the world before the Greater Will arrived. You can't return to the Crucible, can't un-conquer the dragons, can't give the Nox their sky back. The Ancient Dynasty's tree worship evolved into the Erdtree cult which became the Golden Order which broke, and every ending is a choice about what to build on the rubble. Not what to restore — what to build. The game understands that the layers are load-bearing. You can't excavate your way to an original condition because the original condition is also a construction, the Dynast was already a prophet who replaced the pluralistic nature worship that came before him. It's palimpsests all the way down.

The merchants are the detail I keep coming back to because the merchants are the game's thesis statement compressed into a single faction. The Great Caravan — nomadic traders, harmless, different — accused of heretical beliefs, rounded up, buried alive underground. And in their despair they summoned the Frenzied Flame, which is the force that would burn literally everything back to undifferentiated nothing. The Golden Order's most destructive enemy exists because the Golden Order committed a genocide against people who were no threat. The logical endpoint of persecution is the persecution's victims deciding that if this is what order looks like, then order itself has to go. That's not a fantasy storyline. That's the entire history of radicalization in three sentences, and the game buries it in item descriptions for a musical instrument and a set of merchant's clothes because it knows you'll either find it or you won't, and if you find it, you'll understand why the Three Fingers exist, and if you don't, you'll just think the chaos ending is edgy nihilism. (It is edgy nihilism — that's the point. The nihilism has a material cause. It always does.)

I keep thinking about Morgott dying alone defending a dead tree that never loved him and I keep thinking about Hewg chained to the Roundtable Hold forging weapons he doesn't remember being asked to forge and I keep thinking about Thops sitting outside the Academy his entire life developing a spell that turns out to be genuinely revolutionary and dying before anyone acknowledges it. The game is full of people whose devotion exceeds the object of their devotion. People serving institutions that have already failed, defending borders that no longer mean anything, loving gods that are at war with themselves. The Mausoleum Knights cut off their own heads to serve soulless demigods who may never revive. Finlay carried Malenia's unconscious body across the entire continent alone. Niall commanded spirits to defend a long-dead master because no one told him the war was over.

These aren't heroic sacrifices in the traditional fantasy sense. They're the behavior of people embedded in systems that have outlived their purpose but still generate loyalty because the alternative — accepting that it was all for nothing — is worse than continuing. Which is not a medieval problem. That's the problem of every veteran's organization, every declining church, every state agency running on institutional memory after the mission changed three administrations ago. Elden Ring understands that people will die for a symbol long after the thing the symbol represented has rotted from the inside, and that this is simultaneously the most noble and the most futile thing human beings do, and that you cannot cleanly separate the nobility from the futility because they're the same impulse expressing itself under different conditions of information.

The Erdtree is dead. The world worships its ghost. Someone's going to reforge the Elden Ring and set new parameters and the cycle will start again with new names and new theologies layered over the same root system, and in a thousand years somebody will dig up the ruins of Leyndell and find the ash and the wax seals and the golden phantoms and try to figure out what happened here, and they'll get it mostly wrong, because that's also how it works.
