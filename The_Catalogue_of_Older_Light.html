The Catalogue of Older Light

by Pellegrin

The document arrived in Leyndell in a sealed crate, confiscated during the sealing of the Nokron access shaft in the forty-third year of Queen Marika's reign. It was logged as Item 1167 in the Index of Heretical Materials maintained by the Office of Doctrinal Integrity, described in the intake ledger as "astronomical charts, provenance unknown, written in Old Nox with marginal annotations in a second hand." It was placed on a restricted shelf in the Archive of Proscribed Knowledge, where it sat undisturbed for two hundred and eleven years.

Caelwin found it by accident.

He was not looking for Nox astronomy. He was looking for a misattributed incantation scroll that the Archdeacon of the Erdtree Sanctuary believed had been misfiled during the Second Reorganization of the Archive — a bureaucratic catastrophe that had displaced roughly four thousand items over a period of eight months and which the Office of Doctrinal Integrity preferred not to discuss. Caelwin had been assigned to the recovery effort because he was junior enough to be given unpleasant work and senior enough to be trusted with restricted materials. He was, in his own estimation, the ideal functionary: too competent to fail, too obscure to threaten anyone.

The crate was on the wrong shelf. The incantation scroll was not inside it. What was inside it was a bound codex of two hundred and fourteen pages, written in ink that had oxidized to a dark brown, in a script that Caelwin recognized as Old Nox only because he had memorized the character set as part of his doctrinal training. The Office required its analysts to be able to identify heretical languages. It did not require them to read those languages. Caelwin could do both.

He opened the codex. He should not have. The regulations governing restricted materials specify that any item in the Archive of Proscribed Knowledge may be accessed only with written authorization from the Archdeacon or a designated deputy. Caelwin had neither. He opened the codex because the binding was unusual — a pale leather he did not recognize, stitched with silver thread in a pattern that resembled a star chart — and because he was, underneath the functionary, a scholar, and scholars open books.

The first page read: A Catalogue of the Observed Positions of Seven Hundred and Twelve Stars as Recorded from the South Tower of Nokstella over a Period of Forty Years, Compiled by Astronomer-Royal Leshaia of the Third Administration, with Corrections and Annotations by Her Student Eseld.

Caelwin sat down on the floor of the restricted archive, which was cold and slightly damp, and began to read.

The catalogue was, at its surface, exactly what it claimed to be. Seven hundred and twelve stellar positions, recorded with a precision that Caelwin found startling. Each entry included the star's apparent magnitude, its color as classified on a six-point scale that Caelwin did not recognize, its angular distance from a reference point that Leshaia called the Nail, and its observed position at four intervals across the forty-year observation period. The entries were dry. The methodology was rigorous. The data was meticulous.

Entry 1: "Nail-star, reference zero. Magnitude 1.2, color class Amber-Deep. Position unchanged across observation period. This star serves as the fixed point from which all angular measurements in this catalogue are derived."

Entry 47: "Companion of the Nail, magnitude 3.1, color class Blue-Pale. Angular distance from Nail: 7.4 degrees at 214 compass. Unchanged across observation period. Note: this star is not visible from all positions within Nokstella. The vault curvature occludes it from the eastern residential quarter."

Entry 203: "The Shepherd's Left Hand, magnitude 2.6, color class White-Clear. Angular distance from Nail: 43.2 degrees at 87 compass. Position has shifted 0.3 degrees over the observation period. Leshaia notes: 'This is within the expected range of the rotation mechanism's accumulated drift. The enchantment will be recalibrated in the Sixth Administration.'"

Caelwin found himself reading the entries the way he read incantation formulae — not for content, initially, but for structure. He was cataloguing the catalogue. The method was sound. The nomenclature was unfamiliar but internally consistent. Whatever else the Nox had been, they had been careful.

The annotations were something else.

Eseld, whoever she was, had written in the margins in a smaller, sharper hand, and her annotations were not corrections to the data. They were corrections to the premise. Leshaia had recorded the positions of seven hundred and twelve stars as observed from the false sky of Nokstella. Eseld's annotations compared those positions to a second data set — one she referred to, without further explanation, as "the Inherited Catalogue."

Caelwin did not know what the Inherited Catalogue was. He read on.

Eseld's annotation beside Entry 47: "The Companion of the Nail is catalogued in the Inherited Record at 7.4 degrees, 214 compass. It is catalogued in the Karath surface observation of Year 1170 at 7.4 degrees, 214 compass. These agree. This star has not moved."

Eseld's annotation beside Entry 203: "The Shepherd's Left Hand is catalogued in the Inherited Record at 43.2 degrees, 87 compass. It is catalogued in the Karath surface observation at 31.6 degrees, 74 compass. The disagreement is 11.6 degrees in position and 13 degrees in compass bearing. This is not drift. This is displacement."

By the twentieth page, Eseld's annotations had become a running argument with her teacher. Leshaia's catalogue recorded the false sky as it existed in the Third Administration. Eseld's annotations demonstrated, star by star, that certain positions in the false sky did not correspond to the positions those stars currently held in the real sky above the surface.

This was not surprising. The false sky was a construction. It was built from memory and engineering and the accumulated grief of a banished civilization. Of course it did not match.

What was surprising was the direction of the error.

Caelwin had studied Golden Order cosmology as part of his training. He knew the standard positions of the major constellations — the Crucible Crown, the Elden Arc, the Ring of Regression. He knew the Fundamentalist framework: the stars were expressions of the Greater Will's causality, their positions encoding the order's mathematical structure. The stars did not move because the order did not change. Goldmask himself had written that stellar fixity was proof of divine intention.

Eseld's annotations showed that the false sky's positions were not random errors. They were internally consistent. They described a sky — but not the sky that Caelwin could see from the Erdtree Sanctuary on a clear night.

The false sky's constellations were rotated. Shifted. Certain stars that occupied prominent positions in the Golden Order's sky were absent from the false sky entirely. The constellation that the Fundamentalists called the Elden Arc — five stars arranged in the shape of the Elden Ring's outer curve, a formation cited in every major treatise on Golden Order cosmology as proof that the Ring's structure was encoded in the heavens — did not exist in Leshaia's catalogue.

Five stars. The most theologically significant formation in the sky.

Absent.

Eseld's annotation beside Entry 412, where one of the Arc stars should have been: "There is no star at this position in the Inherited Catalogue. There is no star at this position in Leshaia's catalogue. There is a star at this position in every post-banishment surface observation I have obtained. This star is new."

Caelwin turned pages. The annotations grew denser. By Entry 500, Eseld had stopped annotating individual stars and begun tabulating. Of the seven hundred and twelve stars in Leshaia's catalogue, five hundred and sixty-five matched the post-banishment surface sky to within measurement error. One hundred and forty-seven did not. Of these one hundred and forty-seven, one hundred and twenty-eight had been displaced — moved from the positions recorded in both the Inherited Catalogue and the false sky to new positions in the post-banishment sky. Nineteen had been removed entirely. And seven stars in the post-banishment sky had no corresponding entry in either the Inherited Catalogue or Leshaia's catalogue.

Seven new stars. Nineteen deleted. One hundred and twenty-eight repositioned.

Caelwin was trained to evaluate evidence. He knew the difference between a coincidence, a pattern, and an argument. This was an argument. It was an argument made of numbers by a dead woman in the margins of someone else's research, and it was either the most elaborate forgery he had ever encountered or it was true.

He kept reading. He could not stop. The scholar in him had taken the reins from the functionary, and the scholar wanted to know.

Eseld's annotation beside Entry 614: "The star the surface observers call the Erdtree Crown — magnitude 0.8, the third brightest object in the post-banishment sky, cited in Goldmask's 'On Regression' as the stellar anchor of the Golden Order's geometric proof — does not exist in the Inherited Catalogue. It does not exist in Leshaia's catalogue. I have checked forty-seven pre-banishment sources. None contain it. This star was placed in the sky after we were buried. The brightest proof of the Golden Order's divine geometry is younger than the Nox's imprisonment."

The false sky showed different stars.

Caelwin set the codex down on his lap and looked at the ceiling of the restricted archive, which was stone, and dark, and had no stars of any kind.

He picked the codex up again.

Eseld's annotation on page forty-seven: "The positions recorded by Leshaia match the Inherited Catalogue to within three arc-minutes. The Inherited Catalogue was compiled before the banishment. The false sky was built from the Inherited Catalogue. The false sky shows the sky as it existed before we were driven underground. The question is not why the false sky differs from the current sky. The question is why the current sky differs from the false sky."

Caelwin read this annotation four times.

The implication was precise and it was devastating. If the false sky had been built from a pre-banishment catalogue — if it showed the sky as the Nox had observed it before the Greater Will cast them underground — then the differences between the false sky and the current sky were not errors of reproduction. They were evidence that the real sky had changed.

Stars had moved. Stars had been added. Stars had been removed.

The Greater Will had edited the sky.

Caelwin closed the codex. He sat in the cold archive and breathed and thought about what he had just read and what it meant if it was true and what it meant if it was not true and which of those possibilities was worse.

If it was true, then the stars were not fixed. The stars were not expressions of unchanging divine order. The stars were a text, and the text had been revised, and the revision had been performed by the Greater Will after the Nox were driven underground where they could not see it happening. The current sky — the sky that Goldmask cited as proof of causality, the sky that the Fundamentalists held up as the visible structure of cosmic law — was a second draft.

The Nox had preserved the first.

If it was not true, then a banished civilization's homesick reconstruction of a sky they could no longer see happened, by coincidence, to describe an internally consistent alternative cosmology that differed from the Golden Order's in exactly the ways you would expect if the Golden Order had deliberately rearranged the heavens. This was possible. It was the kind of thing that could happen. Caelwin did not believe it.

He opened the codex to the last page of Eseld's annotations.

"I have compared the Inherited Catalogue to every surviving surface observation made before the Second Administration. The agreement is within measurement error. I have compared Leshaia's catalogue of the false sky to the Inherited Catalogue. The agreement is within three arc-minutes. I have compared Leshaia's catalogue to four independent surface observations made after the banishment, obtained at great cost and personal risk by agents whose names I will not record. The disagreement is systematic. It affects one hundred and forty-seven of seven hundred and twelve catalogued stars. Of these, thirty-one have been displaced by more than ten degrees. Nineteen are not present in the post-banishment sky at any position. Seven stars present in the post-banishment sky are absent from both the Inherited Catalogue and Leshaia's catalogue. They are new. They were placed after we were buried. I do not know what purpose they serve. I know they are not old. I know the sky we built is older than the sky that replaced it. I know this because I have the numbers and the numbers do not lie and I am tired of the numbers and I wish they said something else."

Caelwin read this and did not move for a long time.

He considered, methodically, the objections a reasonable person would raise. The Inherited Catalogue could be fabricated. The Nox had motive to construct a false record that made the Greater Will appear to have tampered with the sky. An exiled civilization claiming that their oppressor had rewritten the heavens is not evidence. It is grievance wearing the costume of scholarship.

But the specificity defeated him. A fabrication would show systematic displacement — every star moved, or every star moved in the same direction, or every prominent constellation altered. Eseld's data was messier than that. Five hundred and sixty-five of seven hundred and twelve stars were in the same position. The disagreements were scattered. Some affected bright stars. Some affected dim ones. Some constellations were untouched. Others had one star displaced and four unchanged. The pattern, if it was a pattern, was not the pattern a forger would choose. A forger would make an argument. The data just sat there and refused to be tidy.

He was a Golden Order functionary. He believed in the Order. He believed that the Erdtree was the axis of creation, that the Elden Ring encoded the laws of reality, that the Greater Will's design was the structure of the cosmos made manifest. He believed these things because they were supported by evidence — by the visible operation of grace, by the mechanics of incantation, by the Erdtree's measurable effect on the cycle of death and rebirth.

He had not previously considered the possibility that some of the evidence had been installed.

The Nox sky was a construction. Everyone knew that. The Golden Order's position was that the Nox, in their exile, had built a crude facsimile of the true sky, and that its inaccuracies reflected the limitations of their engineering and the degradation of their memory. This was the standard interpretation. It was what Caelwin had been trained to believe.

It was elegant and dismissive and it had never been tested against the actual data, because no one in the Golden Order had ever bothered to compare the false sky's positions to the real sky's positions star by star. Why would you. The false sky was heretical. Heretical objects are catalogued, stored, and forgotten. They are not studied. Studying them would imply they might contain something worth learning.

Eseld's numbers proposed a different reading. The false sky was not a degraded copy. It was an accurate record of an earlier state. The current sky was the revision.

The Greater Will had not merely banished the Nox. It had then rearranged the heavens, and the rearrangement had been performed after the only witnesses who might have noticed were already underground.

The restricted archive was very quiet. The lamp on the wall flickered. Somewhere above him, Caelwin knew, the Erdtree was glowing, and the faithful were going about their day, and the sky was performing its nightly argument for the perfection of divine order, and the argument was strong, and the argument was beautiful, and the argument had been staged.

He thought about Goldmask. He thought about the treatise on stellar fixity. He thought about the five stars of the Elden Arc and the seventeen treatises that used their positions as evidence for the Greater Will's cosmic geometry, and he thought about those five stars not existing before the banishment, and he thought about what it means to build a theology on evidence that your god manufactured for the purpose.

He put the codex back in the crate. He put the crate back on the wrong shelf. He filed no report. He told no one.

He would not tell anyone. He understood this about himself with a clarity that felt permanent. He was not a heretic. He was not brave. He was a man who had opened a book he should not have opened and found something inside it that he could not give back, and the only power he had over it was silence.

In the intake ledger, Item 1167 remained as it had been logged two hundred and eleven years earlier: "Astronomical charts, provenance unknown, written in Old Nox with marginal annotations in a second hand."

The sky that night was clear. Caelwin stood in the courtyard of the Erdtree Sanctuary and looked up at the stars and tried to see them as he had seen them yesterday, when they were facts.

He found the Elden Arc. Five stars, bright and unambiguous, arranged in the precise curve that every novice learned to identify in their first week of training. The visible signature of divine order. The proof that the cosmos itself was shaped by the Ring.

Five stars that had not existed when the Nox were on the surface.

He looked at them for a long time. They had not moved. They would not move. They were where they had been placed, and they would stay where they had been placed, and the placement was perfect, and the perfection was the problem, because before yesterday he had believed the perfection was evidence of truth, and now he could not stop considering the possibility that it was evidence of craft. A craftsman places things precisely. A fact simply is where it is.

The stars burned. They did not explain themselves.

Caelwin went inside and did not look up again for a very long time.
