Iji told me not to come.
He said it politely, which for a War Counselor of his stature meant he phrased the refusal as a scheduling problem. "The Lady is indisposed." "The Manor is undergoing renovations." "Perhaps next season, when the stars are better aligned." The excuses were elaborate and competent and each one was a door closed in a language I was expected to understand without being told.
I understood. I came anyway.
Ranni's story had been circulating for six months before I heard it from someone who wasn't repeating it third-hand. Six months of rumor and contradictory accounts and the specific kind of silence that institutions produce when someone important does something unforgivable and the institution hasn't decided yet whether to call it treason or prophecy. The Carian royals said nothing. The Academy said nothing. The Two Fingers — well. The Two Fingers had said quite a lot, actually, but none of it in words, and the Finger Readers who interpreted their spasms offered six incompatible translations of the divine response, which told me more about the Finger Readers than about the response.
What I knew: Ranni had killed her body. Not died. Killed it. An Empyrean — one of the three or four individuals selected by the Greater Will to potentially replace Marika — had looked at the trajectory of her divine appointment and said no. Not passively. Not through withdrawal or abdication or the kind of gentle noncompliance that lets everyone pretend the system is still working. She had found Destined Death, the one force that the Golden Order's entire theological architecture was designed to contain, and she had used it on herself.
She survived. Her body didn't. She was now — and the reports diverged wildly on this point — inhabiting a doll. A puppet. A constructed form that walked and spoke and carried the essence of a demigod inside a frame that the Greater Will could not track, because the Greater Will tracks flesh and Ranni's flesh was dead.
I am not a theologian. I am a linguist. But any linguist who works in the Lands Between long enough learns that theology here is grammar — the rules that determine what can be said, what must be said, and what cannot be said without altering the structure of reality itself. The Golden Order is, at its foundation, a syntax. Subject-verb-object. The Greater Will speaks. The Fingers interpret. The world obeys. Marika is the subject. The Elden Ring is the verb. Everything else is the object.
Ranni had refused to be the object.
I reached Ranni's Rise on the third day. The tower was — I don't know the architectural term. Uncanny. It looked like a Carian tower, the blue stone and astral geometry that the royal family had been building for centuries. But it felt occupied by something that did not share the tower's assumptions about what a building was for. Doors opened onto rooms that were not rooms. Stairs descended to heights. A balcony on the second floor looked out onto a sky that was not the sky above the tower but a different sky entirely, one with different stars in a different configuration, arranged in patterns I did not recognize.
Blaidd met me at the entrance. He was enormous. Not in the way that a tall man is enormous — in the way that a wolf standing upright is enormous, all mass and potential and the particular physical authority of a predator who is currently not hunting. His eyes tracked me the way a hunter tracks something he hasn't decided to kill.
"The linguist," he said.
"Yes."
"Iji said you'd come anyway."
"Iji knows me."
Blaidd's lip pulled back. Not a snarl. A wolf's version of a smile, which involves teeth and is therefore difficult to distinguish from a threat unless you know what you're looking at. "She'll see you. She's curious. She's always curious about people who ignore warnings."
He led me up the stairs. The stairs were wrong — seventeen steps covering a vertical distance that should have required forty. I counted. I always count.
Ranni was sitting in a chair that was too large for her body. The doll was beautiful. Porcelain-pale, four arms, dressed in blue, with a face that was a sculptor's idea of serenity — too perfect to be a face and too expressive to be a mask. She was smaller than I expected. Empyreans are supposed to be imposing. Marika is imposing. The Gloam-Eyed Queen was, by all accounts, terrifying. Ranni looked like someone had carved the concept of a princess out of wood and then forgotten to make her the right size.
Her voice was not wood. Her voice was the thing in the room that was real.
"You study how people speak," she said.
"I study what speaking costs."
She tilted her head. Two of her four hands folded. The other two rested on the arms of the chair. "An unusual emphasis."
"Every language has a price. The Golden Order's language costs you your autonomy — you speak in its grammar and you accept its subject-verb-object structure, which means you accept that there is a subject who acts and objects who are acted upon. Carian magic costs you your body — the moon's grammar is older and stranger and it demands that you learn to think in ways that erode the boundary between your mind and the sky. The Giants' language costs you your warmth. Every grammar takes something from the speaker in exchange for the power to mean something."
Ranni's porcelain face did not move. It couldn't. But her voice shifted — not in pitch but in weight, the way a sentence lands differently when the speaker stops being polite. "And what does my language cost?"
"I don't know yet. That's why I'm here."
We spoke for four hours. Blaidd stayed. Iji arrived during the second hour, ducking through the door that was not sized for a troll, settling against the wall with the practiced patience of someone who has listened to Ranni hold court many times before and knows that interrupting is neither welcome nor useful.
The conversation was not an interview. It was a negotiation. Ranni answered questions the way a general reveals troop positions — selectively, strategically, and only when the information served her purposes. She wanted something from me. I could feel it. The shape of her want was still unclear but the fact of it was everywhere in the room.
She told me about the night of the Black Knives.
Not the military operation. Everyone knows the military operation — the assassins, the Rune of Death, the fragment stolen from Maliketh, the synchronized strike. She told me about the grammar of it. How she had to learn a new language to accomplish what she did. Not a spoken language. A theological one.
"The Greater Will," she said, "communicates through structure. Not words. The Elden Ring is a sentence in its language. Each rune is a clause. The subject of the sentence is Marika. The verb is 'order.' The object is everything. To change the sentence, you don't argue with it. You don't refuse to parse it. You learn its grammar and then you write a sentence it cannot contain."
"You rewrote yourself."
"I deleted myself." Two of her hands moved — a gesture I hadn't seen before, the lower pair opening while the upper pair closed, a motion that suggested simultaneous holding and releasing. "The Greater Will's grammar has no tense for a subject that voluntarily becomes absent. An Empyrean who dies is a tragedy the system can process — death is built into the grammar, the Erdtree recycles it, the sentence continues. An Empyrean who kills her own body and persists is a grammatical error. The sentence cannot parse me. I am outside its syntax."
"You're a loanword," I said.
Ranni went still. The doll's face was incapable of surprise, but the four hands went motionless in a way that was, unmistakably, surprise.
"A loanword," she repeated.
"A word borrowed from another language that doesn't follow the host language's rules. It sits inside the sentence but it conjugates differently. It declines differently. The native grammar routes around it because it can't absorb it. That's you. You're a loanword in the Greater Will's syntax. Still present. Still meaningful. Unassimilable."
Blaidd made a sound. Low, involuntary. The wolf-equivalent of a laugh.
Iji, against the wall, said nothing. His silence was load-bearing. I could feel him calculating whether this metaphor served or threatened his Lady's interests.
Ranni's upper hands opened. The lower hands remained closed. "I have been called a traitor, a heretic, a parricide, and an abomination. Nobody has called me a loanword." She paused. "I find it the most accurate."
We talked about the cost.
But first she wanted to talk about the Black Knives. Not the assassins themselves — their operational details were well-documented and, she said, boring. She wanted to talk about the preparation. The years of it. Learning to think in a grammar that had no native speakers.
"The Rune of Death was not a weapon," she said. "It was a conjugation. Maliketh's blade contained a grammatical operation — the ability to make the verb 'to end' transitive in a language where 'to end' had been locked as intransitive for the duration of the Golden Order. Marika sealed it because a world where things can truly end is a world where her sentence can be interrupted. I needed that verb. Not to use on others. To use on myself."
"How long did it take you to learn?"
"The verb? Three years. The courage to conjugate it? Longer."
Blaidd shifted against the wall. Not the predator's alertness this time. Something else. Something that looked, in the body of a half-wolf standing in a demigod's tower, uncomfortably like grief.
I asked her what she had lost. She said: everything that the Greater Will's grammar gave her. The ability to be tracked by grace. The comfort of knowing that her suffering had a place in a divine plan. The warmth of flesh. The assurance that when she died — truly died, finally died — the Erdtree would receive her and she would continue.
"Do you believe the Erdtree would have received you?"
"Yes."
"You gave up eternal recurrence."
"I gave up being a clause in someone else's sentence." Her voice was very level. The doll-face was serene. The hands — all four — were still. "Eternal recurrence in a grammar you did not choose is not immortality. It is a very long prison term."
I wrote that down. I am still writing it down.
Iji spoke for the first time. His voice was deep enough to feel in the stone floor. "You are recording her words."
"Yes."
"For whom?"
"For anyone who wants to know what it sounds like when an Empyrean invents a new language."
"It sounds like treason," Iji said. Mildly. As though treason were an observation about the weather. "To the Golden Order, to the Two Fingers, to every institution that requires the grammar to remain stable — what my Lady has done is not a linguistic innovation. It is an attack on the structure of meaning itself."
"Is it?"
"You tell me. You are the linguist."
I looked at Ranni. She was watching me with the patience of someone who has asked this question of herself many times and is curious to hear someone else's answer.
"Every new language is an attack on the old one," I said. "Every pidgin. Every creole. Every time a conquered people take their conquerors' words and conjugate them wrong on purpose — that's an act of war. The grammar fights back. The old syntax tries to absorb the new one, regularize it, file down its edges until it fits. Sometimes it succeeds. Sometimes the loanword wins. Sometimes the loanword changes the grammar so fundamentally that the host language becomes something new."
"And which am I?"
"I don't know. Nobody does yet. You might be absorbed. You might change everything. The system is still trying to parse you."
"Let it try," Ranni said.
Blaidd's wolf-grin widened.
There was one more thing before I left. Ranni asked me to listen to Seluvis.
Seluvis was a sorcerer who served Ranni and despised the arrangement. He was, by any measure, a terrible person — a puppeteer who trapped souls in constructed bodies, a man whose relationship to consent was, at best, theoretical. But his speech patterns were remarkable. He spoke about Ranni in the third person even when she was in the room. "The Lady requires." "The Lady demands." Never "Ranni." Never the first name. He used the formal register that Carian courtiers use for the royal family, which is a register that grammatically encodes distance — the subject is always spoken of as though observed from far away, which is a linguistic mechanism for managing the cognitive dissonance of serving someone whose power exceeds your comprehension.
But Seluvis used it wrong. He used the formal register with contempt markers from the informal register — diminutives, conditional tenses where indicative was appropriate, subjunctive moods that implied doubt about the subject's agency. He was using the grammar of deference to communicate the opposite of deference. It was, technically, brilliant. It was also the speech pattern of a man who had followed a loanword into a new grammar and discovered that the new grammar gave him no vocabulary for what he actually wanted, which was the old grammar's certainties with the new grammar's freedoms.
Ranni knew. She let him do it.
"He is useful," she said, when I raised it. "And he is honest. His grammar says what his mouth won't."
I thought about that. A leader who reads her followers' loyalty in their conjugation patterns. A leader for whom syntax is surveillance. That was the cost I hadn't identified yet — the cost not to Ranni but to the people around her. To follow a loanword, you have to leave the host grammar. Blaidd left it willingly. Iji left it knowingly. Seluvis left it and brought the old grammar with him like a suitcase he refused to unpack, and the suitcase was rotting, and everyone could smell it, and nobody said anything because the new grammar didn't have a polite way to say "you still think in the old language and it's killing you."
I left the tower at dusk. The stairs, on the way down, had twenty-three steps. I counted. The number had changed. The tower was adjusting to something — to Ranni's presence, or to mine, or to the conversation that had happened inside it, which had introduced new terms into the local vocabulary and the architecture was metabolizing them.
Iji walked me to the treeline. The troll's footsteps made no sound on the moss, which was impressive given that he weighed as much as a small building.
"You will publish this," he said. Not a question.
"Not yet."
"When?"
"When I understand what it costs."
He nodded. Trolls nod the way mountains settle — a barely perceptible shift that implies vast structural rearrangement beneath the surface. "She frightens me," he said. "Not because of what she has done. Because of what she has become. A grammar that cannot be parsed is either a new language or noise. If she is a new language, the world changes. If she is noise, the world goes on and she is forgotten. Both possibilities are terrible. I serve her because the first possibility is worth the second."
I thought about that for a long time after he disappeared into the trees. A man who serves a grammar he cannot parse because the alternative — a grammar he can — is worse. That is either the most sophisticated political philosophy I have ever encountered or the most honest description of faith.
I left Liurnia with six pages of notes and a phonemic transcription of the way Ranni said the word "choice." The word, in standard Carian, has two syllables and a rising intonation. In Ranni's pronunciation it had three syllables — she inserted a glottal stop between the first and second that turned a single word into a compound: the-act-of-choosing. As though the word "choice" in its standard form was too compressed. As though what she had done required a bigger container.
The smallest changes are the loudest. A single phoneme, inserted where no phoneme belongs, that cracks a word open and lets the meaning spill out.
I have nine languages. Ranni has one. It is the language in which "no" is a complete sentence, and the subject and the verb and the object are all the same word, and the grammar has no room for anyone else's syntax.
It might be the most dangerous language in the Lands Between.
It might be the only honest one.
