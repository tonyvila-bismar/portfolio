The Naming Of The Omen

by Sidonei ne Sangbelles

The Naming of the Omen

Look, I know what you're going to say. You're going to say, "Sidonei, the Golden Order was a theocratic regime that committed atrocities against its own population, and you're not telling anyone anything they don't already know." And you're right. You're correct. Everybody knows the Omen were persecuted. Everybody has heard about the sewers. Everybody has seen the pictures of the shackles, the little iron cages sized for children, the prayer-wheels mounted on the cell walls so the guards could technically claim the imprisonment was a form of worship. Everybody knows. Gold star. You passed the exam.

Now tell me what an Omen is.

Not what they looked like. Not how they were treated. What they are. Give me the definition. The actual taxonomic definition as it appears in the Golden Order's own literature, the Fundaments of Sacred Morphology, which is a real document that real theologians spent real decades composing, and which states — I am not making this up — that an Omen is "a humanoid subject exhibiting one or more persistent cruciate aspects indicating incomplete divine reversion."

Take that apart. Every word in that sentence is doing a remarkable amount of work.

"Humanoid subject." Not person. Not citizen. Not child of the Erdtree. Subject. You're a specimen before the sentence is half-finished. The word "humanoid" is particularly gorgeous — it means shaped like a human but not quite one. You're human-adjacent. You're in the neighborhood. You are doing a very convincing impression of someone who counts.

"Exhibiting." Not born with. Not possessing. Exhibiting, as in displaying symptoms, as in your horns are a medical presentation rather than a fact about your body. This is the quiet genius of the whole construction. You don't have horns the way you have arms. You are exhibiting horns the way a patient exhibits a rash. The horns are an event happening to you. An event that, with the proper intervention, could presumably be made to stop happening.

"Persistent cruciate aspects." Here is where the theology earns its keep. Cruciate — of or relating to the Crucible, the primordial state of the Erdtree when life was undifferentiated. The Crucible was sacred. The Crucible was the origin of all life. The Crucible was, and I cannot stress this enough, the thing they built the entire religion around. But when the same traits that defined the Crucible appear in a living person, they are "aspects" — partial, vestigial, leftover. Not the thing itself. A residue. A stain from an earlier draft that should have been erased. The sacred becomes the symptomatic in nine syllables.

"Indicating incomplete divine reversion." This is the kill shot. Reversion means a return to a prior state. Divine reversion means the Erdtree's maturation from Crucible to Golden Order. And "incomplete" means your body failed to finish the process. Everyone else reverted properly — cleaned up, smoothed out, became the golden-skinned golden-souled golden-everything subjects of the new regime. You didn't. Something in you got stuck. Something in you is still the old thing, the Before thing, and the Before thing is no longer sacred.

So an Omen is not a person who was born wrong. An Omen is a person whose body is evidence that the Golden Order's central claim — that the Erdtree's maturation was total and complete and affected all life equally — is a lie. They don't hate you because you're ugly. They hate you because you're a counterexample.

Congratulations on your diagnosis. Here is your cell.

I realize I should declare an interest. I have no experience with horns, golden orders, or imprisonment beneath capital cities. I have extensive experience with being reclassified. With waking up one morning as one thing and going to bed that evening as something else — not because anything about you changed, but because the institution that had been defining you updated its definitions. The Night Queen did not need to cut anything off me. She didn't need to. She just needed to rewrite what I was in the official record, and the record did the rest.

The mechanism is the same. It is always the same. You take a person. You make a document that describes them in language they didn't choose. You make the document official. Now the person is the document. Now the person is "exhibiting." Now the person is a "subject." Now the person is whatever you need them to be for the system to function, and if the person objects, well, they would, wouldn't they — that's a symptom too.

But you came here for procedure, not feelings, and Sidonei ne Sangbelles does not waste your time with feelings when there is procedure to eviscerate.

The Golden Order's Omen Containment Protocol — and yes, they really did call it containment, as though horns were contagious — operated in six documented stages. I want to walk through all six, because the specifics are where the comedy lives, and by comedy I mean the kind that makes you want to set something on fire.

Stage One: Identification. A birth attendant — always a member of the Erdtree clergy, because births in Leyndell required clerical witness, because of course they did — inspects the newborn for "cruciate anomalies." There is a checklist. Horns, obviously. Tail buds. Subcutaneous scale formation. Asymmetric cranial protrusions. The checklist has fourteen items. Fourteen things a baby can be wrong about before it has taken its first breath.

Stage Two: Documentation. The attendant files a Morphological Deviation Report with the Office of Sacred Compliance. The report includes measurements — horn length, horn curvature, estimated growth trajectory. A sketch, if the attendant can draw. Most could not. The surviving sketches are perfunctory and ugly, which is somehow worse than if they had been detailed. They couldn't even be bothered to look carefully at what they were condemning.

Stage Three: Parental Notification. This is the one that gets me. The parents are informed that their child has been classified as an Omen. They are given a document — a different document, not the Morphological Deviation Report, a separate, gentler document with softer language and a border of golden filigree — explaining that their child will be "remanded to the Subterranean Chapel of Remedial Grace" for "spiritual correction aligned with Erdtree doctrine." They are told it is for the child's benefit. They are told the child will receive care. They are told.

The parents are not present for the transfer.

That sentence exists in the protocol. Stage Three, subsection four. It is not an omission. It is an instruction. "The parents are not present for the transfer." Someone wrote that down. Someone decided that the moment a baby is taken from its family should be documented as a logistical requirement and not as — I don't know — the single worst thing that can happen to a person before they can speak.

Stage Four: Transfer. The child is moved to the Subterranean Chapel. "Chapel" is doing heroic work in this sentence. The Subterranean Chapel of Remedial Grace is the sewer system beneath the Royal Capital. I want to be precise about this. It is not a metaphorical sewer. It is not a disused tunnel system that happens to be underground. It is the sewage infrastructure of Leyndell, repurposed as a containment facility for children born with horns. The prayer-wheels I mentioned earlier were installed after the first generation of inmates, which means someone went down there, saw children living in a sewer, and concluded that the problem was insufficient liturgical furniture.

Stage Five: Remediation. This is the horn removal. "Remediation" is the official term. The horns are sawed off at the skull using consecrated instruments — the consecration is important, because it reclassifies mutilation as sacrament — while the subject is conscious. Conscious. No anesthesia. The theological justification is that the pain constitutes "purgative suffering aligned with the Erdtree's cleansing function." The child must feel it. That is the point. The pain is the medicine.

Survival rates for the procedure: ninety-two percent for single-horn presentations, dropping to eighty-four percent for bilateral, and I will not look up the number for the children with tails because there is a limit to what I will make myself read in one sitting and I hit it about three paragraphs ago but here I still am, still talking, because if I stop talking I will have to sit with what I just wrote and I do not want to do that.

Stage Six: Reclassification. The surviving children are redesignated as "Remediated Omen, Subclass Provisional." They may be released into the general population if their scarring can be concealed by a standard coif or helm. If not, they remain in the Chapel. The word "provisional" never expires. There is no Stage Seven. You are provisionally human for the rest of your life.

Six stages. Birth to provisional humanity in six administrative steps.

The genius of it — and I use that word the way you use it for a disease that has figured out how to survive every treatment — is that the system never touches the word "person." It never has to. The Fundaments of Sacred Morphology does not contain the sentence "Omen are not people." That would be crude. That would invite argument. Instead it provides a vocabulary in which personhood is simply not one of the available categories.

You are a subject, an aspect, a deviation, a presentation. You are a morphological event. You are an incomplete reversion. You are fourteen items on a checklist and a sketch drawn by someone who couldn't be bothered to look at your face. Somewhere in that blizzard of terminology, "person" disappeared, and nobody had to make the argument because the argument was never had.

Every system that decides some of its people are contaminated builds the same machine. I know. I've been inside it. Different continent, different theology, different things growing out of people's bodies, same six stages with the serial numbers filed off. Stage One, someone inspects you. Stage Two, someone writes it down. Stage Three, someone tells your family it's for your own good. Stage Four, you are moved. Stage Five, something is removed. Stage Six, you are provisionally allowed to exist as long as you conceal the evidence.

I went through all six. The Night Queen's version used different words — "assessment," "notation," "counseling," "relocation," "correction," "conditional pardon." Nicer words. More syllables. Same machine.

And here is what I want to talk about, what I actually came here to talk about, before I got distracted by the pleasure of dismantling a taxonomy: Morgott.

The one who loved the machine that was built to grind him down.

Morgott, the Omen King. Born with horns. Imprisoned beneath his own capital. Put through all six stages. And then — and this is the part, this is the part that destroys me — he came back up and defended the city that put him in the sewer. He loved the Erdtree. He loved the Golden Order. He fought for it, bled for it, went out in disguise as Margit the Fell Omen and blocked the gate at Stormveil and took blow after blow to protect a kingdom that would have shoved him back underground if they'd seen what he was underneath the cloak.

"Though born one of the graceless Omen, he loved not in return, for he was never loved, but nevertheless, love it he did."

I want to make fun of him for this. I want to say he was a fool. I want to say he internalized the taxonomy and became its perfect product — the remediated subject who loves his remediation, the provisional human so grateful for the provision that he lays down his life for the provisioner. I want to say that, and I can't, because I know what it is to love the thing that named you wrong. I know what it is to defend the Tower that threw you out. I know what it is to stand in the rain saying "I am still yours" to a door that closed twenty years ago and has not opened since.

I was not going to say that. That was not supposed to be in this essay. The essay was supposed to be about procedure. Six stages. Administrative taxonomy. The comedy of bureaucratized cruelty. That was the bit. That was the angle.

But you can't write about Morgott and stay in the bit, because Morgott is the bit's failure case. He is what happens when the machine works perfectly and the person inside it refuses to break the way the machine requires. They took his name. They gave him a category. They filed his deviation report and sawed off his horns and shoved him in a sewer and called him provisionally human, and he came back up and chose devotion anyway. Not because the system was right. Because the devotion was his, and they couldn't file it away.

His twin chose differently. Mohg looked at the same six stages and said: fine, if I am what you say I am, then I will be it all the way down. I will take the blood you call cursed and I will build a palace with it. I will find the most precious thing in your world and I will carry it underground and I will love it the way you said I could not. That is also a response to the taxonomy. That is also coherent.

But Morgott's response is the one I can't stop thinking about. Because it is not defiance. It is not acceptance. It is a third thing that the taxonomy has no word for, which is: I know what you called me, and I know what I am, and those are not the same thing, and I will spend my life proving the difference even though you will never acknowledge it.

His brother built a dynasty. His brother made a statement. Morgott just stood in the rain and held the gate, and when they found his body they found it facing the Erdtree, and the horns they couldn't saw off were reaching toward each other above his skull like two hands trying to clasp and falling four inches short.

I read that. I read that and I thought: that is the open-crown formation. That is devotion that reaches and reaches and never arrives. And I thought: every exile I have ever known has that gap somewhere. The place where your love for the thing that rejected you almost meets itself and doesn't. The four inches of air between your outstretched hands and the door. You can't close it. You can't stop reaching. You live in the gap.

The Erdtree didn't deserve him. The Golden Order didn't deserve him. Nothing about the system that classified him deserved a single second of his devotion, and he gave it all of them, every second, until the last one, and the last thing he saw was the tree.

You want to know the difference between Morgott and me? He never stopped loving the thing that named him wrong. I stopped. I stopped twenty years ago. I stopped and I walked away and I built my jerry-rigged life out of cave mushrooms and stolen reagents and furious competence, and I tell myself every day that stopping was the right choice, that spite is fuel enough, that I do not miss the canopy.

I miss the canopy.

The metaphor got a little away from me there. Let's get back to procedure.

Actually, no. No, let's not get back to procedure. The procedure is documented. You can read it yourself. Six stages, fourteen checklist items, no anesthesia, provisional humanity. It's all there. The Fundaments of Sacred Morphology is thorough, I will give it that. Thorough the way a trap is thorough. Every edge accounted for.

What is not in the document is Morgott standing in the rain. What is not in the document is Mohg's palace of blood. What is not in the document is a baby's horns growing back after the remediation, because they do — did you know that? — they grow back, sometimes, not always, but sometimes the body remembers what it was before the consecrated saw, and the whole thing has to be done again. Stage Five, repeated. "Iterative remediation." A child, awake, for the second time.

What is not in the document is what it feels like to be fourteen items on a checklist. To have someone measure the curvature of the thing growing out of your skull and write a number in a box and close the file and move on to the next baby. To be born and weighed and sketched and categorized and transferred and cut and redesignated and released, provisionally, into a world that has decided you are an incomplete reversion of a primordial state that everyone else successfully moved on from, and that the best you can hope for is a coif that covers the scars.

The Crucible was sacred. The horns were sacred. The tail was sacred. The wings were sacred.

Every aspect was a gift from the primordial tree, the original undifferentiated life-force that made the world. And then the Erdtree grew up — matured, they called it, as though genocide were puberty — and everything the Crucible had been became something the Golden Order had to eradicate, and the people whose bodies still carried the old gifts became evidence of an embarrassing past, and the evidence was managed the way all evidence is managed: it was buried, and cut, and renamed until it stopped reminding anyone of what was lost.

They had words for everything the Crucible was. An old woman I spoke to — a cataloguer, not a believer, just someone who had studied the liturgical vocabularies — told me there were once eleven distinct terms for the different types of scale transition alone. Eleven words for the way human skin becomes something harder, brighter, more alive. The Golden Order replaced all eleven with "dermal anomaly, partial." Three words. Three flat, dead, diagnostic words to replace eleven that sang.

That's what a taxonomy does. It replaces eleven words with three. It replaces a name with a number. It replaces a person with a subject. And then it files the subject in a sewer and calls it grace.

I should end this. I've been talking too long. I always talk too long. That's what happens when you've had nobody to talk to for twenty years — you corner someone and you unspool and by the time you realize what you're doing they've got that look on their face, the one that says "I came here for an essay about bureaucracy and this man is telling me about his mother."

I'm not telling you about my mother. I'm telling you about a taxonomy. A clean, efficient, six-stage taxonomy that turned sacred children into administrative problems and solved the problems by putting them underground and sawing pieces off them, and the reason I am telling you this at such length and such volume is not because I think you don't know — you know, everybody knows, gold star, you passed the exam — it is because knowing is not the same as feeling the fourteen items on your own skin, and if I talk fast enough and specific enough and vicious enough, maybe for one sentence you will feel it, and that sentence will be the one that matters.

Morgott stood in the rain. The horns reached toward each other and did not touch. He loved the thing that named him wrong, and the gap between his hands and the door was four inches, and he died in the gap, and I am alive in mine.
