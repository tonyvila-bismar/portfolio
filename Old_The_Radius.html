Old The Radius

by Pellegrin

The Radius

Aspect manifestation follows a gradient.

This is not theology. It is observation. The temple records, which go back eleven generations at the Thornwall observatory, show a consistent pattern: individuals living within forty leagues of a mature Erdtree sapling manifest aspects at a rate of roughly one in nine. At sixty leagues, the rate drops to one in twenty. At eighty leagues, it drops to one in fifty. Beyond a hundred leagues, manifestation is so rare that most settlements do not have a word for it.

The gradient is not uniform. It follows the root network. A village forty leagues from the nearest sapling but directly above a deep root system will have manifestation rates equivalent to a village at twenty leagues. A village at the same distance but on bedrock with no root penetration will have rates equivalent to eighty leagues or more. The field — if it can be called a field, and I believe it can, though the temple elders dislike the word because it implies something measurable rather than something holy — propagates through the root structure of the Erdtree and its predecessors the way warmth propagates through stone.

I have spent eleven years measuring this.

My name is Sera. I am a naturalist attached to the Temple of the Unformed at Thornwall, which means I hold a position that the temple considers necessary and the garrison considers eccentric and neither considers important, which is, in my experience, the ideal condition for doing actual work.

The gradient was not my discovery. The temple has known about it, in broad terms, for centuries. Aspect manifestation clusters near the great trees. This is not controversial. What is controversial — what has made my eleven years of fieldwork a source of persistent institutional discomfort — is the corollary.

If manifestation follows a gradient, then the gradient has an edge.

And at the edge, manifestation does something the theology does not predict.

The standard account, which every temple novice learns and every temple elder repeats, is that the Crucible is the primordial state of all life. Before differentiation, before species, before the categories that the Litany so carefully enumerates, there was the mingling — the great unity from which all forms emerged. Aspect manifestation is the Crucible remembering itself through living flesh. A horn, a tail, a wing, a thickening of bone — these are not mutations. They are recollections. The body remembering what it was before it became what it is.

This account is beautiful. It may even be true.

But it does not explain what happens at the edge of the gradient, where the field is too weak for full manifestation but too strong for none.

At the edge, you get partial aspects.

I need to be precise about what I mean by partial, because the temple uses the word to mean "incomplete" — an aspect that has not yet finished developing — and I use it to mean something different. I mean an aspect that has developed fully, that has reached its terminal form, and that terminal form is a fraction of what the same aspect would be at higher field strength.

Consider horns. Within twenty leagues of a mature sapling, cranial aspects present as dense, structurally complete formations — the logarithmic spirals that the Litany classifies as Types I through IV, load-bearing, capable of sustaining impact forces equivalent to a cavalry charge. The bone is layered, the growth rings are regular, the surface texture is consistent.

At sixty leagues, cranial aspects present as shorter, thinner formations. Still structurally sound. Still classifiable. But reduced. The spiral is tighter. The bone is less dense. The growth rings are compressed.

At eighty leagues, cranial aspects present as nubs. Rounded protrusions of dense bone, rarely more than two inches long, often asymmetric. The Litany classifies these as Type V — "emergent" — which is a polite way of saying they do not fit the existing categories.

At ninety leagues, I have documented something the Litany does not classify at all. Individuals who report a sensation of pressure at the temples — a persistent, low-grade ache, as though something is trying to emerge but cannot. They have no visible aspect. Their skulls, when examined, show no structural anomaly. But the sensation is consistent across multiple subjects, it does not respond to treatment, and it correlates with proximity to root networks in exactly the way that full manifestation does at higher field strength.

I call these phantom aspects. The temple does not call them anything, because acknowledging them would require acknowledging that the Crucible's sacred mingling has a threshold below which it produces not a horn but the memory of a horn, and the theological implications of that are —

Well. The theological implications are why I work alone.

The phantom aspects are the reason I requested permission to establish a permanent observation station at the village of Harren's Well, which sits at approximately ninety-two leagues from the nearest sapling and directly above a root network that my soil samples suggest is degrading. Degrading is another word the temple dislikes. Roots do not degrade. They are eternal, like the tree, like the Crucible itself. Except that they do degrade, measurably, and I can show this through mineral analysis of the surrounding soil, and I have shown it, in three separate reports that the temple has acknowledged receiving and has not responded to.

Harren's Well has a population of sixty-three. Of those sixty-three, eleven report the pressure sensation. None have visible aspects. None have family histories of manifestation. They are, by every standard the temple uses, unaspected.

But they are not. They are at the edge of the gradient, where the field is too weak to produce form but strong enough to produce the impulse toward form. They carry the Crucible's intention without its execution. They are, if the standard theological account is correct, people whose bodies are trying to remember something and cannot quite.

I find this remarkable.

I should describe the methodology, because the methodology matters, and because the temple's objections to my work have largely taken the form of objecting to my methods rather than my conclusions, which is easier.

I measure field strength indirectly, through three proxies. The first is soil mineralization — the root network deposits trace elements that can be identified through acid dissolution, and the concentration of these elements correlates with the root's metabolic activity. The second is ambient biological response — certain mosses and lichens grow exclusively in the presence of active roots, and their distribution maps the network's extent with reasonable precision. The third is manifestation rate itself, tabulated across a population over time and corrected for family history, age distribution, and seasonal variation. The temple accepts the third proxy. It considers the first two irrelevant, on the grounds that the Crucible's influence cannot be reduced to mineral deposits and moss.

I disagree. But I am a naturalist, and disagreement is my native condition.

The eleven years of data have produced what I call the Gradient Map — a representation of field strength across the northern districts, keyed to root network depth and activity. The Map shows exactly what the temple does not want to see: a finite, measurable, geographically bounded phenomenon with clear edges and a center. The Crucible is not everywhere. It is somewhere, and the somewhere has a shape, and the shape is shrinking.

I submitted the Map with my seventh annual report. The temple acknowledged receipt. They did not respond.

I find this remarkable because the standard account says that the Crucible does not choose — that its mingling is universal and undirected, that every aspect is equally sacred because every aspect is equally a fragment of the primordial unity. But the gradient says otherwise. The gradient says the Crucible's reach is finite. It says that sacredness — if sacredness is what we are measuring — diminishes with distance, follows root networks, and can be degraded by geological processes that have nothing to do with theology.

The temple's position is that the gradient is a feature of the physical world, not the sacred one. The Crucible's spiritual reality is uniform. Only its material expression varies. The field measures proximity to roots, not proximity to the divine.

I understand this position. I have considered it carefully.

I do not believe it.

I do not believe it because of what I observed at Harren's Well during the third year of my fieldwork, when a woman named Alde, who was forty-seven years old and had lived in the village her entire life and had never manifested any aspect and had reported the pressure sensation since the age of twelve, woke one morning to find that the pressure had stopped.

She came to me because I was the only person within a hundred leagues who would understand why this was significant. She sat in my observation room and told me that the ache she had carried for thirty-five years was gone. Not diminished. Gone. As though a hand that had been pressing against the inside of her skull had simply lifted.

I examined her. I found nothing. No structural change. No new growth. No sign that an aspect had manifested and receded. The phantom was simply absent.

I checked the soil samples from the root network beneath the village. The mineral content had shifted. The degradation I had been tracking for three years had crossed a threshold — the root network beneath Harren's Well was, by every measure I had available, inert. Dead, if roots can die, and I believe they can, though the temple will not use the word.

The field had withdrawn.

And Alde — who had spent thirty-five years carrying the Crucible's intention in her body, whose temples had ached with the ghost of a horn that never came — felt it leave.

She asked me what it meant.

I told her the truth. I told her I did not know. I told her that the gradient was a physical phenomenon and that its withdrawal was measurable and that the cessation of her phantom aspect was consistent with the root network's degradation and that none of this told me whether the Crucible was divine or mechanical or something the existing categories could not contain.

She asked me whether the ache had been sacred.

I told her that I was a naturalist and that questions of sacredness were the temple's domain, not mine. This was, I acknowledge, a cowardice. She knew it. I knew it. We sat with it.

What I did not tell her — what I have told no one — is that I have the same data for every root network in the northern districts. Fourteen networks are currently active. Three are in measurable decline. Two have crossed the threshold for phantom manifestation in the last decade. The pattern is consistent. The degradation proceeds from the periphery inward, the way frost retreats from a windowpane in stages.

The question the data raises is not whether the Crucible is withdrawing from the northern districts. The data answers that. The Crucible is withdrawing. The question is whether the withdrawal is local — a fluctuation, a seasonal shift in a process that operates on timescales I cannot observe — or terminal.

I do not have enough data to answer this. Eleven years is not enough. A hundred years might not be enough. The root networks were laid down over millennia. Their degradation may take millennia more. Or it may accelerate. I have no model for predicting which.

What I have is a woman in Harren's Well who misses an ache that was never a horn.

Here is what I believe, though I have not written it in any report and will not write it in any report, because the temple does not want to know and the garrison does not care and there is no institution on this continent whose purpose is to hear what a naturalist thinks about the sacred.

I believe the gradient is the Crucible. Not a side effect. Not a material expression of an immaterial reality. The gradient itself — the field, the propagation through roots, the diminishment with distance, the threshold below which intention becomes phantom and phantom becomes nothing — is what the Crucible is. A physical process that produces forms in flesh, that diminishes with distance, that can be measured and mapped and, eventually, exhausted.

The theology says the Crucible is eternal. The gradient says the Crucible is a resource. The two cannot both be true.

I do not know which I want to be right.

The observation station at Harren's Well is still operational. I take measurements every month. The root network continues to degrade. Two more of the eleven phantom-aspect residents have reported cessation of the pressure sensation. The gradient is retreating, village by village, league by league, as the root network beneath this part of the continent slowly fails.

I document this. That is my function. I measure the field. I record the numbers. I note the dates.

The Crucible is withdrawing from Harren's Well the way a tide withdraws from a shore. Slowly, and without malice, and with the absolute indifference of a physical process that does not know it is holy.

Alde still lives in the village. She is forty-nine now. She tells me she sometimes misses the ache.

I understand this. I think about it often. The phantom aspect was never a horn. It was never going to become a horn. It was the body's record of a field that was passing through, leaving a trace too faint to produce form but strong enough to be felt. And the feeling was — she has said this to me, on more than one occasion, in the careful language of a woman who has thought about it for a long time — the feeling was of being part of something larger than herself.

It was, she said, like being remembered.

The gradient does not remember. The gradient is a measurement. But Alde remembers the gradient, and what the gradient felt like, and I have recorded this too, because I record everything, and because some things that are not measurable are still worth writing down.

The field at Harren's Well is now below the threshold for phantom manifestation. In another decade, based on current degradation rates, it will be below the threshold for detection entirely. The village will be, by every instrument I possess, outside the Crucible's reach.

The horns and the tails and the wings will be somewhere else. The ache will be gone. The people of Harren's Well will live their lives without the pressure at their temples, without the memory of something trying to emerge, without the sense of being part of a process that is older than any temple or litany or classification.

They will be outside the radius.

I have considered, more than once, what Alde would say to this prognosis. I think she would nod. She is a practical woman who has lived her whole life in a village that the temple considers peripheral and the garrison considers irrelevant and I consider the most important observation site in the northern districts. She would understand the numbers. She would ask whether there was anything to be done.

There is not. The gradient is not a policy. It is not a decision. It cannot be reversed by decree or prayer or the construction of a new temple. The roots are degrading because roots degrade. The field is retreating because fields retreat when their source fails.

I would tell her this. She would nod again.

I do not know what that means for them. I do not know what it means for the Crucible. I know only what it means for my measurements, which is that there will be fewer of them, and that eventually there will be none, and that I will close the station and file my final report and the temple will acknowledge receiving it and will not respond.

The gradient is retreating.

I have one more thing to record.

In the fifth year of my fieldwork, I walked the full extent of the gradient's edge across the northern districts. It took seven weeks. I walked from Harren's Well east to the Ashford settlements, then north to the hill villages above the Greenvale, then west along the ridgeline where the root network surfaces briefly before plunging into bedrock.

The edge is not a line. It is a zone — roughly five to ten leagues wide — where the field transitions from detectable to absent. Within this zone, the communities are different from those inside the gradient and different from those outside it. Inside the gradient, aspects are part of life. They are named, classified, celebrated. Outside the gradient, aspects are stories — things that happen to people in other places, reported secondhand, not quite believed.

In the transition zone, aspects are rumors that happen to your cousin.

The people in the transition zone have a relationship to the Crucible that the theology does not account for. They are not aspected. They are not unaspected. They are adjacent. They live close enough to the sacred to feel its pull — the phantom sensations, the pressure, the sense of almost — but too far for it to crystallize into anything the temple would recognize.

They are, in a sense, the Crucible's audience rather than its participants.

I do not know what happens to a faith when its holy phenomena have a measurable radius. I do not know what happens to the people who live just outside that radius, who can see the horns and the tails and the wings on their trading partners and know that the difference between them is not virtue or devotion or the Crucible's favor but geography. I do not know what that knowledge does to a person over the course of a lifetime.

But I suspect it is where the fracture begins. Not inside the gradient, where the faithful are sustained by visible proof. Not outside it, where the faithless have never felt the pull. At the edge. Where you can almost touch it, and it is withdrawing, and no one at the temple will tell you why.

I am still measuring.
