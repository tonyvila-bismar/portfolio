The Cuckoo Garrison

by Eitan

The worst soldiers I've ever seen were the best-equipped.

This is not a paradox, though it sounds like one if you haven't spent enough time around armies. Good equipment comes from good funding. Good funding comes from an institution that values its military. An institution that values its military either controls it or worships it, and the Academy of Raya Lucaria did the second thing, which is the dangerous one.

The Cuckoo Knights garrisoned Raya Lucaria. I use "garrisoned" loosely — they occupied the grounds, the approaches, the bridges, the lakeshore positions, and a significant stretch of the surrounding countryside in a pattern that was neither defensive perimeter nor patrol route but something I eventually identified as territorial display. They were in the places where they could be seen. The positions they held had excellent sight lines, which is tactically sound, except the sight lines faced inward, toward the roads, toward the villages, toward the people they were nominally protecting. The sight lines did not face outward, toward threats. There were no threats. The Cuckoos had made sure of that a long time ago by being the threat.

I spent three days observing before I spoke to anyone. (I know. Tamar tells me this is the behavior of a man who treats social interaction as a reconnaissance operation, and I tell him he's right, and he sighs, and I don't change, because changing would mean being the kind of person who walks into an unfamiliar military installation and starts asking questions before he knows which questions will get him killed. I have been that person. It was unpleasant.)

The equipment was superb. Cuckoo armor was standardized — a medium-weight half-plate with a distinctive blue-gray finish that I later learned was achieved through a proprietary tempering process the Academy's artificers had developed and which, in a detail that tells you everything you need to know about the relationship between sorcerers and soldiers, the knights themselves didn't understand. They wore it. They didn't know how it was made. They couldn't maintain it. When a breastplate cracked, they sent it back to the Academy for repair, the way a child returns a broken toy to a parent.

The swords were Academy-issue. The pikes were Academy-issue. The horses were Academy-bred, Academy-fed, Academy-shod.

The soldiers were not Academy-educated. This matters.

An army that depends on its patron for everything — arms, armor, horses, funding, food, quarters, and the proprietary maintenance of its own equipment — is not an army. It is a department. It has the organizational structure of a military unit and the institutional psychology of a purchasing office. It fights when the patron says fight, rests when the patron says rest, and in the intervals it does what all departments do, which is protect its budget and expand its jurisdiction.

The Cuckoos had expanded their jurisdiction to include the entire Liurnian lakeside. Every village within two days' march paid something. Not taxes — the Academy levied taxes through official channels, with receipts, which the sorcerers were meticulous about because sorcerers are meticulous about everything except the consequences of their meticulousness. The Cuckoos collected something else. Call it consideration. Call it the price of proximity to armed men who answer to a school that can't be bothered to supervise them.

McMeister writes in the Second Commentary: "An unsupervised soldier is not a soldier but a man with a weapon and permission." I've quoted this to every officer I've ever trained and none of them have liked hearing it, which is how I know it's accurate.

The permission was the Academy's. The Academy trained sorcerers. The sorcerers studied stars. The stars were the mechanism by which reality maintained its structural integrity, or something along those lines — I confess the cosmological details escape me, and I've never trusted a theology that requires mathematics. The point is that the Academy considered itself essential, and essential institutions require protection, and protection requires soldiers, and soldiers require — here is where the logic gets slippery — soldiers require latitude.

Latitude. That word was used three times in the single administrative document I managed to examine, a supply requisition that had been annotated by someone in the Academy's bursar's office. "The Knights of the Cuckoo are granted latitude in matters of local security." Not authority. Not jurisdiction. Latitude. Which is a word that means "we know what you're doing and we've decided not to define it precisely because precision would require us to either authorize it or forbid it and we'd rather do neither."

(The Codex has a word for this. The word is "complicity." Book Gimmel, Chapter Three: "He who averts his eyes from the sword's work does not sheathe the sword but merely consents to its edge without watching where it falls." I read that passage when I was seventeen and thought it was about war. I read it again at thirty-four and realized it was about institutions.)

I talked to a Cuckoo sergeant on my third evening. His name was Patrus. He'd been garrisoned at Raya Lucaria for eleven years, which is too long for any posting and which explained, without excusing, the quality of his posture, which was bad, and the quality of his local intelligence, which was excellent. He knew every village headman by name. He knew which farms produced what yields. He knew who was sleeping with whom and who owed money to whom and which roads flooded in the wet season and where the best fishing spots were.

He did not know the name of the Academy's current head sorcerer.

This is the thing. Patrus was not a bad man. He was not cruel, not stupid, not drunk. He was a professional who'd been given a job without boundaries and had filled the available space the way water fills a vessel. He collected his considerations and provided his services and the services were real — bandits cleared, roads maintained, disputes arbitrated with the casual authority of a man who carries a sword and whose patron is the most powerful institution in the region. The villages hated him and needed him and hated that they needed him, which is the authentic emotional signature of a population living under a protection arrangement.

I asked Patrus what he'd do if the Academy fell.

He looked at me the way you look at someone who's asked what you'd do if the sun went out.

"The Academy won't fall," he said.

"But if it did."

"Then we'd be eleven years into a posting with no resupply, no maintenance contract for our armor, no farrier for our horses, and no legal authority for anything we've been doing since I got here."

He took a drink.

"So we'd probably keep doing it," he said. "Because what else would we do?"

I should have left it there. That's the clean ending to the conversation — the sergeant's pragmatic shrug, the strategic assessment delivered as resignation. I've had a hundred conversations like this with soldiers in a hundred garrisons and the smart move is always to nod, finish your drink, and write it up later in the privacy of your own quarters where the subject can't see what you're writing about him.

I didn't leave it there.

"You could farm," I said.

Patrus looked at me the way a sword looks at a plowshare. Not with contempt — with incomprehension. The two objects occupy the same category (metal, shaped, purposeful) and share no actual function. He opened his mouth. He closed it. He took another drink.

"I have six men on the eastern bridge," he said, "and four on the northern approach, and a rotating patrol of three along the lakeshore road from the fishery to the waystation, and a standing watch of two at the Academy's outer gate which the sorcerers haven't opened in four months but which I maintain because someone should, and the total strength of my section is twenty-two including myself, and of those twenty-two, fourteen can read, eleven can ride, seven are original garrison and the rest are replacements who came through the standard recruitment pipeline which is a man with a sword showing up at a village and asking if anyone wants to eat regularly."

He put his cup down.

"Farmers," he said.

On my fourth day I watched a Cuckoo detachment collect considerations from a village called Theren, which was three miles east of the Academy along the lakeshore road. The detachment was four soldiers under a corporal named Bess who had the particular walk of a woman whose knees had been badly set after a break and who had compensated by shifting her weight distribution in a way that made her devastating in close quarters and useless on horseback. She collected six baskets of lake perch, two wheels of cheese, a bolt of undyed linen, and an unspecified quantity of a local spirit that smelled like burning peat and tasted, I was later informed, like a strong opinion about mortality.

The villagers handed these over with the practiced efficiency of people who have done this many times. No anger. No negotiation. The transaction had the emotional temperature of paying rent, which is exactly what it was, and the fact that the landlord was a military unit rather than a property owner changed nothing about the fundamental exchange: you live here, we allow it, this is the price.

Bess was polite. She asked after a child's fever. She told the headman that the patrol had cleared a wolf pack from the southern road and the shepherds could move their flocks without escort. She mentioned, casually, that the Academy's stores were running low and any surplus grain would be appreciated, not required, appreciated, which is a word that does the work of "required" while maintaining the fiction that the arrangement is voluntary.

I've seen this before. I've done this before. Not the same thing — Codex's military operates under layers of oversight that would have given McMeister an aneurysm to imagine anyone operating without — but the mechanics are the same. A garrison serves a purpose. The purpose justifies the garrison. The garrison outlives the purpose and the justification becomes the garrison's own existence. At some point the question stops being "why are we here" and becomes "where else would we be," and the second question has no answer, which makes it the more powerful of the two.

What else would we do. The Codex does not have a passage for this. I've checked. McMeister addresses the dissolution of military units in the Fourth Commentary, but his framework assumes the soldiers have homes to return to, identities separate from their service. Patrus did not have a home. Patrus had a posting. The posting had become a home in the way that a barnacle's rock becomes a home — not chosen but grown into, not loved but structurally inseparable.

The Academy did fall. Not while I was there — later, during the Shattering, when everything fell, when the stars moved and the sky changed and every institution that had defined itself as permanent discovered what permanent actually means.

Which is nothing. The Academy sealed itself behind a barrier. The sorcerers locked the doors. And the Cuckoos were outside.

I don't know what happened to Patrus. I know what happened to the Cuckoos as a force, because the reports reached me eventually. They kept going. The Academy was sealed. The pay stopped. The resupply stopped. The maintenance contracts lapsed and the proprietary tempering process became irrelevant because there was nobody left to perform it. The blue-gray armor cracked and stayed cracked. The Academy-bred horses aged and were not replaced.

The Cuckoos kept collecting their considerations. They kept patrolling their roads. They kept arbitrating disputes. The sorcerers who had granted them latitude were behind a magical barrier, unreachable, and the latitude continued because latitude, once granted, does not expire when the grantor disappears. It just becomes the air you breathe.

Tarnished who passed through Liurnia reported fighting Cuckoo knights on the bridges, on the approaches, in the ruins of villages that had no one left to protect. The knights fought with the mechanical competence of soldiers doing their duty, and their duty was the same duty it had always been, and the institution they served no longer existed, and they served it anyway, because Patrus was right.

I received one account from a Tarnished who'd killed a Cuckoo knight on the bridge approach and searched the body afterward. Standard procedure, nothing personal.

She found a patrol schedule in his belt pouch. Neatly written. Current. The schedule covered a route that no longer needed patrolling, through villages that no longer existed, on roads that had been swallowed by the lake.

The schedule was dated. He'd been updating it.

What else would they do?

McMeister doesn't cover this. I've looked. The closest he comes is Book Dalet, Chapter Nine: "An institution is a promise made by the dead to the living, and the living are not obligated to keep it." But the Cuckoos weren't keeping the Academy's promise. They were keeping their own. The promise they'd made to themselves about who they were and what they did and why the armor mattered even when the armor was cracking and the sorcerers were gone and the villages were emptying and the roads led nowhere.

I think about Patrus sometimes. Eleven years at a posting. The name of every headman. The location of every fishing spot. The best farrier in the third village east. These are the details a man learns when he's stopped being a soldier and started being a resident, and the tragedy — not the Codex kind, the real kind, the kind McMeister didn't write about because it's too small and too common — the tragedy is that Patrus probably knew this. He probably knew he'd become a villager with a sword. And the sword was the one thing that kept him from admitting it.

I carried a sword for years. I carried it because a very large man without a sword is a liability. A very large man with a sword is an asset.

The difference between the two is a piece of metal and the institutional permission to use it. I know this. I have always known this. Knowing it has not once stopped me from strapping the blade on in the morning and feeling, for one precise instant, like the permission justifies the metal rather than the other way around.

The Cuckoos had the metal. The permission was in a sealed building behind a magical barrier. And they swung anyway.

I put the notebook away. Some reports don't need filing. Some observations are just a man looking at a garrison and seeing his own profession from the angle he tries not to look at it from, which is the angle where the sword is the reason and not the tool, and the uniform is the identity and not the job, and the Codex passage about averting your eyes applies to the man quoting it as much as to anyone else.

(Book Gimmel, Chapter Three. I know. I know.)
