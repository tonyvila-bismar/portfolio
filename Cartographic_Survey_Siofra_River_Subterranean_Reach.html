Cartographic Survey: Siofra River, Subterranean Reach

by March

CARTOGRAPHIC SURVEY: SIOFRA RIVER, SUBTERRANEAN REACH

First Expedition. Filed with the Royal Geographical Office, Leyndell.

Surveyor: Cartographer Brenn, Third Class.

Commission: Standard survey charter, authorized by the Geographical Office, Year 413.

Duration: Forty-one days.

Status: Expedition terminated. Partial survey complete.

SCOPE AND METHOD

The survey was commissioned to map the subterranean course of the Siofra River from the Mistwood elevator terminus to the navigable limit. The Geographical Office's records contain no existing map of the subterranean Siofra. Prior to this survey, the only documentation was a marginal note in a trade ledger from Year 112 that reads: "Elevator functions. River continues underground. Did not proceed."

The note does not explain why the author did not proceed. After forty-one days underground, I can offer several possibilities.

Standard cartographic method was employed: triangulation using baseline measurements, compass bearings corrected for magnetic anomaly, and distance estimation by pacing. Three assistants were assigned: Journeyman Pellan (instrument bearer), Apprentice Thessa (recorder), and a hired porter named Coll who was not guild-affiliated and who was paid 6 runes per day. Coll's rate was twice the standard porter rate. He set the price after visiting the elevator terminus and looking down. The Geographical Office approved the rate without negotiation.

FINDINGS: PHYSICAL GEOGRAPHY

The Siofra River descends approximately 800 feet below the surface of Limgrave through a series of natural caverns and engineered passages. The distinction between natural and engineered is not always clear. Some passages show tool marks consistent with excavation. Others show tool marks consistent with decoration. The decorated passages are wider, higher, and lined with a stone that does not match any geological stratum in the Geographical Office's Limgrave survey.

The river itself is approximately 40 feet wide at the elevator terminus and widens to approximately 200 feet at the furthest point of the survey. The water is clear. The current is steady. The temperature is 12 degrees lower than the surface Siofra, which is consistent with depth but not with the thermal readings taken at the far survey point, where the water temperature rises to approximately 4 degrees above surface normal. There is no identified heat source.

The cavern system opens into a space that the survey designates "the primary chamber." The primary chamber is large enough that the far walls were not visible from the entrance by lantern light. Triangulation from three baseline positions within the chamber produces a width estimate of approximately one mile and a height estimate of approximately 600 feet.

There are stars on the ceiling.

FINDINGS: THE STARS

The ceiling of the primary chamber displays points of light. The points are white, steady, and distributed in patterns that do not correspond to any known constellation visible from the surface.

The points are not bioluminescent organisms. Journeyman Pellan discharged a glintstone flare at the ceiling from a distance of approximately 500 feet. The flare illuminated stone. The points of light continued to shine through the illuminated stone. They are not embedded in the ceiling. They are not reflected from below. They appear to be behind the stone, or through it, or in a place that the word "behind" does not adequately describe.

The survey's instruments include a standard astrolabe. I used it. The readings are internally consistent: the points have fixed positions relative to each other, they do not move over a twelve-hour observation period, and their angular separations are measurable. They behave, in all mechanical respects, as stars.

They are underground.

I have reported this finding without interpretation because interpretation is outside the scope of a cartographic survey. The Geographical Office may assign an arcanist or astronomer to examine the phenomenon. I note that the survey's charter does not include a budget for specialist consultation and that the Geographical Office's specialist consultation fund was depleted in Year 411.

FINDINGS: THE STRUCTURES

The primary chamber contains built structures. They are not ruins. They are not collapsed. They are intact, maintained, and in some cases occupied.

The structures are of a style that does not appear in any architectural reference held by the Geographical Office. The stone is dark. The proportions are elongated — doorways approximately nine feet high, ceilings approximately fifteen. The decorative motifs are geometric: circles, crescents, and a recurring symbol that resembles an eye or a stylized star. There are no Erdtree motifs. There are no Golden Order inscriptions. The structures predate the Golden Order.

How far they predate the Golden Order, I cannot determine. The stone shows no weathering. Underground stone does not weather in the same way as surface stone, but it does erode, discolor, and accumulate deposits over centuries. This stone shows none of these signs. Either the structures are very young or they are maintained by a process I did not observe.

The occupied structures are occupied by people. I use the word "people" because I do not have a more precise term that the Geographical Office would accept. They are humanoid. They are approximately six feet tall. Their skin is grey. They wear robes and armor that matches the architectural style.

They did not speak to us. They observed us. On three occasions, individuals approached our survey camp and stood at a distance of approximately fifty feet for periods of between ten minutes and two hours. They carried no weapons that I could identify. They did not gesture. When Apprentice Thessa raised a hand in greeting, one of them raised a hand in what might have been a return greeting or might have been something else.

On the fourth occasion, an individual approached to within ten feet of our camp and examined our instruments. I permitted this because I did not know what else to do. The individual picked up the astrolabe, looked through the sighting vane at the ceiling, adjusted the alidade with evident familiarity, and set it down. The adjustment was correct for the primary chamber's latitude equivalent — a calculation that had taken me three hours using reference tables. The individual performed it by hand in approximately four seconds.

The survey charter instructs the surveyor to record all encountered populations and to note "their disposition toward the Crown." I am recording the population. Their disposition toward the Crown is unclear. Their disposition toward the astrolabe was better than their disposition toward us.

FINDINGS: THE AQUEDUCT

The eastern edge of the primary chamber contains an aqueduct system. The system is functional. It diverts a portion of the Siofra River through a series of channels and sluices that I was able to trace for approximately half a mile before the passage narrowed to a point that the survey party could not pass.

The aqueduct's engineering is beyond anything in the Geographical Office's construction records. The joints between stone sections are invisible to the naked eye. The sluice gates operate on a counterweight system that I spent two hours studying and could not fully diagram. The water flow is regulated to a precision that I estimated, using our instruments, at plus or minus one-quarter cup per minute across the entire system.

Coll, the porter, has experience with municipal water systems in Leyndell. He examined the aqueduct joints and stated that the tolerances were impossible. He used the word "impossible" three times. He then stated that he would not proceed further and returned to the base camp.

I am not reporting Coll's assessment as expert testimony. I am reporting it because the survey log should contain all observations made by members of the expedition, and Coll's observation was the only one that accurately conveyed the surveyor's subjective experience of the aqueduct.

TERMINATION

The survey was terminated on day forty-one when Apprentice Thessa reported that her compass had begun pointing toward the ceiling. Journeyman Pellan confirmed the reading. I confirmed it a third time. All three compasses — standard Geographical Office issue, calibrated before departure — indicated magnetic north as directly overhead.

The survey party withdrew to the elevator terminus over a period of six hours. During the withdrawal, the compasses returned to normal operation in stages: ceiling, then forty-five degrees, then approximately north-northeast, then true north. The transition occurred over a horizontal distance of approximately one mile. I marked the positions. They may be useful to whatever expedition follows this one.

I have no recommendation regarding a second expedition because the Geographical Office has not asked for one. I note only that the primary chamber extends beyond the limits of this survey, that the aqueduct system continues into passages we did not enter, that the population we encountered was not hostile but was also not communicative, and that the stars on the ceiling remain unexplained.

The map is attached. It covers approximately one-fifth of the estimated total area. The uncovered area is marked with the standard notation for unsurveyed territory, which is a blank space. In this case, the blank space is larger than the map.
