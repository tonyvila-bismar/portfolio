The Substrate

by Sidonei ne Sangbelles

I want to be clear about something before we start: I am not a heretic. A heretic has a competing theology. I have data.

The distinction matters. It mattered to the tribunal, too — they spent an afternoon deciding which word to use before they expelled me. "Heretic" would have required a doctrinal hearing, and doctrinal hearings produce transcripts, and transcripts can be read, and the last thing the Crucible Temple of the Western Reach wanted was a readable account of exactly what I'd found in the breeding records. So they went with "disruptive influence," which requires no transcript, no hearing, and no explanation beyond the signatures of three presiding ministers.

I counted. It took the first minister four seconds to sign. The second took six. The third took eleven, which I found touching, because eleven seconds is a long time to hesitate when the other two have already committed, and I think — I genuinely think — she might have read the report.

She still signed. They always sign. The hesitation is not conscience. It is the body's last protest before the hand does what the institution requires.

Anyway. Let me tell you about the aspects.

The Crucible Temple maintains — maintained, past tense, I am no longer authorized to use the present — a breeding registry for every knight-aspirant in the district. Twelve generations of records. Parentage, aspect type, manifestation age, density rating, incantation capacity. The records exist because the Order needs to predict which children are worth recruiting and which are not, and prediction requires data, and data requires someone to sit in a basement and organize twelve generations of parentage records into something navigable.

That someone was me. For nine years.

(I should mention that I was not assigned to the registry because of my scientific abilities. I was assigned to the registry because I am — was — a junior herblore adept whose mentor died during a border engagement that the Temple later described as a "training exercise," and the dead man's apprentice is the dead man's problem, and problems go in the basement. This is a universal institutional principle. I have since observed it in four different civilizations across the Lands Between, and it is always the same: the person who inherits the inconvenient question inherits the inconvenient office.)

The basement was cold. The records were a mess. Three different filing systems had been attempted across the twelve generations, each abandoned when the clerk who devised it died or was reassigned, and none of the three were compatible. I spent the first two years just making the data legible.

And then I read it.

Here is what the breeding records show, if you are the kind of person who reads breeding records, which I am, because I was in a basement with no windows and no friends and a candle budget that the quartermaster reviewed quarterly with the enthusiasm of a man who has been told to care about candle budgets:

Aspects are heritable.

Not perfectly. Not simply. But the pattern is there if you have twelve generations and a candle and no social life. Horn-type parents produce horn-type children at a rate of sixty-eight percent. Scale-type parents produce scale-type children at seventy-one percent. Caudal with caudal: sixty-four percent. Wing-type — rare, only fourteen documented lineages in the full registry — wing-type with wing-type: eighty-two percent.

Mixed parentage produces mixed results, obviously. But even the mixed results follow patterns. Horn-and-scale parentage produces horn in the child more often than scale, by a ratio of roughly three to two. Caudal seems to be recessive to everything — it shows up in children whose parents showed no caudal traits at all, which means it was hiding in the lineage, waiting.

You can map it. I mapped it. It took me three years and four hundred sheets of vellum and a system of notation that I invented because no existing system was designed for this, and when I was done I had something that no one in the history of the Crucible Temple had ever produced: a predictive model for aspect manifestation based on hereditary lineage.

It was, and I will say this without false modesty because false modesty is a luxury of people who still have careers, the most significant piece of biological research conducted in the Western Reach in a generation. Possibly two.

And here is what the Temple did with it: nothing.

No. Worse than nothing. Nothing would have been fine. Nothing would have meant they ignored me, which I am used to. What they actually did was read the report, understand the report, verify the report's methodology (Minister Galven personally rechecked my sample against the original records and found two transcription errors in four hundred sheets, which is a rate so low that he told me, to my face, that my work was "meticulous"), and then convene a tribunal to determine whether the report constituted a disruptive influence on temple morale.

The problem — the theological problem, the one they couldn't say out loud — was this: if aspects are heritable, they are biological. If they are biological, they are not gifts from the Crucible's primordial wellspring. If they are not gifts, the entire recruitment theology collapses. The Order does not select children because they are chosen by the divine. The Order selects children because their parents had horns.

This is not a complicated inference. A child could make it. Several children probably have made it, because children are observant and cruel and they notice when every kid with horns also has a parent with horns, and they notice when the Temple scholars insist this is coincidence and divine mystery. Children know when adults are lying. They just don't have the data to prove it.

I had the data. And I was stupid enough to write it down.

The expulsion was — look. I have described expulsions before. I have been expelled from enough places that the process has become almost procedural, like being fitted for armor except in reverse, each piece of institutional identity removed in a specific order. First the access (they took my key to the records basement, which I had carved myself from erdtree heartwood because the original was lost decades ago and no one had bothered to replace it). Then the title (Junior Adept, struck from the rolls, my name written over with the phrase "position vacant," which I saw because the clerk did it while I was still in the room). Then the quarters. Then the district.

They let me keep my notes. This was either an act of mercy or an act of contempt — the notes were useless without the original records to verify them, and the records were in the basement, and the basement was locked, and the key was erdtree heartwood and they had it now.

I walked east for six days. I ended up in a settlement that I will not name because the people there were kind to me and I have learned that kindness extended to expelled researchers tends to attract institutional attention, and institutional attention tends to end the kindness. They let me sleep in a granary. They fed me. An old woman who kept goats asked me what I knew about animal husbandry, and I said I knew about hereditary trait expression in Crucible-aspected humanoids, and she said that was close enough.

She was right. It was close enough. The same patterns that I'd mapped in twelve generations of knight-aspirants showed up in her goat herd within two breeding cycles. Ear shape, coat density, horn curvature — all heritable, all predictable, all following the same recessive and dominant logic that the Temple had decided was theologically inconvenient.

The goats did not care about theology. This is the primary advantage of goats over temples. They also did not care about my credentials, my institutional status, or the particular political implications of my findings. They cared about feed quality and breeding timing and whether the fence was solid. I respected this enormously. I still do.

I built a practice. Not the practice I'd trained for — I was a herblore adept, I was supposed to be distilling aspects, amplifying incantation capacity, doing the fine psychokinetic work that a Crucible Temple herbalist does. Instead I was breeding goats and treating foot rot and teaching farmers which pairings would produce kids with denser coats for the mountain winter. Applied biology. The same science, pointed downward instead of upward.

(The Temple would find this degrading. A man trained to understand the divine wellspring of life, using that understanding to improve livestock. I find it honest. The wellspring is not divine. It is biological. It operates in goats the same way it operates in knights. The only difference is that no one builds a theology around goat horns.)

The farmers, incidentally, understood heredity perfectly. They had always understood it. Ask any goatherd in the eastern lowlands which buck to pair with which doe and they will tell you, in language the Temple would call "crude" and I call "precise," exactly which traits breed true and which skip a generation and which show up only when both parents carry the tendency. They knew this before I arrived. They knew it before the Temple existed. The Temple took the same knowledge, dressed it in golden robes, and called it theology. The farmers called it animal husbandry and got on with their day.

I mention this because it matters. The knowledge I was expelled for producing was not new. It was old. It was so old that it was common sense to anyone who worked with living things rather than theorizing about them. What I did — the thing the tribunal called disruptive — was apply common sense upward instead of downward. I said: the patterns you observe in goats also apply to knights. The biology does not distinguish between sacred and mundane. It does not check credentials.

This, apparently, was unforgivable.

I treated my first aspected patient three months after arriving in the settlement. A boy, maybe ten, with emerging scale across both forearms. His mother brought him because the nearest temple was a six-day walk and the scales were inflamed, red at the margins, and hot to the touch — the body's immune response attacking the new growth as foreign tissue. This happens in about twelve percent of scale-type manifestations. The Temple treats it with erdtree sap infusions and prayer. I treated it with cold compresses, a topical preparation of willow bark and rendered goat fat, and the observation that if you keep the new scale moist during the first three days of emergence the inflammation resolves on its own.

No sap. No prayer. The boy's scales settled within a week. They came in beautifully: amber-brown, close-set, each one about the size of my thumbnail. His mother looked at them and did not know whether to weep or be proud.

Both, I told her. Both is correct.

I wanted to tell her about the inheritance patterns. I wanted to say: your husband's grandmother had scaling, didn't she? And your mother's brother? I could see the lineage in the boy's aspect density, could almost read the generations backward from the specific configuration of scales on his forearms. But I didn't. Because telling her would mean explaining how I knew, and explaining how I knew would mean explaining the registry, and explaining the registry would mean explaining the expulsion, and I have learned — slowly, painfully — that not every truth needs to be delivered at maximum velocity to the first available audience.

Sometimes you just treat the inflammation and let the scales come in.

I should tell you about the wings.

This is the part that still — after everything, after the expulsion and the granary and the goats and the years — this is the part I cannot stop thinking about.

Wing-type aspect manifestation is the rarest. Fourteen lineages in twelve generations. I tracked every one. Most produced children with vestigial structures: small, non-functional, more decorative than aerodynamic. Painful during emergence (the skin of the upper back splits, and the cartilage that extends is soft at first, vulnerable, and the Temple's standard treatment was a poultice of erdtree sap and crushed amber applied twice daily for a month while the cartilage hardened, and I know this because I mixed that poultice forty-three times during my apprenticeship, and every single time I wondered why the cartilage hardened at different rates in different patients, and no one — no one — had thought to check whether the hardening rate correlated with the parents' wing development).

It did. Of course it did.

The fourteen lineages could be sorted into three groups. Group one: vestigial only, small, non-functional, the cartilage hardening within two weeks. Group two: extended structures, functional enough for gliding, cartilage hardening over one to two months. Group three — two lineages only, both from the same original bloodline — full manifesting wings. Functional. Load-bearing.

The third group. Two families. Both traced back to a single ancestor seven generations ago, a woman named Ureth who is listed in the records as a knight-commander and whose aspect density rating was the highest ever recorded in the Western Reach: ten out of ten. Every descendant of Ureth who manifested wing-type aspects produced functional wings. Every single one.

This is not divine selection. This is inheritance. Ureth's bloodline carries whatever configuration of the Crucible's biological substrate produces functional flight, and it breeds true.

I wanted to find them. The living descendants. I wanted to see them. Not for the science — though the science would have been extraordinary, to study a living member of the third group, to understand why their cartilage grows differently, why the musculature supports flight when every other wing-type atrophies before reaching functional capacity. Not just for that.

I wanted to see someone fly.

That's all. I wanted to sit in a field somewhere and watch a person — a person whose body carried seven generations of a gift that the Temple calls divine and I call heritable and that is, in the end, the same thing described in different languages — I wanted to watch that person open wings that actually worked and leave the ground.

I don't know why this is the thing that undoes me. I have survived worse losses than a research program. I have survived the loss of the research program. I have survived the granary and the goats and the six-day walk and the tribunal and the eleven-second hesitation of the minister who might have read my work. These are survivable things. I survived them.

But I think about Ureth's descendants and I cannot breathe normally.

Probably because they don't know. They don't know that their wings are not a random gift, that the capacity for flight runs in their family like eye color or the shape of a jaw, that seven generations of their line have carried this possibility and the Temple has never told them because telling them would mean admitting that the Crucible's gifts are biological rather than bestowed.

They think they are chosen. They are inherited.

Both of those are beautiful. The Temple cannot see that both of those are beautiful. That is the thing I cannot forgive.

I'm still in the settlement. The goats are doing well. The old woman died last winter — quietly, in her sleep, which is the best anyone gets out here — and her granddaughter runs the herd now, using the pairing charts I made her. The granddaughter is seventeen. She has a small horn bud behind her left ear that she hides under her hair because the settlement has no temple and an aspected child with no temple is a complicated thing. I pretend I haven't noticed.

(I have noticed. I have measured it with my eyes from across the room, because I cannot stop measuring, and it is a primary cranial aspect, probably horn-type, density rating maybe four. She will grow a horn. It will be small. It will not qualify her for the Order. She will keep raising goats, and the horn will curve gently behind her ear like a question mark, and nobody will write her name in a registry.)

That's fine. Not everything needs to be in a registry. Not everything needs to be tracked and typed and rated and recruited.

Some things just grow.

The erdtree outside the granary has been dropping seeds again. Golden, small, warm to the touch. I collect them. I plant them in rows behind the goat pen, evenly spaced, in soil I've amended with composted dung and powdered calcium from old bones.

The Temple would be appalled. Erdtree cultivation is sacred work, restricted to ordained groundskeepers. I am planting sacred seeds in goat shit and bone meal, and they are growing faster than anything in the Temple gardens, because goat shit and bone meal are what plants actually need, and sacredness is not a fertilizer.

The seedlings are six inches tall. They glow, faintly, at dusk.

I think about my report sometimes. Four hundred sheets. Meticulous, Minister Galven said. Two errors. Two errors in four hundred sheets and twelve generations and they buried it because the truth was disruptive.

Here's the thing about the truth being disruptive. The truth doesn't care. Aspects are heritable whether the Temple acknowledges it or not. Wing-type breeds true whether anyone maps the lineage or not. The Crucible's gift — and it is a gift, I am not so far gone that I cannot see the beauty of it — the Crucible's gift operates through biology, through substrate, through the specific and mappable and predictable machinery of inheritance, and this does not make it less miraculous. It makes it more.

A miracle you can predict is still a miracle. It is just also science. The Temple cannot hold both of those ideas at the same time. I can. I am standing in a field behind a goat pen watching erdtree seedlings glow at dusk and I can hold both of those ideas at the same time, and the fact that this is apparently disruptive is not my problem.

It is the Temple's problem.

I am going to keep planting.
