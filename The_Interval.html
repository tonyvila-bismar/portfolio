The Interval

by Pellegrin

The storm around Farum Azula does not move.

This is the first thing Vasko notices when the communion rite deposits him on the crumbling platform at the temple's outer edge. He had expected wind, noise, the tearing of air against stone. What he finds instead is stillness of a particular kind: the stillness of a river that appears motionless only because every particle of water is traveling at the same velocity. The clouds churn. Lightning forks between the shattered towers. Debris hangs in the air at every altitude. But none of it progresses. The storm is frozen at the instant of its greatest violence, and Vasko stands inside it the way a man might stand inside a photograph of a catastrophe.

He has come to eat a dragon's heart.

This is not metaphor. The Dragon Communion at the Cathedral of the Emerald Coast is a practice of literal consumption. The suppliants travel to the islands where the ancient corpses have been interred, and they eat. Some of those islands are where the dragons fell. Some are where the dragons chose to die. These are not always the same place. Scales, marrow, the dense cartilage of wing-joints, and sometimes, if the supplicant is ambitious or desperate, the heart itself.

What follows consumption is transformation. The body receives what the body eats. A man who devours the eyes of a lightning drake will find, in the weeks that follow, that his own eyes have developed a secondary iris of pale gold, and that when he speaks certain words in the dragon's tongue, the air around his hands ionizes.

Vasko has already consumed the spine of a lesser wyrm and the stomach lining of a magma drake. His left forearm is plated with something that is not quite scale and not quite skin. When he flexes his hand, the plates shift against each other with a sound like the settling of gravel.

The communion has already clarified several things. The boundary between your body and another body is a convention, not a law. Flesh is negotiable. What you eat becomes what you are, and what you are was always more porous than you believed.

But he has not come for a lesser wyrm.

He has come for Placidusax.

The beastmen find him on the second day. They do not attack. This surprises him. He had been told that the beastmen of Farum Azula guard the temple against all intruders, and that reaching the Dragonlord's resting place requires either overwhelming force or the willingness to die in the attempt. The other suppliants told him this. The texts in the Cathedral's restricted archive told him this. The priestess who administered his third communion told him this. She had lost both her legs below the knee to scale-conversion and moved through the Cathedral on a wheeled platform with a serenity Vasko found both admirable and frightening.

The beastmen do neither. They watch him. They stand at the ends of corridors and on the ledges of broken walls and they watch him with an attention that he can only describe as veterinary. They are assessing him. They are determining whether he is a threat or a patient or something else entirely, and until they have determined this, they will not act.

He passes among them for three days.

The architecture of Farum Azula resists navigation. The complexity is not the problem. Complexity can be mapped. The problem is that the temporal suspension affects spatial relationships in ways Vasko's training did not prepare him for. A corridor that takes twelve minutes to walk in one direction takes six in the other. A staircase descends to a chamber that is topographically above where he started. By the end of the first day he learns to navigate by the lightning: each frozen fork is a landmark, and the angles between them can be triangulated if he is patient. He is patient. The communion has taught him that too.

On the third day he finds the Dragonlord.

Placidusax is not dead. This is the thing that changes everything, though Vasko does not understand how much it changes until later. The Cathedral's archives describe the ancient dragon as a corpse, or at most a remnant, a body so suffused with residual power that it persists after death. A heated stone holds warmth long after the fire is gone. The archives assume something similar. The archives are wrong. Or rather, the archives describe something that is not death and not life and not sleep, and the closest word they have is "remnant," and the word is inadequate, and no one has cared enough to find a better one because no one has been here in a very long time.

The Dragonlord sits at the center of a platform of white stone. Two of his heads are missing. The stumps are too regular to be scars, too intentional. They look less like wounds than like places where something was retracted. The remaining two heads are lowered, jaws resting on the stone, eyes open. The eyes are the color of lightning viewed through deep water. They do not blink. They do not track. But they are not the eyes of a dead thing. Vasko has seen dead dragons. Their eyes are milky, flat, mineral. These eyes have depth. Something behind them is still processing.

He sits down across from the Dragonlord at a distance of perhaps thirty feet and does not move for a day and a half.

This is not bravery. It is procedure. The communion rites prescribe a period of proximity before consumption, a period the texts call the Interval. According to the priestess on the wheeled platform, the purpose is acclimation. Any being of sufficient age and force generates an ambient field of power. A forge radiates heat even when banked. The supplicant's body must learn to read that field before it can absorb the tissue.

A supplicant who consumes without acclimation risks rejection: the body treats the dragon's tissue as poison rather than instruction, and the transformation fails, and the supplicant dies in the specific and reportedly excruciating manner of a body that is trying to become two things at once and cannot complete either.

Vasko sits. He acclimates. And during the Interval, something happens that the rites do not describe.

He begins to perceive the storm differently.

The change is gradual. On the first morning of his vigil, the storm is what it was when he arrived: frozen, static, a three-dimensional image of violence suspended in amber. By the first evening, he notices that the lightning forks have shifted. Their positions are the same. Their angles are the same. But some quality he cannot name has changed. It is as though the lightning were a word in a language he does not know, and between morning and evening a different word has been substituted. Same length. Same emphasis. Different meaning.

By the second morning, the storm is moving.

Not quickly. Not in any way that a casual observer would detect. The clouds advance across the sky at a rate he estimates, after watching a particular formation for six hours, as roughly four inches per hour. The lightning forks creep. The debris drifts.

The entire tempest is in motion, but the motion operates on a timescale so vast that Vasko's first two days on the platform were simply too brief for his perception to register it.

The storm is not frozen. The storm is slow.

What Vasko understands, sitting on cold stone while the air around him shifts by fractions of degrees, is that the Dragonlord is thinking.

He arrives at this conclusion reluctantly, and only after the evidence has left him no alternative. The storm's movement is not random. It follows patterns that repeat on what he estimates to be a cycle of roughly forty years.

What he had taken for lightning forks are a network, a branching structure connecting to every fragment of debris, every cloud formation, every suspended droplet of water in the tempest. The storm is a single interconnected system performing operations with a regularity that cannot be accounted for by meteorology. The storm is the Dragonlord's cognition made visible. The Interval has given Vasko the literacy to read it.

The Dragonlord is engaged in an act of cognition so vast and so slow that from the perspective of a human lifespan it is indistinguishable from death. The two missing heads were not severed. They were given, surrendered to a god who has since fled, and whose departure the Dragonlord has been processing for an amount of time that Vasko cannot calculate because he does not know when the god left, only that the storm's current thought-cycle suggests the processing is not complete.

The Dragonlord is waiting for the god to return.

He has been waiting for so long that his waiting has become a form of architecture. The storm is the shape of his patience. The frozen lightning is the structure of a question he cannot stop asking. The beastmen are not guards. They are attendants to a mind that is still working, still turning the problem over, still running the calculation of whether the god's departure was a betrayal or a test or a necessity or a kindness, and the calculation has been running for so long that it has become self-sustaining, and the attendants have been born and have died and have been replaced by their children and their children's children, and none of them remember what the question was, only that the question is still being asked, and that their purpose is to ensure it can continue to be asked for as long as asking takes.

Vasko sits with this for a long time.

Then he stands up and walks to the Dragonlord's body and places his hand on the jaw of the nearer head, just behind the lower mandible where the tissue is softest and where, the archives say, the concentration of transformative power is highest.

The scale is warm. Stone-warm.

He takes out his knife.

Behind him, at the edge of the platform, a beastman shifts its weight. He hears the scrape of claw on stone but does not turn. A second beastman appears on the broken wall above. Their veterinary attention has acquired a new quality. They are no longer assessing. They are witnessing.

The heart is buried deep. Behind a cage of ribs harder than any stone Vasko has ever encountered, beneath a sternum that may have been reorganized by millennia of temporal suspension in ways no archive could predict. He has spent three years preparing tools for this. A blade of meteoric iron. A chisel forged from a Crucible Knight's shattered gauntlet. A saw whose teeth are dragonbone.

The tools are good.

He sets the blade against the tissue below the jaw, where the scale thins and the flesh beneath is dense and dark and, when his knife dimples the surface, warm enough that he can feel it in his wrist. The body under the blade is alive. His hands know this before his mind does. The meat resists the way living meat resists. Not the dry give of carrion. Not the mineralized crunch of a dragon dead two centuries. This has blood in it, old blood, blood that has been circulating through the same channels for longer than any civilization Vasko has ever studied.

He presses harder. The knife sinks a quarter inch. A bead of something dark wells up around the edge of the blade.

The emanation changes.

It does not intensify. It does not become hostile. What happens is subtler than that and worse. The emanation, which until this moment has been ambient, unfocused, the cognitive background radiation of a mind the size of a weather system, acquires for the span of one heartbeat a quality that Vasko can only describe as local. As though the vast process behind the Dragonlord's eyes had contracted its attention to the exact point where the knife meets flesh.

Then it passes. The emanation returns to its ambient state. The Dragonlord resumes thinking at a scale that does not include Vasko.

But for one second it did.

Vasko pulls the knife out. The wound seals behind the blade almost immediately, the scale-tissue knitting itself closed with the unhurried precision of a process that has been repairing this body for millennia. In ten seconds there is no mark. In twenty there is no sign the knife was ever there.

He is shaking. He notices this with the detached interest of a man who has trained himself to observe his own reactions. His hands are shaking and his breath is short and the plates on his forearm are vibrating at a frequency he can feel in his teeth.

He puts the knife down.

The communion has taught him that flesh is negotiable, that the boundary between self and other is a convention, that what you consume becomes what you are. These teachings are true. He has verified them in his own body. The communion works.

But the communion has always involved the dead.

The lesser wyrm whose spine he consumed had been dead for two hundred years. The magma drake had been dead for sixty. Their power persisted in their tissue the way a song persists in a recording, present and accessible but no longer in conversation with itself.

Consuming them was not different, in any meaningful sense, from reading a book. The author was absent. The text remained.

Placidusax is not absent.

Placidusax is in the middle of a sentence.

He sits down again.

He will spend four more days at Farum Azula. During that time he will map the storm's movement with increasing precision. He will identify what he believes are three distinct cognitive processes operating simultaneously: the question of the god's departure, a continuous proprioceptive scan of the body's structural integrity, and a third process whose purpose he cannot determine but whose pattern suggests recursion. He will not cut. He will not consume. The knife will stay in his pack.

On his last day, one of the beastmen will approach him. In its jaws it will carry a fragment of scale no larger than a coin, shed during a previous thought-cycle, fallen from the Dragonlord's flank. Waste. Surplus. Belonging to nothing the Dragonlord is still using.

The beastman will set it at Vasko's feet and withdraw.

Vasko will eat it.

The transformation will be minor. A thickening of the skin at his temples, a faint electrical charge in his saliva, and in moments of extreme stillness, a capacity to perceive the ambient cognitive emanation of any sufficiently old living system. Trees. Rivers. The geological strata beneath his feet.

He will return to the Cathedral. The priestess will ask whether he consumed the heart. He will tell her no. She will ask why.

He will say, The heart is occupied.

The communion recognizes flesh. The communion transforms. It does not ask whether the flesh is still in use.

The storm turns. Four inches per hour. Forty years per cycle.

The Dragonlord thinks.
