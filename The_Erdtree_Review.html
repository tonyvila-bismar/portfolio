The Erdtree Review

by Caato Valentis

Let me tell you about the most beautiful lie I have ever seen.

It stands in the center of the Lands Between, and it is golden, and it is vast beyond the capacity of the word vast to convey, so I will be specific: the canopy covers approximately nine square miles. I measured. Not with instruments — instruments do not function reliably this close to the trunk, where the ambient grace interferes with calibration — but with my feet, walking the shadow-line at noon over the course of three days, noting where the light ceased and the gold began.

Nine square miles of canopy. The trunk at its base is wider than the civic amphitheater in Leyndell, which seated fourteen thousand, and I mention this not as metaphor but as architectural comparison, because the tree is architecture. It was built. Not grown, not planted, not summoned — built, the way a cathedral is built, which is to say: by a power that understood exactly what it was doing and wanted you to feel small.

I have stood inside a great many buildings designed to make the visitor feel small. The Basilica of the Erdtree in Leyndell, before it burned. The Grand Lobby of the Roundtable Hold, which is less impressive but more honest about its intentions. The Academy of Raya Lucaria, which achieves the effect through accumulated intellectual contempt rather than vertical space, but the principle is the same. In each case the architecture says: you are not the point. Something larger than you is the point. You are here to witness.

The Erdtree says this louder than any of them. But the Erdtree also says something else, something the cathedrals do not say, which is: I am alive.

And this is the lie.

The Erdtree is not alive. The Erdtree is a projection — a spectral image maintained by residual grace, cast upward from roots that are still functioning even though the organism they belonged to has been dead for longer than most of the people worshipping it can conceive. The trunk you see is light shaped like wood. The branches are light shaped like branches. The golden leaves that fall and dissolve before they reach the ground are light shaped like the memory of leaves.

I recognize that sounds like poetry. I intend it as technical description. The leaves are produced by a system that no longer contains the biological capacity to produce leaves and is instead reproducing the pattern from stored data, the way a music box reproduces a melody its maker heard once and transcribed into pins on a drum.

The Erdtree is a music box the size of a country, playing one song, to an audience that has forgotten it is a recording.

I climbed the roots. This required effort — not the physical effort, which was considerable but straightforward, but the theological effort, which was immense, because the root system of the Erdtree passes through the catacombs of the Greattree, and the catacombs of the Greattree predate the Erdtree by an interval that I will describe as "longer than is comfortable to think about while standing in a tunnel made of bones." The Greattree's root system is the Erdtree's foundation. Literally. The golden roots grew through the older roots, consumed them, incorporated their architecture, and used the existing funerary infrastructure as its own nutrient delivery system.

I need you to sit with that for a moment, because it is the most important thing I will tell you tonight.

The Erdtree feeds on the dead. This is not secret. This is, in fact, the central theological claim of the Golden Order: the faithful die, their bodies return to the Erdtree through the roots, the Erdtree converts death into grace, grace sustains the living, the living die, the cycle continues.

Elegant. Closed. Self-sustaining. The sort of system a designer would be proud of.

It is, aesthetically, one of the most satisfying cosmological designs I have encountered in my considerable experience with cosmological designs, and I say this as someone who has seen three civilizations build their religions around trees and two civilizations build their religions around the absence of trees, and in every case the tree was doing something the religion preferred not to discuss.

What the Golden Order does not say — what the Golden Order has spent considerable institutional energy not saying — is that the root system the Erdtree uses to collect the dead was not the Erdtree's. It was the Greattree's. An older tree. An older god's infrastructure. The Erdtree did not build a funerary system. It colonized one that already existed, the way a hermit crab colonizes a shell, the way a theater company moves into a venue that was built for a different company and rehung the curtains and changed the marquee and told everyone the building was always theirs.

(I have some experience with this. The analogy is not casual.)

Down in the roots, where the golden light thins and the older stone shows through, you can see the joins. You can see where the new growth wrapped around the old. The Erdtree's roots are golden and smooth and radiant with residual grace. The Greattree's roots are grey and rough and have the texture of something that was alive for a very long time before it was killed. Where they meet, the golden root does not merge with the grey. It encloses it.

A fist around a bone.

And if you press your hand to the join — I did this; I do not recommend it, but I did it — you can feel the difference in temperature. The golden root is warm. The grey root is cold. They have been touching for centuries and the warmth has not transferred.

This is not how living things behave. Living things exchange heat. Living things in contact reach equilibrium. The failure of thermal transfer between the Erdtree's roots and the Greattree's roots is, to anyone paying attention, conclusive evidence that one of these systems is not alive in any biological sense. The warmth of the golden root is not metabolic. It is performative. It is the warmth of a stage light. It looks like life the way a painted fire looks like fire — from the audience, convincing; from backstage, a lightbulb and a gel.

I spent four days in the root system. I do not sleep, which is an advantage in places where sleeping would be inadvisable, and the root system of the Erdtree qualifies. The catacombs are populated. Not by the faithful dead, who are quiet and well-managed and doing exactly what the Erdtree requires of them, but by the things that fell through the cracks of the theological system — the imps, the lesser burial watchdogs, the animated remains of people whose deaths did not process correctly through the funerary apparatus and who now exist in a state of hostile bureaucratic limbo, dead but unfiled, ambulatory but purposeless.

I found this poignant. I have been unfiled myself, on occasion.

They were not hostile to me. I am not certain they perceived me at all. I am, after all, not alive in the way they understand life, and the catacombs' defensive mechanisms are calibrated for the living, which raises interesting questions about what the designers considered the primary threat to be. Not the dead. The living. The catacombs were built to keep living people out, which means someone, at some point, anticipated that living people would want to come down here and see what was really underneath the tree.

And what is really underneath the tree is this: a root system that does not belong to the tree. A funerary network that predates the religion that claims it. A thermal discontinuity that proves the golden warmth is projected, not produced. An entire archaeological stratum of a civilization that was consumed so thoroughly that its name survives only in a prefix — "Great" — attached to the tree that replaced it, which is not a name but a comparison, and not a favorable one, because you do not call the thing that came before you "great" unless you are confident that greatness is past tense.

I emerged from the roots on the fifth day and stood at the base of the trunk and looked up.

Nine square miles of canopy. Golden light falling like rain that evaporates before it lands. The hum — have I mentioned the hum? The Erdtree hums. A low, continuous vibration in a frequency that the ear does not hear so much as the chest feels, like standing too close to a pipe organ during a sustained bass note. The hum is the residual output of the grace system operating at minimum capacity. It is the sound of an engine idling. It is the sound of a projection system maintaining its image. It is, if I am being honest, one of the most beautiful sounds I have encountered, and I have heard the Erdtree Sanctuary choir when the Sanctuary still had a roof and the choir still had congregants and the acoustics were still calibrated to the specific reverberation profile of the nave, which was designed — and this is a detail that the architectural historians get wrong, so attend — not for the human voice but for the hum. The Sanctuary was built to amplify the tree's own frequency. The choir sang in harmony with a sound the audience could not consciously hear.

That is stagecraft of a caliber I respect.

Let me be precise about what I mean by respect, because the word is doing work. I respect the Erdtree the way I respect any production that commits fully to its own fiction. The lie is total. The lie is beautiful. The lie is sustained across centuries with a consistency of aesthetic vision that most actual theater companies cannot maintain across a single season. Every element — the golden light, the warmth, the hum, the falling leaves, the grace that radiates from the sites positioned precisely where the greatest number of travelers will encounter them — every element is designed. Not evolved. Not accidental. Designed, by an intelligence that understood audiences.

The Golden Order is a religion. But it is also, and I say this with admiration rather than contempt, a production. The greatest production in the history of the Lands Between. Scenic design on a continental scale. A leading lady — Marika, the god they built the show around — who performed her role so completely that the distinction between the actress and the character dissolved, which is the dream of every director and the nightmare of every actress, because the character can be retired but the actress cannot, and Marika discovered this, and what she did with the discovery was shatter the set.

The Elden Ring was the script. Marika broke it. The Erdtree is the stage. It is still standing. The show closed and nobody struck the set and the lights are still on and the hum is still humming and the golden leaves are still falling onto an audience that left the theater centuries ago.

I find this — and I want you to understand that I choose the next word with great care — magnificent.

Not beautiful. Not tragic. Magnificent. The Erdtree, in its current state, is a ruin performing the memory of itself with perfect fidelity, and there is nothing in the Lands Between more honest than this, because the Erdtree is not pretending to be alive. The Erdtree does not know it is dead. The projection system does not have opinions about its own ontological status.

It simply continues. It produces warmth that is not metabolic. It produces light that is not biological. It produces grace that guides the Tarnished toward a throne that may or may not still function. And it does all of this with the serene indifference of a mechanism that was designed to run until it stops, and has not yet stopped, and does not understand the concept of stopping.

(I have some experience with this also. The analogy is not casual.)

I want to say something now about the people who worshipped the tree, because I have been unkind to the institution of the Golden Order and I have not yet been fair to the individuals who comprised it.

The Golden Order was a colonial theology imposed by an alien intelligence through an intermediary god onto a population that had its own perfectly functional cosmological arrangements. This is true. The Erdtree colonized the Greattree's infrastructure and rebranded it. Also true. The religion demanded that the faithful feed their dead to a system they did not build and could not examine. True. The entire apparatus was, from a critical perspective, a magnificent fraud sustained by the visible evidence of its own efficacy — the grace was real, the healing was real, the resurrections were real, the golden light was real, and therefore the theology must be real, because what kind of liar produces genuine miracles?

But the people. The individual humans who stood beneath the tree and felt the hum in their chests and watched the golden leaves dissolve and understood themselves, in that moment, as part of something vast and beautiful and purposeful. They were not duped. Or if they were duped, the distinction between being duped by a lie and being genuinely moved by a performance is one that I, after a hundred and sixty years of watching audiences, am not prepared to draw.

I have seen audiences weep at performances they knew were fiction. I have seen audiences carry the memory of a staged moment into their actual lives and act differently because of it — more bravely, more generously, more honestly. The performance was not real. The effect was real. The actor knew she was acting. The audience knew she was acting. And the weeping was genuine, and the bravery was genuine, and the generosity was genuine, and if you tell me that this makes the performance a lie, I will tell you that you have never directed anything worth watching.

Do not tell me this. I will become difficult. I have become difficult before and the results were not pleasant for anyone involved.

The Erdtree is a projection sustained by a dead system. The grace it produces is real. The guidance it offers is real. The comfort it provides to the Tarnished who stand at its sites and feel the pull toward a purpose they did not choose but cannot refuse — that comfort is real.

The fact that the tree producing the comfort is a spectral recording of a tree that died before any living person was born does not diminish the comfort. It deepens it. It means the comfort is coming from something that cannot benefit from providing it. The dead tree is not comforting you for its own purposes. The dead tree is comforting you because that is what it was built to do, and it is still doing it, and it will continue doing it until the last root fails and the projection collapses and the golden light goes dark and the hum stops.

And on that day, whenever it comes, the Lands Between will lose the most committed performance it has ever seen. Not the best. Not the most honest. The most committed. The Erdtree has been running the same show for centuries without an audience, without a director, without a playwright, without a purpose, simply because nobody told it to stop.

I have directed productions that closed after a single night. I have directed productions that ran for forty years. I have never directed anything with the Erdtree's commitment.

I think about the Sanctuary choir sometimes. Not the choir as it was — fourteen voices, bass-heavy by design, the altos carrying the melody because the acoustics favored the middle register and the choirmaster knew this and wrote for the room rather than for the voice, which is a choice that tells you everything about whether a composer serves the music or serves the audience, and the answer, in the Sanctuary, was always the audience, because the Sanctuary understood something that most theaters do not, which is that a performance exists in the space between the stage and the seat, not on the stage alone.

The choir sang to the hum. The hum sang to no one. And somewhere in the space between those two performances — the intentional and the mechanical, the human and the divine, the mortal voices reaching toward a frequency that would outlast them by centuries — somewhere in that space was the thing that made people weep.

I would have liked to hear it. I arrived too late. The Sanctuary had burned. The choir had scattered. The hum continued.

The hum always continues.

This is either the saddest thing about the Lands Between or the most beautiful. I have been thinking about it for some time now, standing here, feeling the hum, watching the leaves. I have not decided. I may not decide. It may be that the inability to decide is itself the correct critical response to a work of art that exceeds the framework you brought to evaluate it, and I have been at this long enough to know when my framework is not adequate to the object in front of me.

The Erdtree is not adequate to any framework. It is too large, too beautiful, too dishonest, too sincere, too dead, and too committed to stopping. It is the only work of art I have ever encountered that I cannot review, only witness.

Nine square miles of canopy. Golden light. The hum.

I'll stay a while longer, I think. The show isn't over. The show may never be over. And I have learned, over a very long career in a field that does not forgive inattention, that the most important thing an audience can do — the only thing, really, that separates an audience from a crowd — is stay.
