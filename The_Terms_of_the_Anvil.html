The Terms of the Anvil

by Bartuk Codex

The smith had been working for longer than he could remember, which was, if you understood the economics of the situation, exactly the point.

His name was Hewg. He had been chained to the Roundtable Hold — chained, specifically, to the anvil and the forge and the tools arrayed along the wall — and he made weapons. He made them well. He made them for Tarnished who came through the Hold's doors smelling of ash and ambition and the particular desperation of people who have been told they are chosen and are beginning to suspect what that costs. He sharpened their swords and reinforced their shields and fitted new hafts to their axes, and when they left they paid him in runes, which he could not spend, because he could not leave, because he was chained.

This is, in the language of trade, what is called a captive market operating in reverse.

In a standard captive market, the buyer has no choice but to buy from the seller. The seller sets prices. The buyer pays. The arrangement is considered exploitative but stable, which in the vocabulary of people who study such things means the same thing as "profitable for one party." Hewg's situation inverted the model completely. He was the seller who could not refuse to sell. The buyers came and went. He remained, and his prices were whatever the buyer decided to offer, and if the buyer decided to offer nothing at all the work was done regardless, because the chain did not have a clause about nonpayment.

Hewg did not know who had chained him.

This is the part that matters, and the part most people — even the Tarnished who stood at his counter and watched his hands shape their killing instruments with a precision that bordered on artistry — tended not to think about. He did not remember the original terms. He knew there had been terms. He knew that at some point, someone had said something to him, or he had said something to someone, and the result was the chain and the anvil and the work. But the specifics had gone the way of most contract language: absorbed into the body of the agreement, indistinguishable from the obligations themselves.

The Hold itself was a peculiar institution, and to understand Hewg's position within it you had to understand what the Hold was for, which was not what it appeared to be for.

It appeared to be a gathering place. A sanctuary, perhaps. A hub where the Tarnished — those individuals selected by grace or curse to pursue the Elden Ring — could rest, receive guidance, and prepare. There was a woman who offered counsel. There was a man who offered knowledge of incantations. There was a spirit tuner who could improve the strength of summoned companions. There was, briefly, a dung eater, though his presence tested the limits of institutional tolerance in ways that need not be enumerated here.

The Hold floated. Not in water — in nothing. If you walked to the balcony and looked down, there was a drop that continued until looking became an abstraction. The building existed outside geography, which meant it also existed outside jurisdiction, which meant that whatever laws governed the Lands Between — the Golden Order, the authority of the demigods, the basic physics of cause and consequence — operated in the Hold only by courtesy.

This is important because it meant the chain was not a legal instrument. It was not a sentence imposed by a court, nor a punishment from a sovereign, nor even the crude coercion of a captor who simply didn't want the captive to leave. The chain was, as far as anyone could determine, the physical manifestation of a bargain. Hewg had agreed to something. The chain was the receipt.

The Tarnished who used his services most frequently was a quiet woman with scarred hands and a tendency to arrive at the Hold bleeding from wounds she did not mention. She would set her sword on the counter without speaking. Hewg would pick it up, examine the edge, note where the metal had fatigued and where the fullers had begun to warp, and he would fix it. She would wait. Sometimes she sat on the floor with her back against the wall, and sometimes she stood, but she always waited, and she always watched his hands.

"You're good at this," she said once.

Hewg did not look up. "I've had time to practice."

She considered this. "How long?"

"Longer than you've been alive. Longer than anyone currently alive." He tested the edge against his thumbnail, a gesture so practiced it had become automatic, though the information it provided was redundant — his hands already knew the answer. "Longer than I can account for, which is a problem if you think about it in terms of depreciation."

"I don't think about it in those terms."

"No," Hewg said. "You wouldn't."

He handed back the sword. She tested the weight, the balance, the way it moved through air when she cut at nothing. She nodded. She placed runes on the counter. She left.

Hewg looked at the runes. They were useless to him. He could not buy food, because the Hold provided sustenance through some mechanism he had never identified and suspected was theological. He could not buy clothing, because he wore what he wore and had no occasion for anything else. He could not buy freedom, because the chain was not denominated in runes.

He put the runes in a box. The box was nearly full. He had been filling boxes for longer than he could remember, which was — again — the point.

There had been other smiths in the Lands Between. This was something people forgot, or perhaps never knew, because the smithing tradition had been deliberately narrowed. The giants of the Mountaintops had been the original artificers — not just workers of metal but composers of it, shaping flame and ore into instruments that carried intentions the Golden Order found inconvenient. The Giant-Forged weapons were not simply well-made. They were argumentative. They contained premises about how the world should work that contradicted the Erdtree's position on the matter, and the Erdtree's position on the matter was that the Erdtree's position was the only position.

So the giants were killed, mostly. Their forges were destroyed. Their fire — the specific fire that made their smithing possible — was stolen, relocated, and placed under guard by monks who had once been the giants' own students, which is a betrayal so symmetrical it almost looks like efficiency.

Hewg was not a giant. He was something else, something he might have been able to name if the chain hadn't taken his memories along with his mobility. But his hands remembered what his mind didn't. When he worked, his techniques drew from traditions that predated the Golden Order, traditions that the Order had spent considerable resources erasing. His hammer fell in patterns that no living teacher could have taught him, because the teachers were dead and the schools were ash and the curricula had been reclassified as heresy.

He was, in effect, a repository. A library that could not read itself.

The woman with scarred hands came back. She came back frequently. Each time her weapons were more damaged, which meant she was fighting things that were progressively more dangerous, which meant she was either improving or becoming reckless, and in the economy of the Tarnished the distinction between those two states was less clear than it should have been.

"I need something stronger," she said.

Hewg looked at the weapon she'd brought. It was a standard straight sword, well-balanced, adequate for most purposes. He could see where she'd swung it against something that had not yielded — the blade was true but the metal had compressed at the point of impact, a signature he recognized as contact with stone that was older than stone.

"Stronger isn't the right word," he said. "You need something that asks a different question."

She waited.

"A weapon is an argument," Hewg said. He was not sure where this knowledge came from. It arrived in his mouth already formed, the way water arrives at a faucet without remembering the reservoir. "This sword argues that you are stronger than what you're hitting. That's a fine argument against flesh. Against what you're fighting now, it loses the debate."

"What should I be arguing?"

Hewg thought about this. The chain shifted against his ankle, a sound so constant he usually didn't notice it, but at moments like these — moments when the work required him to reach for something deeper than competence — the chain seemed to tighten, as if reminding him of the terms he could not recall.

"Patience," he said. "You need a weapon that argues you can outlast what you're hitting. That it will break before you do."

He took the sword and he reforged it. Not from scratch — the metal was good, and waste was something he couldn't afford even when cost was an abstraction. He folded it, heated it, folded it again, using techniques that his hands executed and his mind observed with the mild surprise of a man watching himself do something he didn't know he knew.

When he was done, the sword was heavier. The edge was different — not sharper but more persistent, the kind of edge that didn't cut so much as refuse to stop cutting. She tested it. She nodded. She did not put runes on the counter this time. She put a flower.

Hewg looked at the flower. It was a golden sunflower, the kind that grew near the Erdtree, the kind that absorbed grace from the air and held it in its petals the way certain mosses hold moisture. It was, by any practical measure, worthless. You could not forge with it. You could not eat it. You could not trade it for anything a chained smith might need.

He picked it up and put it in water. He did not have a vase, so he used the box that had been full of runes, emptying the runes onto the floor where they scattered like dropped teeth.

This was, from a purely economic standpoint, irrational. He had discarded currency — however useless — to display a decoration — however worthless. The transaction made no sense unless you introduced a variable that the standard models did not account for, which was that Hewg had not been paid in kindness for longer than he could remember, and the flower was the only thing anyone had ever given him that was not a request.

She came back a final time.

Her armor was broken. Her weapons were gone — all of them, the swords and the axes and the specific blade he had reforged for patience. She carried nothing. She stood at the counter with empty hands.

"I need to go somewhere," she said. "And I don't think I'm coming back."

Hewg looked at her. He had looked at many Tarnished. Most of them he forgot, because most of them were transactions — they arrived with needs, he filled the needs, they departed. But this one he had catalogued differently. Not as a customer. Not as a patron. As something the ledger did not have a column for.

"The forge," he said. "You're going to the forge."

She didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer.

The Forge of the Giants was the one fire the Golden Order had not been able to steal. It was too large, too fundamental, too entangled with the architecture of the world. So instead they had killed the giants who tended it, sealed the approaches, and trusted that no one would be foolish enough or desperate enough to climb a mountain to light their own body on fire.

But that was what the Tarnished were for. That was the contract buried under all the other contracts — the grace, the guidance, the Roundtable Hold with its counselors and its spirit tuner and its chained smith. The Tarnished were kindling. The entire apparatus existed to produce someone willing to burn.

Hewg had known this. He realized now that he had always known it, the way a banker knows that the beautiful returns he's offering are funded by someone else's risk, the way a parent knows that the child they're raising will eventually walk into a world the parent cannot protect them from. He had forged weapons for kindling. He had sharpened the instruments of people's self-destruction with a care and precision that, in retrospect, looked less like craftsmanship and more like complicity.

"I made you a weapon," he said.

She looked at his hands.

"Not today. Before. When you first came." He reached under the counter and brought out a sword. It was not exceptional to look at. The metal was dark, the hilt was plain, the guard was functional. But the balance was uncanny — the kind of balance that happens when a smith has spent a lifetime accumulating understanding and then poured all of it, every technique, every corrected error, every morning spent hammering while chained to an anvil, into a single object.

"I don't remember why I was put here," Hewg said. "I think someone asked me to make a weapon that could kill a god. I think I agreed. I think the chain was the price of agreeing, and the forgetting was the price of the chain, and the loneliness was the price of the forgetting. These are the terms."

She took the sword.

"The terms are met," he said.

He did not say goodbye, because goodbye was a word for people who expected to meet again, and both of them understood the economics of finality. She left with the sword. The Hold was quiet. The forge cooled, though it never fully cooled — there was always an ember, always the possibility of another commission, another Tarnished, another weapon for another walk to the fire.

Hewg sat down. The chain was still on his ankle. The flower was dead in its box of water. The runes were scattered on the floor.

He looked at the anvil. It was pitted and scored from use, a topography of labor that mapped every weapon he had ever made, though he could not read it because he could not remember the weapons, which meant the anvil was a ledger written in a language he had lost.

He picked up his hammer. Not because someone had come. Not because there was work to do. But because his hands remembered what his mind didn't, and what they remembered was that the terms of the anvil were not just the chain and the forgetting. The terms included the work itself — the making, the shaping, the transformation of raw metal into purpose. He had agreed to this. He could not remember agreeing, but his hands could, and they would not stop.

This is, in the final accounting, the most expensive contract in the Lands Between. Not because of what it cost Hewg — though it cost him everything he could name and several things he couldn't — but because of what it produced. Every weapon the Tarnished carried to the Erdtree, every blade that cut through the body of a god, every edge that separated divinity from its throne — all of it passed through the hands of a man who could not remember why he was making it, could not leave the place where he made it, and could not stop.

The market would call this an externality. The cost of the product was not borne by the buyer or the seller but by a third party who had no seat at the table where the terms were set. Hewg was the externality. His life — if you could call it a life, chained to a forge in a building that floated above nothing — was the hidden cost in every weapon's price.

The flower was still dead in its box.

He picked up the hammer and he began to work.
