Thops

by Sidonei ne Sangbelles

You want to know the funniest thing I saw in the Lands Between? Sit down. No — sit down, I'm buying, you're not going anywhere, I haven't talked to a person who wasn't trying to kill me in eleven days and you are going to hear this whether you want to or not.

So there's this man. Thops. You've probably never heard of him and that is precisely, exactly, viciously the point of this story.

Thops was a sorcerer. Educated, trained, brilliant in the specific quietly desperate way of someone who has figured out something important and cannot get anyone to listen. He studied at — well, no, that's the thing, he didn't study at the Academy of Raya Lucaria. He studied outside the Academy of Raya Lucaria. Outside the gates. On the ground. In the rain. Because Thops could not afford the entrance fee.

Let me say that again because I need you to taste it.

The Academy of Raya Lucaria, the most prestigious institution of sorcerous learning in the Lands Between, the place where they literally pull light out of rocks and reshape matter through applied mathematics, the inheritors of lunar wisdom and stellar revelation and a thousand years of accumulated cosmic understanding — they have an entrance fee. A physical key made of glintstone that you must obtain before you are permitted to walk through the door and learn the things that the people inside the door already know.

Thops did not have a key.

Thops sat outside the academy and studied alone. He read what he could find. He practiced what he could practice. He developed — and I need you to understand that this is not a small thing — he developed a spell. An original spell. A forcefield that deflects sorceries. Thops's Barrier. His name is on it. His name is on an original contribution to the field of applied sorcery that he invented while sitting in the mud outside the building where the people who would have benefited most from his work were eating dinner and not thinking about him.

The academy sneered.

That's not me editorializing. The spell description says it. Future generations will know the foolishness of the sneering sorcerers. It's right there in the text, which means someone — whoever writes the spell descriptions, and isn't that an interesting job, what's the pay like, do you get benefits — someone looked at what Thops did and felt compelled to note, for posterity, that the people who should have recognized his genius were too busy being credentialed to notice.

He died trying to get in.

He died trying to get into the building. His bell bearing was found clutched in his dead hand. Do you know what a bell bearing is? It's what's left of you when you're done. It's the part that can be traded. Thops's entire life reduced to a small metal sphere that someone can hand to a merchant and receive, in exchange, access to Thops's spell. The barrier he invented. The one the academy sneered at. Available now for purchase. The marketplace solved the access problem that the institution created, which is —

(Sorry. I need a moment. This is not about me. I am telling you about Thops.)

Right. Thops.

Here's the thing about Thops that nobody talks about, because talking about it would require the kind of structural honesty that makes institutional people very uncomfortable. Thops was not a victim of underfunding. He was not a victim of bad luck. He was not a victim of insufficient talent. Thops was a victim of a credentialing system that had been designed — designed, not evolved, not accidentally emerged, designed by people who understood exactly what they were building — to exclude anyone who could not pay the entry cost, and the entry cost was calibrated to exclude exactly the kind of person Thops was: talented, motivated, poor.

The glintstone key is not a test of ability. It is a test of resources. The ability to obtain a glintstone key correlates with pre-existing access to sorcerous networks, which correlates with family connections to the academy, which correlates with wealth, which correlates with — look, I don't need to finish this sentence for you, do I? You know this shape. Everyone knows this shape.

The shape is a circle. The people inside the circle decide who gets into the circle and they decide based on whether you already look like you belong in the circle, and the ones outside the circle can invent as many original spells as they want and it doesn't matter because the circle is not a meritocracy. It is a lunch table. It is a lunch table with a lock on it.

I knew a Thops. Not him specifically. A version of him. Different world, different institution, different locked door. But the same shape. Same talent. Same exclusion. Same slow corrosion of watching the people on the other side of the glass do worse work than you could do in your sleep and getting funded for it, getting praised for it, getting the title and the laboratory and the assistant while you sit in the cold and teach yourself from stolen notes and dream about the day they'll see what you can do.

They don't see. That's the secret nobody tells you. They don't see, and it's not because they can't. It's because seeing would cost them something. If the academy acknowledged that Thops had invented a barrier spell without any of their resources, without their instruction, without their facilities, without their glintstone, without so much as a warm meal and a dry place to sit — if they acknowledged that — then the entire premise of the academy comes into question. Because the premise is: you need us. The premise is: this knowledge is so dangerous, so complex, so demanding that it can only be transmitted within these walls by these masters according to these protocols. And Thops, sitting in the rain, inventing forcefield sorcery with nothing but his own mind and whatever scraps he could beg, is a walking refutation of that premise.

So they sneered.

Of course they sneered. What else could they do? Give him a key? That would be admitting the key system is arbitrary. Recognize his spell? That would be admitting their monopoly on discovery is a fiction. Let him in? Let him in and watch him outperform their tenured faculty in six months and then explain to the donors and the patrons and the noble houses who've been paying tuition for their idiot children that actually, it turns out, genius can grow in mud?

No.

Easier to sneer. Easier to let him die at the door. Easier to absorb his spell into the marketplace after he's gone, where it can be purchased and used and studied by the same institution that refused to let him cross the threshold alive.

The marketplace takes the cream. The institution takes the credit. The body stays where it fell.

I found his spot, you know. Where he used to sit. Outside the academy gates. There's a little alcove in the stone wall — not a seat, not a bench, just a depression in the masonry where the rock has been worn smooth by someone's back leaning against it for a very long time. That's Thops's chair. That's where he sat and studied and invented and slowly, slowly understood that nobody was coming to open the door.

I sat in it for a while.

The stone was cold. The view is the gates. Just the gates. That's what he looked at every day. Two massive doors made of — I don't know, enchanted something, glintstone-infused whatever. It doesn't matter what they're made of. What matters is that Thops could see them from where he sat and they were closed and he knew what was behind them and he couldn't get there.

I know what that looks like. I know the specific quality of cold that lives in the stone outside a building you should be inside. I know how the light from the windows looks different when you know you're not going to be warm tonight. I know the taste of studying material that was written for someone with access to a library you don't have, catching maybe sixty percent of the references, teaching yourself the other forty from context and guessing and sheer furious refusal to stop.

But I'm not going to do that. I'm not going to sit here and make Thops about me. Thops is about Thops. Thops is about a man who was right and died for being right in the wrong location with the wrong amount of money and the wrong set of connections, and whose contribution to his field — his genuine, lasting, theoretically significant contribution — is available for purchase at a bell-bearing merchant, which is the Lands Between's version of publishing posthumously in a journal that rejected you while you were alive.

You want to hear the punchline?

The punchline is that Rennala is inside the academy. Rennala, who has lost her mind. Rennala, who sits on the floor of the grand library surrounded by the crawling wet failures of her rebirthing experiments, who cannot govern, cannot teach, cannot perform the basic functions of the queen of the institution. Rennala has a room. Rennala has a door that opens for her. Rennala has the key — she is the key — and she is using the resources of the most advanced sorcerous institution in the world to endlessly produce broken things on the library floor.

Thops couldn't get in because he didn't have a key.

Rennala can't get out because she is the key.

The academy sealed itself around a mad queen and locked out a genius and the key system worked perfectly in both directions — keeping out the people who should be inside and keeping in the people who should be free — and the system worked, you understand? The system worked. It did exactly what it was designed to do. It preserved the institution. It maintained the hierarchy. It ensured that access remained controlled and that the only people inside the building were the people who were supposed to be inside the building, and the definition of "supposed to be" had nothing to do with talent or contribution or the ability to invent a forcefield from a ditch.

This is what institutions do. This is what every institution does. They build a circle. They put a lock on it. They calibrate the lock to admit the people who confirm the institution's importance and exclude the people who threaten it. And then they call the lock a standard and the standard a tradition and the tradition a sacred trust and the sacred trust a public good, and by the time they're done, the lock has disappeared into the language so completely that questioning it sounds like questioning civilization itself.

Thops questioned it. Quietly. From outside. With his whole life. And then he died, and his spell survived, and his name is on it, and nobody at the academy says his name.

(I shouldn't be this angry. It's not my story. It's not — it's not about me. The alcove in the wall is not my alcove. The cold stone is not...)

Right.

Another drink. On me. I told you I was buying.

So you wanted to know the funniest thing I saw in the Lands Between. Here it is. After Thops died, after his bell bearing was recovered, after his spell entered the marketplace — someone at the academy bought it. Someone on the inside. A tenured scholar at Raya Lucaria bought Thops's Barrier from a merchant, studied it, and incorporated it into their defensive sorcery curriculum.

They teach it now. Inside the building. Behind the locked door. They teach the spell that Thops invented in the mud outside their gates, and they teach it as part of the curriculum, which means they have acknowledged — implicitly, structurally, in the only language institutions actually speak, which is the language of what they choose to use — they have acknowledged that Thops was right.

They just didn't let him in first.

Future generations will know the foolishness of the sneering sorcerers. That's what the description says. Future generations. Not current ones. The vindication is always deferred to a generation that hasn't arrived yet, because the current generation is the one that did the sneering and the current generation is still in charge of the door.

Same as it ever was. Same as it ever was. Same as it...

Sorry. I need some air. Stay there. I'm coming back. I still haven't told you about the woman with the amber egg.
