The Price Of Horns

by Bartuk Codex

THE PRICE OF HORNS

For as long as the Great Caravan had kept its records — which was longer than most kingdoms kept theirs, and more honest — the pelts of the Lands Between had been among the finest trade goods that crossed the Sea of Fog. Silverskin rabbits from Liurnia, whose fur held the cold blue light of the lakes. Fire-elk from the Mountaintops, whose hides could be worked into leather that stayed warm without a flame. Even the coarse pelts of the ancestral followers, which no one particularly wanted but which lasted forever, and which the caravan sold to ropemakers in the eastern ports at a slim but reliable margin.

Orvath had traded in all of them. He had been a pelt-buyer for the Great Caravan for nineteen years, which meant he had crossed the Sea of Fog eleven times, which meant he knew the Lands Between better than most of its inhabitants. Most of its inhabitants stayed in one region and died there. Orvath moved through all of them and lived.

He was not a brave man. He would have told you so himself.

What he was, instead, was someone who noticed things. He noticed, for instance, that the price of silverskin rabbit had fallen by a third since his last crossing, and that the Liurnian trappers who usually met him at the southern lake-shore had not appeared. He noticed that the roads through Limgrave, which had always been dangerous in the way that roads in a feudal society are dangerous — bandits, tolls, the occasional overzealous knight — were now dangerous in a different way. The soldiers he passed were not collecting tolls. They were staring at nothing.

He noticed, most of all, that the golden light that had always drifted from the Erdtree had dimmed. Not disappeared. Dimmed. The way a candle dims when the wick has burned too low.

Orvath did not know what the Erdtree was, exactly. He knew what the merchants of the caravan had always known: that the Lands Between was governed by a metaphysical system that expressed itself as a very large tree, that the tree's health correlated with the stability of the local economy, and that when the tree was doing well, the pelts were thick and the roads were safe and the local currency held its value, and when the tree was not doing well, you charged a premium for risk.

The tree was not doing well.

He adjusted his prices accordingly and moved inland.

It was in the capital foothills, on the road that wound up toward the Altus Plateau, that Orvath first encountered the cage.

It was not unusual to find cages on roads in the Lands Between. The Golden Order, which governed the spiritual and legal framework of the realm, had opinions about who belonged where, and it enforced those opinions with iron. Orvath had seen cages holding heretics, cages holding those accused of trafficking with outer gods, cages holding people whose only crime, as far as Orvath could determine, was being from the wrong place at the wrong time.

This cage was different.

The person inside it was a child. A boy, perhaps eight or nine years old, though it was difficult to tell because the proportions were unusual. He was human — unquestionably human in the face, the hands, the way he curled against the bars. But from his temples grew two horns, each about the length of a man's hand, spiraling slightly as they rose. They were not grafted or artificial. They grew from his skull the way antlers grow from a deer, as naturally as bone.

The horns had been partially sawed. Someone had taken a rough tool to the left one and cut through it at an angle, leaving a raw, wet cross-section that the boy kept touching with the tips of his fingers.

There was no guard.

Orvath looked at the cage for a long time.

He was a pelt-buyer. He was not a soldier, not a knight, not a theologian, not a liberator of the oppressed. He was a man who could tell you the fair price of any animal skin within thirty seconds of touching it, and who had leveraged that single skill into a career that let him see more of the world than most people ever would. He was useful in precisely one domain and catastrophically unqualified in all others.

He opened the cage.

It was not locked. That was the part that stayed with him afterward. The cage had a latch, and the latch was not secured. The boy could have opened it himself. But the boy had not opened it. The boy sat inside the unlocked cage and looked at Orvath with eyes that were flat and empty and accepting, the way an animal's eyes go flat when it has decided that what is happening to it is simply what happens.

"Come out," Orvath said.

The boy did not come out. He pulled his knees closer to his chest and wrapped his arms around his shins and rested his chin on the sawed horn. The raw cross-section left a smear of something on the fabric of his sleeve.

Orvath was not equipped for this.

He sat down on the road beside the cage, because he could not think of what else to do, and waited.

The word, he learned later, was Omen.

This is what the Golden Order taught about the Omen: they were accursed. They were born bearing the marks of the Crucible — the primordial form of the Erdtree, the state of existence before life had been sorted into its proper categories. In the age of the Crucible, all things had mingled. People grew horns, tails, wings, scales. The boundaries between human and beast had been porous, and the porousness had been celebrated as divine.

That age was over.

The Golden Order — which was, among other things, a theory of what constituted a proper human body — had declared the Crucible's mingling to be contamination. The horns that had once been marks of divinity were now marks of defilement. Children born with them were taken from their families, imprisoned underground, and in many cases had the horns removed by force.

The boy in the cage was one of these children.

Now, Orvath was a trader. He had no theological training, no strong opinions about the metaphysics of the Erdtree, and no emotional investment in the Golden Order's definition of purity. What he had was nineteen years of professional experience in the assessment of value, and nineteen years of professional experience told him that something very strange had happened here.

The horns were the same horns. The biology had not changed. Whatever the Crucible had been — whatever primordial force had once made people grow these features — it was still operating. Children were still being born this way. The product was identical to the product that had once been considered sacred.

The only thing that had changed was the price.

This is a phenomenon that occurs more often than you might think, and it is worth pausing to explain how it works, because the mechanism is not intuitive and the consequences are catastrophic.

In the eastern ports where the Great Caravan did most of its business, there was a spice called reshka that had been, for centuries, the most valuable commodity in the trade. Reshka grew only in the southern marshes. It was used in religious ceremonies, in medicine, in preservation of the dead. A single measure of reshka could buy a horse. Two measures could buy a house. The entire economy of the southern marshes was organized around its cultivation, and the people who grew it were honored accordingly.

Then a physician in the northern cities published a treatise demonstrating that reshka had no medicinal properties. It did not cure fevers. It did not preserve the dead. It was, as far as anyone could tell, a pleasant-smelling powder that did nothing the less expensive alternatives could not do equally well.

The treatise was correct. The science was sound.

Within a decade, reshka was worthless. The southern marshes collapsed. The people who had organized their lives around growing the most valuable commodity in the world found themselves growing a commodity that nobody wanted. They had not changed. Their skills had not changed. The plant had not changed. What had changed was the story that everyone else told about the plant. And because the story was what gave the plant its value, when the story changed, the value vanished.

This is what the Golden Order had done to the Crucible.

It had published a treatise. Not in the form of a scientific paper — in the form of a theology, an architecture of law and faith and aesthetic judgment that declared the old mingling to be impurity and the new separation to be grace. The treatise was comprehensive. It was enforced by knights. It was built into the physical infrastructure of the capital, where the Omen were bricked into underground cells beneath Leyndell's streets.

And the people who bore the marks of the old value — the horns, the tails, the wings — became reshka farmers in a world that no longer wanted reshka.

The mechanism is always the same. First the story changes. Then the price changes. Then the people who depended on the old price are destroyed, not by any force of nature, not by any change in their own qualities, but by the simple, devastating withdrawal of recognition.

Orvath understood this in his bones. He had watched it happen to reshka. He had watched it happen to Liurnian silverskin when a fashion for southern leathers swept the eastern courts. He had watched it happen, on a smaller scale, every time a local lord decided that a particular trade route was no longer under his protection, and the merchants who had depended on that protection found themselves priced out of existence overnight.

He had never watched it happen to people.

The boy was still in the cage.

Orvath's name was not Orvath. That was the name the caravan had given him, because his birth name was in a language that did not cross the Sea of Fog well, and because the caravan preferred names that could be written in the common ledger without special characters. His birth name meant something like "the one who comes after," which was accurate — he was a second son, and second sons in his culture inherited nothing but the obligation to leave.

This is not important to the story except in one respect: it meant that Orvath understood, from childhood, what it was like to be defined by a system that had no use for you. Second sons left. That was the rule. He had followed the rule. He had joined the caravan. He had not looked back.

The boy had no such rule. The boy had nothing.

The boy's name, he eventually learned, was not a name at all. It was a designation. The Omen in the cells beneath Leyndell were numbered, not named. This one had been designated Seventeen. He had been in the cells since birth. He had been transported — Orvath could not determine by whom, or why, or to what destination — and the transport had been abandoned. The cart had broken. The guards had walked away. The child had remained in the cage on the road in the capital foothills, because the cage was where the child belonged, and the child knew it.

Orvath fed Seventeen dried meat from his pack and tried to explain to the boy that he could leave the cage. The boy ate the meat and did not leave the cage. Orvath opened the cage door wide and walked away, hoping the boy would follow. The boy did not follow. Orvath came back.

"You can go," Orvath said.

Seventeen looked at him with polite incomprehension, the way someone looks at you when you are explaining something that does not apply to them.

It was another two days before Orvath encountered anyone who could explain what was happening.

The knight was sitting beside the road at a waystation that had once been a minor church. The church's golden decorations had been stripped — by looters or by time, Orvath could not tell — and the knight was sitting against its bare stone wall in armor that was older than anything Orvath had seen in nineteen years of crossing. The armor was red-gold, heavy, worked with designs that Orvath recognized immediately as organic: leaves and branches, but also scales, feathers, the curve of a tail.

Crucible iconography. Orvath knew the style. He had seen it in ruins, in the deep places where the old worship had been buried under the new. He had never seen it on a living person.

The knight's name was Siluria.

She was old. Not old the way merchants get old — worn by roads and weather and the slow accumulation of trade injuries. Old the way stone gets old. As if she had been sitting in that armor, in that posture, for a very long time, and the years had settled into her the way sediment settles into a riverbed.

She had a spear beside her. It was made of a material Orvath could not identify — not metal, not wood, not bone, but something that looked like all three at once, as if the categories had been mixed and the mixing had been intentional.

"You've found one of the cages," she said, when she saw Orvath approaching with Seventeen trailing twenty paces behind. The boy would not walk beside Orvath. He would not walk ahead. He followed at a distance that suggested he was not following at all, that his movement in the same direction was coincidental, that he owed nothing to the man who had opened the unlocked cage.

"The guards abandoned him," Orvath said.

"They do that now. The system is failing. The Omen-keepers under Leyndell are gone — died, or fled, or went mad. The transports run without destination. The guards walk away." Siluria said this without emotion, the way you describe a supply-chain breakdown. The logistics of cruelty had collapsed, and the collapse had not freed its products. It had simply stranded them.

Orvath sat down.

He was not a man who made friends easily, or who sought out conversation on the road. Nineteen years of solitary trade had made him efficient with language and reluctant with intimacy. But Siluria had something he needed, which was context, and in Orvath's experience the fair price of context was reciprocal disclosure. He told her what he was. A pelt-buyer. A foreigner. A man who crossed the fog for a living.

She told him what she was. A Crucible Knight. One of the last. She had served the old order — the order before the Erdtree, before the Golden Order, before the categories had been sorted and the sorting had been declared sacred. She had served the Crucible itself.

"You served the tree?" Orvath asked.

"I served what the tree was before it learned to be ashamed of itself."

It was, Orvath thought, the most expensive sentence he had ever heard. He did not know yet what it cost, but he could feel the weight of the price in the way she said it — the way a lifetime of watching something you revered become something everyone else despised settles into the voice as a kind of geological patience.

There is a concept in trade that Orvath had spent his career thinking about, and it is this: the difference between intrinsic value and assigned value.

Intrinsic value is what a thing is worth because of what it does. Silverskin rabbit fur is warm. Fire-elk leather resists flame. These properties are real. They do not depend on anyone's opinion. You can test them.

Assigned value is what a thing is worth because of what people agree it is worth. Gold is valuable because everyone agrees gold is valuable. Currency works because everyone agrees currency works. The entire apparatus of civilization — law, property, contract, status — operates on assigned value. It is the most powerful force in the world, and it is made of nothing but consensus.

The trouble comes when intrinsic and assigned values point in opposite directions.

The Crucible's traits were intrinsically real. The horns grew. The tails manifested. The wings, in some cases, functioned. These were not cosmetic features. They were expressions of a fundamental life-force that the Lands Between had once organized its entire civilization around. The Crucible Knights — Siluria's order — had channeled these aspects into incantations of extraordinary power. Tail sweeps that could shatter stone. Wing manifestations that carried a knight above the battlefield. Bestial transformations that made the boundary between human and animal irrelevant.

All of this still worked. The incantations still functioned. The traits still manifested. The biology had not changed.

But the assigned value had inverted. The Golden Order's consensus was that these traits were contamination, and consensus, once established, is self-reinforcing. If the Omen are imprisoned, then the Omen must deserve imprisonment, because why else would they be imprisoned? If the Crucible is impure, then anyone who bears its marks must be impure, because the marks are the proof. The logic is circular, but circular logic is the most efficient kind — it requires no external evidence, because it generates its own.

Orvath had seen this before. Not here. Everywhere.

This is not a market failure in the ordinary sense. A market failure is when information asymmetry or externalities prevent a market from reaching an efficient outcome. This is something worse. This is a market where the price signal itself has been captured by an ideology, so that the market cannot correct because the mechanism of correction — the ability to observe reality and adjust — has been overwritten by a story about what reality should be.

The Golden Order looked at a child with horns and saw contamination. Orvath looked at a child with horns and saw a child with horns. The difference was not moral superiority. The difference was that Orvath had no investment in the Golden Order's consensus, and therefore no reason to see what the consensus demanded he see instead of what was actually there.

This is the only advantage of being an outsider. You see the price, not the story.

"What happens to them?" Orvath asked.

Siluria did not look at Seventeen, who had settled into a crouch thirty paces away, eating the last of the dried meat. The boy had not spoken. He ate carefully, with the methodical attention of someone who had learned that food was intermittent.

"Some are kept below the capital. In the cells. The ones whose horns are small enough to saw are sometimes released into the general population, if they survive the cutting. The ones whose horns are too large, or who manifest other traits — tails, growths, denser bone — are kept permanently. There is an entire infrastructure. Guards, feeders, surgeons who perform the horn-removal. A bureaucracy of exclusion."

"And this was different before."

"In the age I served, the horns were the mark of the divine. A child born with them was celebrated. A warrior who manifested Crucible aspects in battle was honored above all others. The same body. The same biology. The same horns growing from the same skull in the same pattern. Celebrated and then imprisoned. Honored and then sawed."

She said this with the flatness of someone who had said it many times, to many people, and who no longer expected it to change anything. The statement had been drained of its original heat by repetition, the way a coin is drained of its original sharpness by passing through too many hands.

Orvath thought about reshka. He thought about the southern marshes. He thought about the farmers who had woken up one morning to learn that the thing they were best at growing was now the thing nobody wanted.

"The children," he said. "The ones in the cells. How many?"

"I don't know. I stopped counting."

That was not the answer Orvath had expected. He had expected a number. Numbers were what he worked with. Numbers were honest. Numbers did not tell stories about themselves. A number was a number was a number, and the gap between what a number said and what was true was, in Orvath's experience, the narrowest gap in the world.

"Why did you stop?"

"Because the number went up every year, and knowing the number changed nothing, and I discovered that I preferred not to know a thing that I could not act on."

Orvath recognized this. It was the trader's calculation — the cold assessment of which information is actionable and which is merely painful. He had made the same calculation himself, many times, about many things. The famine in the eastern ports. The slave markets south of the fog. The caravan's own practice of buying pelts from trappers who starved their apprentices. He knew about all of these things. He had decided, professionally, that knowing about them and doing nothing about them was preferable to not knowing, because at least the knowing preserved the possibility of future action, even if that future never came.

It was a reasonable position. He had held it for nineteen years. He was beginning to suspect it was a lie he told himself so that he could sleep.

Seventeen did not speak for four days. When he finally did, it was to ask Orvath a question.

"Where are you taking me?"

Orvath did not have an answer. He had been walking generally southward, toward the coast, toward the port where the caravan's ship waited for the return crossing. He had been walking the way he always walked: purposefully, with a destination, measuring the distance in the terms he always measured distance — days of travel, cost of provisions, expected margin on the pelts he had acquired.

He had not thought about what would happen to Seventeen when he reached the ship.

"I don't know," he said.

The boy considered this. He was, Orvath noticed, remarkably self-contained for a child who had been abandoned in a cage on a road. He did not ask for things. He did not complain. He ate what was offered, walked when Orvath walked, stopped when Orvath stopped. He did not touch his sawed horn anymore. The wound had scabbed over, and the scab had taken on the dull, fibrous quality of healing bone.

"Can I stay in the cage?" Seventeen asked.

They had left the cage behind. It was still on the road, three days north. The boy was asking if he could go back.

"Why?" Orvath said.

"Because I know what the cage is."

Orvath sat down on a rock and looked at the child and felt something move inside his chest that he recognized, after a moment, as the feeling he got when he realized he had mispriced something badly. The gut-drop. The cold recognition that the number on the ledger was wrong, that it had been wrong for longer than he wanted to admit, and that the correction was going to cost more than he had.

The boy preferred the cage. Not because the cage was safe — it was not safe — but because the cage was legible. Inside the cage, the boy knew what he was. He was Seventeen. He was an Omen. He belonged in a cage. The system was cruel, but it was comprehensible. Outside the cage, the boy was nothing. He had no designation, no structure, no way to understand his own place in the world. Freedom, to a child who had never been anything but a category, was not liberation. It was vertigo.

Orvath understood vertigo. He sold pelts for a living.

This is what the Golden Order had done, and this is why it was worse than simple cruelty. Simple cruelty destroys people. The Golden Order had done something more efficient. It had taught people to destroy themselves. It had built a cage and then built the Omen to need the cage, so that even when the guards walked away and the latch was open and the road was clear, the product of the system would sit inside the system and wait for the system to resume.

That is the mark of a truly successful ideology. It does not need enforcers. It has been internalized. The price has been set so thoroughly that the commodity prices itself.

Siluria had been following them. Orvath had not noticed this, which was embarrassing for a man who prided himself on noticing things, but in his defense, Crucible Knights were very quiet when they wanted to be, and Siluria moved through the landscape the way old stone moves through geological time — slowly, inevitably, without disturbing anything.

She appeared on the fifth morning, sitting by their dead fire, her spear across her knees.

"I have something to show you," she said.

She led them off the road, up a hillside, through a stand of trees that had once been part of a larger forest and were now the last survivors of whatever had burned the rest away. The trees were old. Their bark had the quality of memory — ridged and layered, each ring a year that the tree had survived while the world around it changed.

At the top of the hill was a ruin.

It was not a ruin in the way that most ruins in the Lands Between were ruins — collapsed structures slowly being reclaimed by earth and moss. It was a ruin in the way that a stripped-down machine is a ruin. The walls were intact. The roof was intact. The doors still hung on their hinges. But everything inside had been removed. Every piece of furniture, every fixture, every trace of the purpose the building had once served had been taken away, leaving only the shell.

"This was a Crucible sanctum," Siluria said. "In the old age, before the Golden Order, this is where we brought children who manifested the aspects. Horns. Tails. Wings. They came here and they were taught what they were — what the aspects meant, how to channel them, how to live in a body that the Crucible had touched."

She walked through the empty rooms. Her armor scraped against the doorframes.

"It was not a kind place, by modern standards. The training was hard. The aspects could be painful. Some children did not survive the manifestation. But the children who came here knew they were valued. They knew the horns were sacred. They knew the tails were gifts. They knew the wings, if they came, were the highest mark of grace the Crucible could bestow."

She stopped in the largest room, which had been the central chamber. The walls were bare stone. But high on the wall, too high to reach without a ladder, someone had carved — or grown — a relief. It showed a figure with horns and a tail and spreading wings, and the figure was surrounded by smaller figures who were kneeling, and the kneeling figures had their hands raised in the universal gesture of worship.

Orvath looked at the relief and then looked at Seventeen, who was standing in the doorway with his arms at his sides, his sawed horn catching the light.

The relief depicted exactly what Seventeen was. A child with horns. The same child, the same horns, the same biology, the same marks on the same skull. In the relief, the child was a god. On the road, the child was cargo.

"How much," Orvath said, and then stopped, because the question he wanted to ask was not a question that had an answer in the terms he usually worked in. He wanted to ask how much the transition had cost. Not in gold, not in pelts, not in any currency he could write in a ledger.

He wanted to ask how much it cost a civilization to look at its own children and decide that the thing it had once loved most about them was now the thing it hated most about them. He wanted to know the price of that decision — the full price, including the externalities, including the intergenerational damage, including the cost borne by every child who had been born with horns into a world that had decided horns were a disease.

He could not calculate it.

For the first time in nineteen years of professional valuation, Orvath encountered a cost he could not price. Not because the cost was infinite — nothing is infinite; even grief has a duration — but because the cost was distributed across so many people, across so many generations, in so many forms, that the accounting would require a ledger larger than the world that had generated the debt.

"I can't put a number on this," he said.

Siluria looked at him with something that was not pity and was not sympathy and was not even understanding. It was recognition. She recognized a man arriving at a conclusion she had arrived at a long time ago.

"No," she said. "You can't."

Orvath took Seventeen to the ship. He did not ask the caravan's permission. He did not file the proper documentation. He walked the boy up the gangplank and told the quartermaster that the child was his apprentice, and because the caravan was a practical institution that valued productivity over genealogy, and because Orvath was one of its best buyers, and because the quartermaster had seen stranger things on the docks of the Lands Between, the lie was accepted without examination.

Seventeen crossed the Sea of Fog with his horn-stump scabbing over in the salt air and his remaining horn catching the light when the fog thinned. On the other side, in the eastern ports, people stared at the horn. They stared at it the way people stare at anything unusual — with curiosity, with mild alarm, with the quick calculation that all people perform when they see something they have never seen before and must decide whether it is dangerous, valuable, or irrelevant.

Nobody imprisoned him. Nobody sawed. Nobody called him accursed.

The horn was a horn. In the eastern ports, where the Golden Order had no jurisdiction and the Erdtree was a rumor and the Crucible was a word that meant nothing, the horn was simply an unusual feature on an unusual child, no more meaningful than red hair or a crooked nose. The people of the eastern ports had their own cruelties, their own exclusions, their own categories of contamination. But the horn was not one of them. The price signal had not been set.

This should have been a comfort. Orvath wanted it to be a comfort. He wanted to believe that the eastern ports' indifference to the horn proved that the Golden Order's hatred of it was arbitrary — that the horror the Omen faced was not an expression of some deep truth about the world but a local phenomenon, a regional cruelty, a price set by one particular market that other markets did not recognize.

It was not a comfort.

Because the indifference was also arbitrary. The eastern ports did not hate the horn, but they did not honor it either. The horn was irrelevant. Seventeen was not sacred and not accursed. He was just a boy with a growth on his head. The price had not been corrected. The price had been erased. And Orvath, who had spent his life believing that the fair price of a thing was the most important truth about it, discovered that there was something worse than a thing being mispriced. There was a thing being unpriced — stripped of all significance, neither valued nor devalued, simply ignored.

Seventeen grew up in the eastern ports. He learned the pelt trade. He was good at it — better than Orvath, in fact, because his hands were more sensitive and his eye was sharper and he had the preternatural attention to texture that comes from having spent years in a cage with nothing to touch but iron bars and his own sawed horn.

He did not go by Seventeen. Orvath gave him a name — his real name, his birth name, the one the caravan had replaced with something easier to write. It did not matter what the name was. What mattered was that it was a name and not a number.

Seventeen — the boy with the new name and the old horn — never asked to go back. He never mentioned the cage. He never mentioned Leyndell or the cells beneath it or the surgeons who sawed or the guards who abandoned their transports or the Crucible sanctum on the hillside with its carved relief of what he might have been, in another age, under another consensus.

He did not mention Siluria.

He worked. He traded. He grew into a young man whose single remaining horn rose from his temple like a declaration that had been made in a language nobody around him spoke. He did not hide it. He did not explain it. It was simply there, the way a scar is there, the way a birthmark is there — a fact about his body that told a story he was not interested in telling.

Orvath went back to the Lands Between four more times. Each time, the Erdtree was dimmer. Each time, the roads were worse. Each time, the pelts were thinner and the prices were higher and the risk premium that Orvath charged was more justified than it had been the last time.

On his last crossing, he looked for Siluria. He went to the hillside. The Crucible sanctum was still standing — shell-walls, hinged doors, empty rooms. The carved relief was still high on the wall. But Siluria was not there. Her spear was gone. The indentation in the earth where she had sat — Orvath was certain she had sat in the same place for years, perhaps decades — was softening, filling in with grass and fallen leaves.

He stood in the empty sanctum and looked up at the carved figure with its horns and tail and wings, and he thought about what Siluria had said. I served what the tree was before it learned to be ashamed of itself.

He thought about the reshka farmers.

He thought about the boy in the unlocked cage.

He thought about the price of a thing that had been sacred and then profane and then, in the end, simply unnoticed. He thought about whether unnoticed was better than profane, and he concluded, reluctantly, that it was — that indifference was preferable to hatred, the way poverty was preferable to slavery, the way a blank ledger was preferable to one filled with false numbers.

But he also concluded that indifference was not enough. That something had been lost in the transition from sacred to profane to invisible, and the thing that had been lost was not the horn itself — the horn was still there, still growing, still catching the light — but the knowledge of what the horn meant. The horn had meant something, once. It had meant divinity. It had meant grace. It had meant that the boundary between human and beast was not a wall but a gift, and that the gift was the most precious thing the world had to offer.

The Golden Order had destroyed that meaning. But Orvath had not restored it. He had simply moved the boy to a place where the meaning did not exist at all. He had rescued Seventeen from a world that hated him and delivered him to a world that did not notice him, and he was no longer certain which was the greater harm.

The ledger did not balance. It never would.

This is the reckoning that the merchant makes when the ledger will not balance: not that he was wrong, but that he was insufficient. That the transaction he completed was the best transaction available, and that the best transaction available was not good enough. That some debts are owed in a currency that does not exist yet, and cannot be paid until someone invents it.

Orvath left the sanctum. He walked down the hillside. He crossed the Lands Between one last time, collecting pelts that were thinner than they had ever been, trading with trappers who were more desperate than they had ever been, walking roads that were emptier and more dangerous than they had ever been.

He went home. He gave his pelts to the quartermaster. He sat in his room above the warehouse in the eastern port and wrote, in the caravan's common ledger, a single line that no one else would understand:

Omen (child, intact horn x1, sawed horn x1): fair price undetermined.

Then he closed the book.
