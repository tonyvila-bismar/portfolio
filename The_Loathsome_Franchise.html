The Loathsome Franchise

by Kontextmaschine

The Dung Eater is the single most misunderstood character in the Lands Between and it's not close. Everybody reads him as a monster. Serial defiler, curse-spreader, the guy so awful even the Roundtable Hold locked him in a cell. And yeah, sure, all of that is true. But none of it explains what he's actually doing.

Here's what the Dung Eater does: he kills people and defiles their corpses in a way that prevents their souls from returning to the Erdtree. The Seedbed Curse. The body is ruined — specifically, it's ruined in the theological sense, rendered unfit for Erdtree Burial. The soul doesn't go back to the roots. It doesn't rejoin the cycle. It's just out.

And here's the part everyone skips past: the Golden Order already does this. Not to random victims in alleys — to entire populations, systematically, as policy.

The Omen. Born with Crucible traits, classified as "Abeyant" by the Third Erdtree Synod, which means they exist outside the cycle of grace. They do not return to the Erdtree upon death. That's not a side effect. That's the classification. The Golden Order looked at a category of people and said: you are excluded from the afterlife.

The Misbegotten. Listed as livestock. Not excluded from Erdtree Burial technically — just never considered eligible, because livestock doesn't get buried.

Those Who Live in Death. Existing in a state the Erdtree refuses to acknowledge. Not dead enough to process, not alive enough to count. The census doesn't list them because they "should not exist."

The Dung Eater's innovation is not the concept. The concept — you can be alive and still excluded from the only afterlife available — is standard Golden Order theology. The Dung Eater's innovation is retail distribution.

The Golden Order does it wholesale. Through synod votes, through classification systems, through the quiet machinery of a state church that decides which souls are eligible for recycling and which are waste product. It's clean. It's procedural. It has a filing system. The Erdtree Sanctuary in Leyndell has an entire department dedicated to maintaining the categories.

The Dung Eater does it one body at a time, in the dark, with his hands.

And everybody finds him loathsome.

Not wrong. He IS loathsome. But the loathing is interesting, because it's not proportional. The Golden Order excludes entire species from the cosmic cycle and the response is theology. The Dung Eater excludes individuals from the cosmic cycle and the response is horror. Same outcome. Different aesthetic. The aesthetic is doing all the work.

This is what happens when you build a system that classifies some people as outside grace. Eventually someone comes along who agrees with you and applies the logic retail. You built the category. He's just expanding the customer base.

The Seedbed Curse is mechanically identical to the Omen classification. Both remove a soul from the Erdtree cycle. The Omen classification does it at birth through a synod vote that nobody remembers was controversial. The Seedbed Curse does it after death through an act of violence that everybody agrees is monstrous. But the soul ends up in the same place: nowhere. Outside the tree. Excluded from the system that defines what counts as an afterlife in this world.

The difference is authorization.

The Golden Order is authorized to exclude souls. It has institutional backing, theological justification, centuries of precedent, and a filing cabinet full of Morphological Deviation Reports. The Dung Eater is not authorized. He is a freelancer. And freelancers in a monopoly market are always treated as criminals, even when they're selling the same product.

It's worth noting that the Dung Eater isn't even the only unauthorized operator of Golden Order exclusion technology. Mohg — Morgott's twin, the other Omen prince — built an entire underground dynasty premised on a competing divine order. He didn't reverse-engineer the exclusion criteria. He went the other direction: he found a different god and started building a new inclusion system. The Formless Mother. The Mohgwyn Dynasty. A parallel Erdtree alternative for the souls the original Erdtree refused. Mohg is running a competing franchise. The Dung Eater is sabotaging the original franchise's product. Both of them are using the Golden Order's own architectural choices as their business model.

Think about Morgott. Omen-born, hidden his horns, defended the capital for centuries, never received Erdtree Burial eligibility despite being literally one of the demigods. The system that produced him classified him as waste before he could walk. He spent his entire life defending the institution that declared his soul ineligible for its services, and when he died, the institution processed his death exactly as classified. No exceptions. The machinery doesn't have an exception form.

The Dung Eater looks at Morgott's situation and draws a conclusion. Not a sympathetic conclusion — he's not trying to free the Omen or reform the system or make a point about injustice. He draws the structural conclusion: if the system can exclude souls from grace, then exclusion from grace is a technology. It can be deployed. It can be applied. And if the authorized operators refuse to see it as a technology — if they insist it's theology, divine order, the natural structure of the cosmos — then the technology is available to anyone who figures out the mechanism.

He figured out the mechanism.

There's a real-world version of this that's instructive. Every colonial administration that ever built a two-tier citizenship system — subjects and citizens, believers and infidels, civilized and barbarian — eventually produced individuals who applied the classification criteria outside institutional channels. Vigilantes. Paramilitaries. Freelance enforcers of a boundary that the state had drawn but preferred to maintain through paperwork rather than violence. The state's response is always the same: horror at the method, silence about the category. We didn't mean for you to do it like THAT. We meant for it to happen through proper channels. The proper channels being, of course, the ones where nobody has to watch.

The Seedbed Curse works because the Erdtree's recycling system has authentication requirements. The soul has to be in a certain condition to be accepted back. Defile the body in the right way and the soul fails authentication. It's a hack. Not a sophisticated one — more brute force than exploit. But it works because the system was designed with the assumption that only authorized personnel would ever interact with the authentication layer. The Golden Order never imagined someone would reverse-engineer the exclusion criteria and apply them manually, because the Golden Order never thinks of its theology as technology. It thinks of it as truth.

This is always the vulnerability. Every system that builds exclusion into its architecture assumes the exclusion criteria will remain under institutional control. Every time, eventually, someone figures out that the criteria are separable from the institution. You can run the exclusion software on unauthorized hardware. The institution is horrified, not because the exclusion is happening — the institution does that — but because it's happening without authorization.

The Roundtable Hold locked the Dung Eater in a cell. The same Roundtable Hold that employs a chained smith who can't remember why he's chained, maintains a library of incantations developed by the same Golden Order that classified the Omen as subhuman, and serves as the staging ground for Tarnished whose ultimate purpose is to burn their own bodies in the Giants' stolen fire. The Hold's moral calculus is not straightforward. But the Dung Eater is in a cell and the institutional exclusion apparatus is in a filing cabinet, and nobody sees the symmetry because one of them has paperwork.

The Dung Eater ending — if you give him enough Seedbed Curses and he becomes the Elden Lord — produces a world where everyone is excluded from grace. Every soul. No exceptions. The Blessing of the Fell Omen, spreading across the Lands Between like an update pushed to every device on the network.

And this is the part that's genuinely interesting: in that ending, nobody is excluded because everybody is. The category of "outside grace" expands until it contains the entire population, at which point the category becomes meaningless. You can't be excluded from a club that doesn't exist. The Dung Eater, by universalizing the Golden Order's exclusion technology, destroys the technology. Not by opposing it. By completing it.

The Golden Order's theological architecture requires a boundary between inside and outside. Souls that return to the Erdtree and souls that don't. The Omen are outside so that everyone else can be inside. The Misbegotten are outside so that everyone else can be inside. The boundary is the product. Remove the boundary and the entire system collapses — not because the Erdtree stops functioning but because the categories that made the Erdtree's function meaningful no longer apply.

The Dung Eater is the Golden Order's own logic taken to its conclusion. He's not the opposite of the system. He's the last guy in the franchise.
