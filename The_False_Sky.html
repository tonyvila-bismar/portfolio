The False Sky

by Kontextmaschine

The Nox built a fake sky and I can't stop thinking about why.

Eleven generations. That's the construction timeline for the false sky over the Eternal Cities — Nokron, Nokstella, the underground civilization of the Nox. Sixty-two volumes of construction records. Three hundred feet at the vault apex. Thirty-three technicians in the third generation, reduced to four by the fourth. A nine-hundred-year enchantment lifespan, which means someone in generation one did the math and decided that their great-great-great-great-great-great-great-great-grandchildren would need to re-enchant, and built the maintenance schedule into the original design documents.

The Nox were cast underground for defying the Greater Will. Their crime — the specific crime, the one that got them buried — was creating the Fingerslayer Blade, a weapon capable of killing the Two Fingers. They built a tool to cut the communication line between the Lands Between and its god, and the god's response was to remove them from the surface of the world entirely. Standard colonial-administrative response: exile to a place where the rebellion can't infect the compliant population.

And the first thing they did underground was build a sky.

Not a weapon. Not an escape tunnel. Not a plan to return. A sky. Stars. The specific constellations that the Greater Will's order governs, reproduced in enchanted stone three hundred feet above a civilization that had been condemned for rejecting that order. They defied the god who rules the sky and then spent eleven generations recreating the sky with such fidelity that their swordstresses train under it as if it were real.

The conventional reading is defiance. The Nox refuse to accept their exile. They rebuild what was taken. They persist.

I don't think that's right.

I think it's homesickness. I think it's the thing that every exile community does — not out of resistance, not out of ideology, but out of the brute animal need to see the thing you grew up seeing when you looked up. The Irish in Boston built churches that looked like the churches back home. The Japanese in Hawaii planted gardens in the old style. The Nox built a sky because the sky is the first thing you notice is missing and the last thing you stop missing and eleven generations is not long enough to stop missing it.

The homesickness reading is worse than the defiance reading. Defiance has dignity. Defiance is a choice. Homesickness is a wound that organizes itself into architecture, and the architecture is beautiful, and the beauty is the wound, and the Nox maintained it for nine hundred years between enchantment cycles because letting the sky go dark would mean admitting that the surface is gone and the exile is permanent and the stars they're looking at are the stars they made and not the stars they lost.

Four technicians by generation four. Thirty-three in generation three.

The knowledge is dying. The sky will go dark eventually. Not because someone destroys it — because the people who know how to maintain it are fewer each generation, and nobody is training replacements at the rate required, and the enchantment lifespan is generous but not infinite. The false sky is a library that's losing its librarians. The stars will go out one at a time and the last Nox to notice will be the one who can't remember which stars were enchanted and which were always dark.

Ranni's ending — the Age of Stars — takes the Elden Ring off-world entirely. She departs into the actual sky, the real one, the one the Nox couldn't reach. The Nox prophecy said a Lord of Night would bring the Age of Stars. They waited underground, under a false sky, for someone to give them back the real one.

Nobody gave them back the real one. Someone left with it.
