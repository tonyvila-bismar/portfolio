The Great Caravan

by Tess Jyanssen

The merchant had been sitting at the crossroads since before the Shattering, which is to say: longer than anyone alive could verify and shorter than anyone dead would bother disputing. His donkey was tied to the same post it had been tied to the last time the road was safe enough for a caravan. The donkey had opinions about this. The merchant did not ask the donkey's opinions, because he already knew them, and also because the donkey was dead. It had been dead for some time. It stood where it stood because the Erdtree's grace kept everything in the Lands Between in a state of approximate non-decay, and the merchant had not untied it because untying it would mean admitting the journey was over.

His name was Kalpé. He sold weapons, mostly. Small ones: daggers, throwing knives, a few shortswords of indifferent quality, and one truly beautiful estoc that he kept behind the counter of his pack and never showed to anyone who asked about it. The estoc had been his grandfather's. His grandfather had carried it in the Great Caravan, when there had been a Great Caravan, when the roads between the lands had been full of his people and their carts and their song and the particular sound of twelve hundred donkeys all braying at once because one of them had seen a snake.

(The snake is always the problem. Not the brigands, not the weather, not the collapsed bridge or the flooded ford. The snake. One snake, and the whole caravan stops, and you lose an hour, and losing an hour means you miss the gate at Leyndell, and missing the gate means you camp outside the walls, and camping outside the walls means the Cuckoo soldiers come by at midnight to collect their unofficial tariff. Every merchant who has ever run a caravan knows this. The snake is a five-percent surcharge on your entire route, and there is no way to insure against it.)

Kalpé did not think about the Great Caravan. He thought about it constantly.

Here is what happened. You already know what happened if you have been underground, if you have followed the roots of the Erdtree to where they tangle with something older, if you have seen the mad flame dancing in the dark below Leyndell. But most people have not been underground. Most people know only this: the merchants used to be everywhere, and then they were not.

The Golden Order declared the Great Caravan heretical. The charges were specific and absurd, the way charges always are when the actual crime is existing outside a system that does not have a category for you. (The Golden Order has two categories: those who serve the Erdtree, and those who are a problem. The merchants served the road. The road is not the Erdtree.) They rounded up the entire caravan. Every family. Every cart. Every donkey, probably, though no one wrote that down. They took them underground and they buried them.

Alive.

And in the dark, with the weight of the earth above them and the roots of a dead tree pressing against their skulls, the buried merchants did what merchants do when they have nothing left to trade. They cursed. They chanted. They called out to whatever was listening. And something

was

listening. Something that burns so hot it melts the boundary between one soul and the next, between despair and fire, between nothing and everything, and the flame of frenzy was born in the space where a people used to be.

Kalpé's grandfather was not in the caravan when they were buried. He was on the road, three days out, carrying the estoc and a pack of cured hides to a buyer in Liurnia. He came back to find his people gone and his home erased, and he did what any rational person does when their entire civilization is annihilated while they're on a business trip: he sat down at the nearest crossroads and kept selling.

His son did the same. Kalpé did the same. Across the Lands Between, the survivors did the same: set up at a crossroads, spread their wares, sold what they had. They did not organize. They did not resist. They did not seek revenge or restitution or even acknowledgment. They sold things to whoever stopped, including soldiers of the Golden Order, including Tarnished, including the kind of person who would walk up to a merchant and kill him for the bell he carried so they could ring it at the Roundtable Hold and buy from a ghost what they could no longer buy from a man.

People do kill them. Kalpé has watched it happen to others and has expected it himself. The Tarnished are not all heroes. Some of them are simply hungry, or cruel, or in a hurry, and a merchant alone at a crossroads with no guard and no weapon visible is an easy transaction. The merchant dies. The Tarnished takes the bell. The Tarnished moves on. Somewhere in a hold that exists outside the real world, a pair of cloaked women ring the bells and the dead merchant's inventory reappears, purchasable at the same prices, as though the merchant were still alive and had simply moved to a more convenient location.

This is the worst part. Not the killing. The continuation. The prices don't change. The inventory doesn't change. The wares pass from the living merchant to his bell to the ghost merchant in the hold, and the customer cannot tell the difference, and the customer does not care, and the system continues as though the person who gathered and priced and transported those goods was a detail, an intermediary, a cost of doing business that could be optimized away by a bell and a prayer.

Kalpé thought about this at night, when the roads were empty and the grace-light flickered and his dead donkey stood beside him like a monument to patience. He thought about his grandfather and the estoc and the Great Caravan and the sound of twelve hundred donkeys.

He thought about the flame.

He had been underground once. Not far. Just to the edge of the Subterranean Shunning-Grounds, where the pipes from Leyndell's sewers emptied into a darkness that smelled of old water and something else, something hotter. He had gone looking for his people. Not their bodies. Their inventory. Somewhere in the dark, buried with the caravan, were six generations of accumulated goods: fabrics, spices, weapons, tools, the particular kind of useful junk that a trading people acquires over centuries of being the only ones willing to carry things from one place to another. Someone should recover it. Someone should sell it. That was what the goods were for.

He did not find the inventory. He found a door that was warm to the touch, and behind the door he heard something that was not a voice and not a fire and not a song but was somehow all three, and it said a thing that he understood perfectly even though it was not in any language he knew. It said: everything that was done to you can be undone. Everything that was taken can be returned. All it costs is everything else.

Kalpé understood the offer. He was a merchant. He understood all offers. And he understood this one the way he understood the price of cured hide in Liurnia versus the price in Caelid: clearly, immediately, and without sentiment.

He turned around.

He went back to his crossroads. He untied the donkey. He laid the body down and covered it with a saddle blanket, which is not a burial but is what he had. He put the estoc on the counter where anyone could see it, because his grandfather would not have wanted it hidden, and his grandfather would not have wanted the flame, and his grandfather would not have wanted any of this, but his grandfather was dead and Kalpé was alive and the difference between those two things was the whole point.

A Tarnished came by at noon. Young. Confused. Just arrived in the Lands Between, apparently, still blinking in the grace-light like someone who had expected death to be darker. The Tarnished looked at the wares. Looked at the merchant. Looked at the dead donkey under the blanket.

"Are you all right?" the Tarnished asked.

This had never happened before. No one had ever asked. Not once, in however many years Kalpé had been sitting at this crossroads selling weapons to people who might kill him with them.

"I'm a merchant," Kalpé said, which was not an answer.

The Tarnished bought a dagger. Standard quality. Fair price. They paid in runes and did not haggle, which meant they were either very rich or very new. (Very new, Kalpé decided. The rich ones always haggle. It's how they got rich.)

The Tarnished left. Kalpé wrapped the runes and put them in his lockbox. The crossroads was quiet. The grace-light flickered. Somewhere far below the earth, the flame was still burning, still offering, still waiting for him to accept a price he had already declined.

His chapeau was decorated with tiny gems in a wide spectrum of colors. Every merchant of the Great Caravan wore one. The gems had no monetary value. They were simply colors: the colors of the goods they had carried, the roads they had traveled, the places they had been. Red for Caelid spice. Blue for Liurnian glintstone. Green for the Altus grain they hauled in autumn. Gold for the capital, where they were welcome until they were not.

He adjusted the hat. The gems caught the light.

He waited for the next customer.
