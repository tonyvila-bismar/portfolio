Tolerances

by Eitan

TOLERANCES

They sent me to fix the gate.

I should be more specific. In my trade, precision matters. They sent me to fix the portcullis mechanism at the outer curtain wall of a fortress called Stormveil, which I'd never heard of, in a province called Limgrave, which I'd also never heard of, on a continent I had been dead too long to remember. The work order said "gate, stuck." Two words. I have built reductor gear assemblies that required less interpretation.

I am, or was, a military engineer. Walls, gates, siege engines, the rare aqueduct when peacetime threatened to make me redundant. I graduated third in my class from — well. It doesn't exist anymore, the school. I'm told the building is still there, but the instructors are skeletons and the students are worse. I trained under a man named Velstadt, who could calculate the compressive strength of limestone in his head while being shot at. He's dead now. Properly dead, I mean. Not the kind of dead I am.

I should explain about the dead thing.

When I woke up — and I use the word loosely, since what happened was closer to being dragged sideways through a hole in a wet blanket — I was lying on a stone floor in a round room with no doors. There was a two-fingered hand the size of a horse trough mounted on a plinth, and it was twitching. A woman who looked like she'd been folded up and stored in a cupboard for several decades told me I had been granted grace, which apparently meant I was now expected to cross a sea, fight my way through several technically immortal demigods, and sit on a big chair. I said that sounded like a lot of work. She said I could also fix things. Buildings, walls, mechanisms.

Right, I said. Where do I start.

The problem with being resurrected by a cosmic force that communicates through a giant twitching hand is that the orientation process is, to put it charitably, lacking. No one tells you the rules. You work them out by dying — which you can do, repeatedly, without it sticking, which is convenient for the learning process and devastating for your nerves. The first time a soldier put a halberd through my chest outside Stormveil's front gate, I woke up at a little campfire about a hundred yards back with a mouth full of ash and a revised understanding of local hospitality.

The second time, I went around to the side.

Stormveil is — I should talk about Stormveil. I'm an engineer. I notice things about buildings the way a physician notices things about limps, which is to say compulsively and often at inappropriate moments. And what I noticed about Stormveil was that it had been built by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and then modified by someone who absolutely did not.

The original construction is superb. Granite ashlar, tight-jointed, with a batter on the lower courses that would shed any ram you cared to throw at it. The curtain wall follows the contour of the cliff in a way that eliminates dead ground — no spot where an attacker could shelter from the defenders above. The towers are positioned at intervals calculated for overlapping fields of fire, assuming crossbows with a draw weight of about three hundred pounds, which tells you something about the era. The whole thing is a textbook, if your textbook was written by someone who expected to be attacked by professionals and wanted them to die tired.

The modifications, though.

Someone — and I have a pretty good idea who, based on the general aesthetic of "what if we nailed more arms to it" — had punched crude openings through load-bearing walls to install wooden platforms for archers. Wooden. In a stone fortress. I found three separate places where structural buttresses had been hacked away to make room for, as far as I could tell, cages. There were cages everywhere. Some had things in them. I tried not to look.

The grafting, they call it. The lord of this place — Godrick, a name that sounds like it should belong to a cheerful innkeeper but in fact belongs to something I am not going to describe because I was eating when I first saw him and I'd like to be able to eat again — has a habit of collecting limbs from his enemies and attaching them to himself and his soldiers. He also, apparently, collects limbs from his own soldiers and attaches them to his enemies' corpses and then reanimates the corpses, because at some point the man's design philosophy departed from military necessity and entered a territory I'd describe as recreational.

The grafted soldiers are why they need the portcullis fixed, actually. Several of them got jammed in it. Not stuck as in "the mechanism seized." Stuck as in "there are eight additional arms where there should be two and the extra elbows caught in the guide channels." The previous engineer — he was alive, in the old-fashioned sense — took one look at the situation and walked off the cliff. Voluntarily. I watched him do it. He didn't even leave a note. Just set down his plumb line, looked at the gearbox full of fingers, and stepped backward into open air.

I understood completely. But I can't die, so I got on with it.

Here is the thing about a portcullis. The mechanism is simple. Ordinary people think of it as a heavy gate that goes up and down, and they're right, but the engineering is in the counterweight. A good portcullis weighs between six hundred and fifteen hundred pounds. You could lift it with brute force — there are windlass systems for that — but the elegant solution, the one that lets two men operate a gate that could crush a horse, is the counterweight. You calculate the weight of the gate, subtract a small margin for friction (and it has to be small; too much and the gate drifts, too little and you need four men instead of two), and you hang the appropriate mass on the other end of a chain that runs over a pulley set into the gatehouse ceiling.

The counterweight at Stormveil was limestone. Good choice. Dense, workable, not prone to spalling. Someone who knew their business had cut it into a neat rectangular block, drilled a channel through the center for the chain, and secured it with an iron pin. Elegant. Professional. The kind of work Velstadt would have approved of without comment, which from Velstadt was the highest praise available.

The problem was that the limestone block had been removed and replaced with a wicker basket full of severed hands.

I stood there for a while, looking at the basket. The hands were in varying states of decay. Some had been there long enough to mummify. Others were — fresher. The basket itself was coming apart. Wicker has a design life of maybe three years under load, less in damp conditions, and the gatehouse was very damp. One of the hands had six fingers. Two of them were wearing rings.

The weight was wrong, of course. A basket of hands weighs roughly nothing compared to a limestone counterweight. The gate had dropped and stuck because there was nothing on the other end to hold it, and then the grafted soldiers had gotten tangled in it, and then additional hands had been added to the basket, possibly as an offering, possibly as a joke, possibly because whoever was in charge of corpse disposal had run out of ideas and the basket was right there.

I removed the hands. I found the original counterweight in a storage room on the third floor, being used as a bench. I cleaned the chain, re-greased the pulley, and spent an afternoon with a cold chisel widening the guide channels by a quarter inch on each side to accommodate — well. Wider things. It's not a permanent solution. The channels will wear unevenly. But the gate works now, and wider channels mean the next time someone with supplementary elbows gets caught, they'll slide through instead of jamming.

I submitted an invoice.

(I should mention: I have no idea who I'm submitting invoices to. There is a functionary at the Roundtable Hold who deals with supply requisitions and compensation. I've never met him. I leave the paperwork in a drawer that exists in a room that may not be physically real, and somehow, chop silver appears in my pouch. I don't ask questions. I learned long ago that asking questions about the financial mechanisms of divine institutions is the fastest way to stop getting paid.)

After the gate, they sent me to the walls.

Stormveil's walls have a problem that no amount of engineering can solve, which is that they are being hit by a continuous gale that comes from somewhere inside the castle itself. Not wind — wind is weather. This is something else. The gusts carry the sound of screaming, if you listen hard enough, and they don't follow any pattern I can predict. The effect on the masonry is catastrophic. You know what kills a stone wall? Not battering rams. Not trebuchets. Water and thermal cycling. Water gets into the joints, freezes, expands. The ice pushes the stones apart by thousandths of an inch per cycle, and you get a thousand cycles per winter, and in ten years the wall that could stop an army is a pile of rubble that couldn't stop a goat.

This wind does the same thing, but wrong. The stones don't move apart uniformly. They move in directions that shouldn't be possible. I found a lintel stone that had shifted a full half-inch to the north while the stones on either side of it had shifted south. That's not erosion. That's not settlement. I don't know what it is. I repointed the joints with a lime mortar — two parts sand, one part lime putty, a pinch ofiteiteite. Sorry. Old habit. Two parts sand, one part lime putty, pozzolanic ash if I can find it. The ash reacts with the lime and produces a calcium silicate hydrate that's significantly stronger than plain mortar, and more importantly, it's flexible. It gives. A rigid mortar in a wall that's moving in impossible directions will crack in a season. A flexible one will — well, it'll crack too, but it'll take longer. I'm buying time. That's what engineers do.

There's a girl at the castle.

Not a girl — a woman. Young. She has a red hood and a look about her like a dog that's been kicked so many times it's forgotten there was ever a time before the kicking. She was sent here by someone, as some kind of punishment or exile, and she ended up talking to the dead — or the grafted, which isn't quite the same thing but isn't entirely different either. She can hear them in the walls. The ones Godrick used for parts. She told me she can hear them crying, and I told her I'd been hearing the walls crying for a week and assumed it was the wind.

She looked at me like I was the stupidest man she'd ever met, which is a look I'm accustomed to.

Her name is Roderika. She told me she has no gift, no purpose, no idea why grace bothered to notice someone like her. I told her I fix gates. She said that sounded useful, at least.

(I think, sometimes, about Velstadt. He had a saying — "Tolerances are what separate engineering from hope." Meaning: you calculate the acceptable range of deviation, you build to stay within it, and you don't pray. Hope is what you have when you haven't done the math. I've been thinking about that a lot since I got here, because the math in the Lands Between doesn't work. Not in the way it should. I ran the numbers on the main keep's foundation — the settlement pattern, the load distribution, the soil bearing capacity — and the building should have collapsed four hundred years ago. It hasn't. Something else is holding it up. Not engineering. Not physics. Something.)

After Stormveil, they sent me south.

Castle Morne, on the Weeping Peninsula. There'd been a revolt. The Misbegotten — that's what they call them, and I'd object to the name if anyone here cared what an engineer thought about nomenclature — had risen up and killed most of the garrison. There was a man named Edgar who was in charge, or had been in charge, or thought he was in charge. He had a daughter. He had a letter. He had a look in his eye like a wall under shear stress, which is to say he was about to break and didn't know it.

The castle's engineering was poor. Coastal fortification, salt air, and whoever specified the iron fittings had used wrought iron instead of bronze. Every hinge, every bracket, every pin was red with rust. I could have fixed it in a week. Instead, I was asked to assess the structural damage from the revolt.

There wasn't any. Not structural.

The Misbegotten had killed people, not walls. The castle was fine. Every joint tight, every arch sound. The damage was all on the inside — of the people, I mean, not the building. Edgar's daughter was dead by the time I got there. He'd left to receive her letter, and when he came back she was lying at the bridge. I never saw the body. I was told about it by a soldier who told it flatly, the way you describe weather.

I surveyed the castle anyway. Wrote up a report. Specified bronze replacement fittings for all exterior ironwork, a new lime wash for the seaward face, and repairs to the postern gate where the Misbegotten had forced entry. The postern was the only real failure, and it was a design flaw, not a maintenance issue — the door opened inward, which meant the defenders couldn't barricade it from behind. Whoever designed it had assumed the enemy would always be outside.

Nobody read the report.

I went back north. There was a church along the road — one of the old ones, built to collect the golden sap that used to drip from the Erdtree's boughs. Lovely construction. Dressed stone, perfectly proportioned, with an apse that showed real understanding of how to distribute the thrust of a vault. The sap basins were dry. They'd been dry for a long time. Someone had placed flowers in them, which struck me as the kind of gesture that means everything and accomplishes nothing.

There was a turtle in the church. A very large turtle. I mention this because the turtle was the clergyman, which is the sort of thing you accept without comment in the Lands Between because the alternative is standing in a church arguing theology with a reptile, and I had work to do.

The turtle — Miriel, his name was — told me that all knowledge has value. I said that was a nice sentiment. He said he meant it literally: knowledge has a material value, a weight, a presence in the world, and when it is destroyed, something is physically lost that can never be recovered. I said I understood. He asked what I did for a living, and I said I was an engineer, and he said, "Then you understand better than most. You build things that hold other things up. What happens when the thing being held up is no longer there?"

I said, "The supports are still doing their job. They don't know the difference."

He looked at me for a long time. Turtles are good at looking at you for a long time.

I've been thinking about what he said.

I fix things. That is what I do. I am adequate at it — not brilliant, not Velstadt, but adequate. Third in my class, which means I understand the theory and can execute the practice and will never be the one they call when something truly impossible needs doing. I am the one they call when something merely difficult needs doing competently, on time, and under budget. This is not false modesty. I have a clear-eyed assessment of my abilities, which is itself one of my abilities. Velstadt used to say the most dangerous engineer is the one who thinks he's an artist. An artist takes risks. An engineer calculates them.

(Velstadt is dead. I keep saying that. I keep saying it because it keeps being true, and because in a world where death is a temporary inconvenience, the word "dead" has lost its tolerances. It can mean anything now. It can mean sleeping. It can mean changed. It can mean wandering around a swamp eating corpses because a woman with a rotting arm decided to bloom. Velstadt, though — Velstadt is dead in the old way. The way that sticks. I know this because I buried him myself, before I died, in a time I cannot fully remember. I put his plumb line in with him. It seemed right.)

They sent me to the capital.

Leyndell. The seat of the Golden Order. The most important city in the Lands Between, the one built at the base of the great golden tree that I'm told is actually dead but looks very convincing.

The engineering in Leyndell is magnificent. I'm not using the word loosely. The aqueducts alone — there are seventeen still-functional aqueducts feeding the capital, designed with a gradient of precisely one-in-four-hundred, which over a span of six miles produces exactly the right flow rate for a city of that population. The keystones in the main processional avenue are each cut to a different angle, compensating for the curvature of the road, so that the loads distribute evenly despite the grade change. The drainage system under the plaza can handle rainfall equivalent to — look. I could talk about this for hours. The point is that someone, or many someones, over many centuries, built a city that works.

And it's full of ash.

There's ash in the streets. In the houses. In the fine, beautiful joints of the perfectly proportioned walls. The citizens sealed their doors with yellow wax, which tells you they knew it was coming. The soldiers sit at their posts like men waiting for a war that already happened. The tree — the great golden tree, the one the whole city was built to honor — is a projection. A ghost of a tree. The real one burned, and what's left is light pretending to be wood.

I know what happens when you remove the thing a structure was built to support. The structure doesn't know. It keeps doing its job. The arches keep arching. The buttresses keep buttressing. The counterweight keeps counterweighting, even when the gate it's supposed to lift has been ripped off its hinges and thrown into a ditch.

I repointed some walls. I fixed a drainage grate that had silted up with ash. I replaced a sheared pin in a windlass mechanism that nobody was using because there was nothing left to lift. Good work. Competent work. Third-in-class work.

Nobody noticed.

There's a smith at the Roundtable Hold. Hewg, his name is. He's chained there. I don't know who chained him or why, and he doesn't remember either, which is the kind of thing you'd think would bother a person more than it seems to bother him. His job is to forge a weapon capable of killing a god, and he does this job with the quiet focus of a man who has been doing it for so long that the question of why no longer interests him.

I watched him work once. His hammer technique is flawless — consistent stroke weight, consistent angle, the kind of muscle memory that takes decades to develop. He doesn't waste heat. He doesn't waste steel. Every strike is where it needs to be and no more.

I asked him how he knew when a blade was done. He said, "When it holds an edge."

I said, "That's a test, not an answer."

He said, "It's the only answer I have."

I think about Hewg a lot. I think about the fact that he's building a weapon to kill something divine, and he doesn't know why, and he doesn't stop. I think about Velstadt's saying, about tolerances. The acceptable range of deviation. The margin between "this works" and "this doesn't." Hewg's margin is razor-thin — literally — and he maintains it without understanding what it's for.

I have been fixing things in the Lands Between for — I don't know how long. Time doesn't behave here. I've repaired gates, walls, bridges, drainage systems, one trebuchet that someone had assembled backward (the sling was on the wrong end; I don't want to talk about it), and the mechanism for a lift that descends into a vast underground cavern lit by a false sky, which I also don't want to talk about because the cavern has no business existing and thinking about the engineering implications of a false sky gives me a headache.

Nothing I fix stays fixed.

That's not self-pity. It's a structural observation. The walls I repoint crack again within the season. The gates I repair jam with new atrocities. The drainage systems silt up with ash from a tree that already burned. I am maintaining a world that is, in every measurable sense, broken beyond my ability to repair, and I keep doing it because it's what I do.

Velstadt would say I'm operating outside my tolerances. He'd be right. The acceptable range of deviation between "functional" and "irreparable" is a margin I passed a long time ago, and I keep working because the alternative is stepping off a cliff like that engineer at Stormveil, except I'd wake up at a campfire with a mouth full of ash and have to walk all the way back up.

Roderika, the girl with the red hood — she found her gift. Spirit tuning, they call it. She talks to the dead, or the echoes of the dead, or whatever it is that remains when a soul has been pulled apart and stitched into a glowing shape that follows you around and fights for you. She's good at it. She's better than good. She went from a girl who couldn't stop shaking to a woman who handles the fragments of destroyed lives with a steadiness I envy.

I asked her once if the spirits know they're dead.

She said, "Do you?"

I didn't have an answer. I still don't.

Yesterday — if yesterday means anything — I was repointing a wall in a chapel east of the capital. Small job. One of the lintels had cracked, a clean fracture along the bedding plane, the kind that happens when the stone was laid with the grain running the wrong direction. A common mistake. Easily fixed: you chisel out the broken stone, dress a new one with the grain running perpendicular to the load, set it with your flexible mortar, and move on.

I chiseled out the stone. Behind it, in the cavity between the inner and outer wall faces, there was a scroll. Vellum, good quality, rolled tight and sealed with wax. I opened it because I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, and engineers are constitutionally incapable of finding something inside a wall and not looking at it.

It was a building specification. For the chapel I was standing in. Dated — well, the dating system means nothing to me, but the notation style was old. Very old. The spec called for dressed granite, lime mortar with pozzolanic ash, iron cramps set in lead, and a note in the margin in handwriting I recognized.

Velstadt's handwriting.

He'd written: "Lintel over east window — ensure grain perpendicular to load. Previous stone cracked along bedding plane. Replacement spec: same quarry, same bed, rotated 90 degrees. Note for future engineers: this stone will crack again in approximately 400 years. I have done the math."

He had done the math. Four hundred years, give or take the tolerances, and here I was with a chisel in my hand and a cracked lintel and a dead man's instructions.

I dressed the new stone. I set the grain perpendicular to the load. I sealed the scroll back in the wall with the mortar, because whoever comes after me in four hundred years will need it, and I wrote nothing in the margin, because Velstadt had already said everything there was to say.

The stone will crack again. I've done the math.

That is, I think, what the turtle was asking me. What happens when the thing being held up is no longer there? The supports keep working. They keep doing their job. They don't know the difference.

I submitted my invoice. I picked up my tools. The golden light that is apparently grace pointed me north, toward something I'm told is important, and I went south, because there was a report of a subsided foundation in the lake country and someone had to look at it.

The foundation was fine, as it turned out. The building had sunk three inches into saturated clay, which is not subsidence but differential settlement caused by an inadequate footing width for the soil conditions. I specified wider footings, a gravel drainage layer, and a revised load path that distributed the weight more evenly across the bearing stratum.

Nobody will build it. I know that. I write the specifications anyway.

Tolerances are what separate engineering from hope. I have been doing the math for a long time, and the math says none of this will hold. The walls will crack. The gates will jam. The tree is dead and the city is ash and the world is broken along a fracture that runs deeper than any foundation I know how to dig.

But the mortar is flexible, and the grain is perpendicular to the load, and the scroll is in the wall for whoever comes next.

That's not hope. It's engineering.

I think.
