The Albinauric Question

by Kontextmaschine

[reblogging myself from "The Stonemason"]

I mentioned the Albinaurics in passing last time and I want to come back to them because there's a question sitting in the middle of their existence that nobody is asking and it's driving me insane.

Who commissioned them? Who signed the purchase order? Who wanted this?

The Albinaurics are an artificial race. Created by human hands. This is not ambiguous — the game is explicit. Someone, at some point, in some workshop or laboratory or Academy annex, decided to make people. Not golems. Not automata. Not constructs with a limited behavioral repertoire. People. With consciousness, with preferences, with the capacity for suffering and community and hope, with legs that work for a while and then don't.

The first generation was competent work. Functional bodies, real cognition, genuine personhood by any meaningful standard. One design flaw: the legs fail. Progressive mobility loss, inevitable, built into the architecture. The second generation was a rush job — cruder, less differentiated, used as disposable labor from the start. This is not a mystery. This is a production timeline. First gen is the prototype. Second gen is the cost-reduced mass-market version. Someone was iterating.

For what? What was the use case that justified building a species?

The Golden Order rejected them. "Not born of the Erdtree" — the theological stamp that means "doesn't count." But the Golden Order didn't commission them either. The Albinaurics predate the Order's interest in classifying them. Someone made them, and that someone had a use case in mind, and the use case was important enough to justify the R&D costs of creating an entire sapient species, and then — what? The commissioner lost interest? The project was defunded? The Albinaurics outlived their original purpose and the purpose was never recorded?

This is the pattern that gets me. Not the oppression — oppression of artificial beings by their creators is the oldest science fiction story there is and I'm bored of it. What gets me is the

paperwork gap

. Someone funded this. Someone approved the budget. Someone signed off on "we are going to create a self-reproducing sapient labor force with a planned obsolescence feature built into the locomotor system" and that person's name is nowhere. The Albinaurics exist. The purchase order doesn't.

An Albinauric woman in the Liurnia marshes, after the Erdtree stopped working: "The Erdtree told us we weren't people and we believed it for a hundred years." In a world where grace is a visible physical phenomenon — golden light you can see, pointing you toward sites of power — the Albinaurics' exclusion from grace was empirically verifiable. They weren't rejected by a theology. They were rejected by a physics. And they believed it, the way anyone believes physics, until the physics broke.

The legs are the thing I can't get past. Planned obsolescence in a sapient species. First gen Albinaurics have bodies that work and then stop working — not from disease, not from aging, from

design

. Whoever made them built in the expiration date. Built it into the legs specifically, which means they wanted beings that could work and then couldn't, that could move and then couldn't, that would become dependent at a predictable interval.

That's not a flaw. That's a feature. That's a loyalty mechanism built into the chassis.

And nobody remembers who wanted loyal.
