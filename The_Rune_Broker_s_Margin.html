The Rune Broker's Margin

by Bartuk Codex

In the years before the Shattering, there had been no need for a rune broker in the Lands Between. Runes moved as water moves — downhill, toward the lowest point, which in an economy governed by the Erdtree meant toward the roots. The faithful earned runes through labor and devotion. The runes flowed to the Erdtree through tithe and burial. The Erdtree converted death into gold and gold into life and the cycle was, if not fair, at least legible. A farmer could tell you what a season's work was worth. A soldier could tell you the bounty on a troll. The prices were stable because the currency was theological, and theology does not inflate.

Then the Elden Ring broke, and the prices went with it.

Sellen had not intended to become a broker. She had intended to become a sorceress, which she did, and then she had intended to become a great sorceress, which she did not, owing to a disagreement with the Academy of Raya Lucaria regarding the use of certain stones that the Academy considered impermissible and that Sellen considered essential. The details of the disagreement are not relevant here except insofar as they left Sellen expelled, itinerant, and in possession of a set of skills that no institution would pay her to use and that she could not afford to stop using.

She ended up in a cellar in Limgrave, teaching sorcery to Tarnished who passed through. The teaching was adequate. The compensation was interesting.

Tarnished paid in runes. This was the only currency they had, being dead people who had been resurrected without wallets. But the runes they paid were not uniform. A rune pulled from a soldier was worth less than a rune pulled from a knight. A rune from a knight was worth less than one from a grafted scion. The denomination was variable, the supply was unpredictable, and the demand — from Sellen's perspective — was constant, because glintstone doesn't eat but scholars do, and the merchants who sold provisions did not accept theoretical knowledge as payment.

Sellen began keeping a ledger.

This was the moment, though she did not know it, when she stopped being a teacher and became a broker.

The ledger began as a practical tool — tracking which Tarnished owed what for which lessons — but within three months it had become something else entirely. She was recording exchange rates. A Golden Rune [1] purchased two Kukri from the nomadic merchant on the ridge. The same rune purchased four Throwing Daggers from the merchant at the church.

The discrepancy was instructive.

The Kukri merchant valued smithing stones at a rate that implied he believed the mines were still operating. The church merchant priced his inventory as though resupply was impossible. Their prices diverged because their beliefs about the world diverged, and neither had enough information to correct the other, because nobody had enough information about anything.

Sellen, who had spent years observing how the Academy priced knowledge — tuition in glintstone keys, research access in faculty loyalty, tenure in political alignment — recognized what she was looking at. It was not chaos. It was a market without a clearing mechanism.

In the old economy, the Erdtree had been the clearing mechanism. Every rune eventually returned to the tree. Every death fed the cycle. Prices were stable because the supply was circular: runes came from the tree, flowed through the living, returned to the tree through death, and emerged again as golden blessing. The Erdtree was, in financial terms, both the central bank and the mint, and also the primary depositor, and also the regulator, and also the ground on which the economy stood, which is a level of vertical integration that would concern anyone who thought about it carefully, but nobody had thought about it carefully because the tree was God and questioning God's monetary policy was not a recognized field of study.

The tree was still standing. But the ring was broken, and the cycle had cracked.

Sellen noticed two things. The first was that runes were leaking. When a Tarnished died — which happened often — they dropped their accumulated runes at the site of death. If they returned to collect them, fine. If they died again before returning, the runes vanished. Not redistributed. Not absorbed. Gone. Every double death was a deflationary event, a small withdrawal from a money supply that nobody was replenishing. The Erdtree was no longer cycling runes back into the economy because the Erdtree was, despite appearances, no longer fully operational. It glowed. It stood. It did not function.

The second thing she noticed was that rune generation had shifted from the tree to the Tarnished themselves. A Tarnished who killed an enemy extracted runes directly. Not from the tree. From the enemy. This was, in the old theology, impossible — runes were a divine resource, channeled through the Erdtree, not manufactured by violence. But the theology had shattered along with the ring, and the new reality was that anyone with a sword and sufficient determination could produce currency.

The implications were significant.

In the old economy, violence produced nothing. A soldier who killed a bandit earned a wage from his lord, not from the bandit's body. The runes in the bandit's soul returned to the Erdtree through the death cycle, and the Erdtree distributed them according to its own priorities.

Those priorities were opaque, non-negotiable, and widely believed to be just, because justice and opacity are not contradictions when the arbiter is God.

That system was gone.

A Tarnished who farmed soldiers near the Gatefront — and there were several who did, methodically, returning to the same patrol routes as the soldiers respawned in their half-dead loops — was, functionally, operating a mint. The soldiers were the raw material. Death was the manufacturing process. Runes were the output. The Tarnished was not earning wealth. They were creating it, from nothing, through violence, which is how most currencies begin if you look at the history honestly, but which is not how most currencies describe themselves.

Sellen understood that she was sitting at a junction. Tarnished came through Limgrave on their way to everywhere. They all needed sorcery lessons, weapon upgrades, information, supplies. They all paid in runes.

And the runes were not all worth the same thing, because a rune pulled from a Stormveil knight represented a different extraction cost than a rune farmed from a Limgrave footsoldier, even if the nominal value was identical.

She started quoting prices by source.

This was the innovation, though it seemed obvious in retrospect (the best innovations always do). A Tarnished who brought runes from Caelid — where the enemies were stronger, the environment was hostile, and the extraction cost was high — was bringing runes of higher real value than one who had been farming the soldiers at the Gatefront for a week. Not because the runes were different. Because the information was different. Caelid runes told Sellen something: their bearer had survived Caelid. Gatefront runes told her something else: their bearer had not left Limgrave.

She was not pricing runes. She was pricing risk.

The Tarnished did not understand this. They saw a sorceress in a cellar quoting different prices to different people and assumed she was being arbitrary. Some were angry. Some bargained. One, a particularly persistent warrior who had spent two weeks farming the same twelve soldiers in a loop and could not understand why his six thousand runes purchased fewer spells than another Tarnished's four thousand, accused her of cheating.

Sellen explained. She explained calmly, precisely, and without condescension, because she had learned at the Academy that condescension was expensive — it felt good and cost allies, which is a trade that only a tenured professor can afford, and she was no longer tenured. She told the warrior that his runes, while real, were denominated in a currency whose backing had changed. The gold was gold. But the tree that made the gold meaningful was not doing what it used to do, and pretending otherwise was the kind of optimism that got people killed.

The warrior did not understand. He paid her price anyway, because he needed the spells, and need is the only market force that requires no explanation.

There was another customer she remembered better. A merchant — one of the wandering kind, with a donkey and a pack too large for the donkey and a bell that he rang at crossroads to announce his presence. His name was Halve, or that was what he told her, and he came to the cellar not for spells but for information.

He wanted to know what Tarnished were buying.

Sellen told him. Smithing stones, bolts, throwing knives, the occasional crafting material. He nodded as though confirming figures he already had. Then he asked what they were paying, and she told him that too, and he sat quietly for a moment and did a calculation that Sellen could not follow because it involved supply chains she did not have visibility into.

"The bolts are mispriced," he said.

"By how much?"

"By about three hundred percent, depending on where you buy them."

Sellen asked him why he didn't arbitrage the difference — buy low at the church, sell high at the coast. Halve looked at her as though she had suggested he juggle knives. He explained, patiently, that the merchant clans of the Lands Between had been buried alive in a catacomb and cursed, and that the survivors traded under conditions that included a non-trivial risk of being killed by the same Tarnished they were selling to, and that arbitrage requires you to survive the round trip, which in his profession was not guaranteed.

He bought a scroll of Glintstone Pebble. Not because he intended to cast it. Because a Tarnished had told him there was demand for scrolls in the Altus Plateau, and Halve understood that in a broken economy, the thing you sell matters less than whether you can carry it without dying.

Sellen never saw him again. She noted his prices in the ledger. His exchange rates were better than anyone else's — tighter spreads, less variance, closer to what she would have calculated as the theoretical fair price. She wondered, later, whether merchants were the only real economists in the Lands Between, and whether that was why the Golden Order had buried them.

Over the following months, Sellen's cellar became something the Lands Between had not had since the Shattering: a price discovery mechanism. She did not set out to build one. She set out to not starve. But the ledger grew, and the patterns in it became legible, and the patterns said something that she found increasingly difficult to ignore.

The economy was draining.

Not collapsing — draining. Slowly, steadily, the way a reservoir drains when the inflow drops below the outflow but the surface level doesn't change fast enough for anyone to notice until the pumps start sucking air. Runes were being created by Tarnished killing enemies, yes. But runes were also being destroyed every time a Tarnished died twice. And the runes locked inside the demigods — the Great Runes, the massive concentrations of value sealed in bodies that were neither alive nor dead — represented a quantity of wealth so large that its absence from circulation was, by itself, deflationary.

Sellen did the arithmetic. She did it on the back of a page from a sorcery manual, because she did not have proper paper, and because the irony of calculating the collapse of a divine economy on the margins of a textbook about manipulating cosmic forces was not lost on her, though she did not find it as amusing as she might have in better circumstances.

The Great Runes held, by her estimate, roughly forty percent of the total rune supply. Locked. Inert. Sitting inside creatures who would not die and could not trade and had no use for the wealth they contained except as a kind of gravitational anchor — a mass so large it bent the economic field around it, pulling value toward itself without releasing any.

Forty percent. In a functioning economy, removing forty percent of the money supply would be called a catastrophe. In the Lands Between, it was called the Shattering, and people talked about it as a theological event, which it was, but it was also a bank run in which the largest depositors had absconded with their accounts and barricaded themselves inside their vaults.

Sellen considered the possibility that every Tarnished who set out to kill a demigod and claim a Great Rune was not, as the Two Fingers suggested, fulfilling a divine mandate. They were performing a liquidity operation. Breaking open the vault. Releasing the locked capital back into circulation. The Two Fingers' guidance — kill the demigods, claim the Great Runes, restore the Elden Ring — was, from a purely monetary perspective, a restructuring plan. The theology was the marketing. The mechanism was repo.

She wrote this in her ledger. She did not share it with anyone, because she had learned from the Academy that being right about systems is not the same as being safe, and because the observation, while true, was the kind of truth that makes people want to hurt the person who said it rather than examine the thing it reveals.

She thought about Halve the merchant. About his tighter spreads. About the catacomb.

A man whose people had been buried alive for participating in commerce had better pricing data than anyone in the Academy. This was either an indictment of the Academy or a vindication of the merchants, and Sellen suspected it was both, and that the difference between the two was smaller than either institution would have been comfortable admitting.

The merchants had been cursed. Their prices were still more honest than the Erdtree's. There is probably a lesson in this, though Sellen was not in the business of drawing lessons. She was in the business of drawing margins, and the margins were thinning.

The golden seeds were the detail that kept her awake.

Golden seeds grew on minor Erdtrees throughout the Lands Between. They were real. You could hold them. You could use them to strengthen your sacred flasks. They were, in a meaningful sense, the last functioning output of the Erdtree system — small fruits from an organism whose trunk was a projection and whose roots were rotting.

But the minor Erdtrees were called, in the oldest texts she could find, "illusory trees."

Illusory.

The seeds were real. The trees that produced them were not. The currency of grace — the golden light, the sites of power, the entire infrastructure that the Tarnished relied on to navigate the Lands Between — was rooted in something that the people who named it had already identified as an illusion. Not a lie, necessarily. An illusion is not a lie. An illusion is a real phenomenon that is not what it appears to be.

The light is real. The tree is not.

The seeds are real. The source is not.

Sellen had seen this before. At the Academy, every three years, the faculty voted on research funding allocations. The votes were real. The process that produced the votes — the negotiations, the favors, the threats delivered over wine in faculty lounges — was not what the published minutes described. The minutes were an illusion: a real document that was not what it appeared to be.

Sellen closed the ledger.

She sat in the cellar for a long time. The glintstone on the walls pulsed faintly, which it always did, which she usually found comforting in the way that a heartbeat is comforting — evidence of something alive and functioning in a world where most things were neither.

She thought about the Tarnished who would come tomorrow. There would be at least two, based on the weekly patterns she had recorded. One would want Glintstone Pebble. One would want something more advanced and not have enough runes for it and would try to negotiate. She would hold the price because the price was fair and because holding prices is the only discipline that works when nobody else is holding anything.

She thought about the warrior who had accused her of cheating. She thought about his six thousand runes, farmed from twelve soldiers in a loop, denominated against a currency backed by a dead tree projecting the image of itself into a sky that was, depending on which scholar you asked, either the real sky or another projection. She thought about the price she had charged him and whether it had been fair, and she concluded that it had been, and that fairness in an economy built on illusions is still fairness, the way a contract is still binding even if both parties were wrong about the value of what they traded, because the alternative — the alternative where nothing counts because nothing is real — is not an economy. It is a void, and voids do not produce spells, or bread, or reasons to keep a ledger.

She opened the ledger again. She recorded the day's transactions. She noted the exchange rate between golden runes and smithing stones, which had shifted slightly because a Tarnished had found a new mine somewhere in Liurnia and flooded the local market with stones of a quality that the mines in Limgrave could not match.

The price of smithing stones fell.

The price of sorcery lessons, which Sellen set by consulting nothing except her own judgment of what the market would bear and what her labor was worth, did not change.

She had not thought about the Academy in some time. She had not thought about the expulsion, or the stones they had forbidden her from studying, or the colleagues who had voted to remove her without meeting her eyes. She had not thought about these things because thinking about them was expensive and produced no return, and Sellen, whatever her faults, whatever her heresies, whatever the Academy said about her methods and her ethics and her judgment — Sellen understood costs.

She knew what she had paid. She knew what she had received. The ledger, on this particular transaction, did not balance, and she suspected it never would, and she kept it open anyway, because a ledger that does not balance is still more honest than no ledger at all.
