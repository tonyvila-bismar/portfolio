The Institutional Mechanics Of Becoming Elden Lord

by Kontextmaschine

The Institutional Mechanics of Becoming Elden Lord

Kontextmaschine

So someone in the notes is going "the Roundtable Hold is basically a meritocracy, whoever's strongest gets to be Elden Lord" and like — no. That's exactly the reading the Golden Order wants you to have, and it falls apart the second you look at the actual institutional machinery.

Start with the basic pipeline. You're a Tarnished. You died abroad. Grace gets returned to you — not by anything you did, but because the Two Fingers, who are essentially a bureaucratic intermediary for a cosmic entity that may or may not still be paying attention, flip the switch. Already we're not in meritocracy territory. We're in "you have been selected by a credentialing body whose selection criteria are opaque and whose principal has gone nonresponsive." This is not a tournament. This is a procurement process.

And the Roundtable Hold — the Hold is not a gathering of equals competing on a level field. The Hold is a franchise operation. You've got Hewg, who's literally chained there, forging weapons because someone told him to and he can't remember who. You've got Gideon running intelligence operations and eliminating candidates who get too close to answers he doesn't want found. You've got Ensha, who is basically Gideon's violence made ambulatory, attacking you the second you obtain information about the Haligtree — which is to say, information about an alternative to the existing system. The Hold employed assassins to kill Tarnished who strayed from grace's guidance. This is not a meritocratic arena. This is a managed competition where the managing institution reserves the right to disqualify you with a knife.

The deeper thing — and this is where it gets actually interesting — is that the entire Elden Lord succession system was designed by Marika, and Marika designed it to solve a specific problem that she herself created. She stole divinity through the wrong process. She skipped the death-and-marriage ritual at the Divine Gate. Every subsequent institutional arrangement she builds is an attempt to retroactively legitimate a usurpation, which is a dynamic that shows up constantly in real institutional history (the number of medieval monarchies that spent centuries building legal and theological frameworks to justify a great-grandfather's coup is — it's a lot, and they all converge on suspiciously similar solutions).

So Marika exiles Godfrey. She tells him explicitly: go die, come back, try again. She's not banishing him. She's sending him through the credentialing process she originally skipped. Die abroad. Lose your grace. Get it returned. Cross the Sea of Fog. Collect the shards. Present yourself at the Erdtree. The entire Tarnished system is a manufactured pilgrimage designed to produce a candidate who has fulfilled death's requirement — the requirement Marika herself never fulfilled.

Which means the Roundtable Hold, the guidance of grace, the Two Fingers pointing you toward the next boss — all of it is infrastructure built to process candidates through a specific ritual sequence. It's a pipeline. And like all pipelines, it has dropout points, gatekeepers, and a completion rate that the designers considered acceptable. Most Tarnished fail. That's fine. You only need one to finish.

Vyke almost finished. Vyke was the closest to becoming Elden Lord and then he went down below the capital and got scorched by the Three Fingers, and the game asks whether he chose the Frenzied Flame to save his maiden from self-immolation or whether "some other force lured him with suggestion." Which — this is the part that kills me — this is the system's principal-agent problem made literal. You have a candidate who was performing exactly as the pipeline required, who was about to complete the credentialing process, and at the last gate he either defected because the personal cost was too high (his maiden had to burn) or was actively sabotaged by a competing cosmic entity. Either way, the pipeline failed. The institution could not retain its top candidate.

And then you've got Bernahl, whose maiden threw herself into the fire before he was ready, so he just — stopped. Dropped out. Became a Recusant. The pipeline lost another top candidate because the ritual's human fuel supply — the Finger Maidens, who have to die for the process to work — is not infinitely renewable and has its own agency.

The Finger Maiden situation deserves its own post honestly but the short version is: the succession system requires women to burn themselves alive as a resource input, the system provides no mechanism for informed consent (Melina doesn't explain what "being taken to the foot of the Erdtree" actually means until very late), and the system has no contingency for when the maiden dies too early, dies too late, or refuses. Hyetta gets guided toward the Three Fingers through a process that involves eating human eyeballs she's been told are grapes. The institution is feeding its own candidates into a meat grinder and calling the output sanctified.

(Marika telling the Tarnished "should ye fail to become aught at all, ye will be forsaken, amounting only to sacrifices" is — she's not threatening them. She's describing the system's design parameters. Failed candidates are consumables. Successful candidates are products. This is a production line with a theological wrapper.)

Here's the temporal rhyme that keeps nagging at me. The Hornsent had an ascension system. It was a specific ritual: sacred couple, burial shrouds, solar bark, Divine Gate. The system had rules. The rules existed because the power on the other side of the gate was dangerous and the ritual was the containment protocol. Marika broke the containment protocol. She stole the output without going through the process. And everything — everything, the entire Golden Order, the Shattering, the Tarnished system, Miquella's whole DLC quest — is a consequence of that original institutional failure.

Then Miquella comes along and tries to do it properly. He sheds his body piece by piece. He secures a consort (Radahn, killed and resurrected for the purpose). He walks the stations of suffering. He approaches the Divine Gate through the correct process. And it doesn't work because you show up and kill his consort's body in the boss fight. The one guy who understood that the institutional process matters, that you can't just grab power and expect the system to hold, gets defeated by a Tarnished who is themselves a product of the broken system — a candidate processed through Marika's illegitimate pipeline, credentialed by the very institution that was built to cover for the original usurpation.

(Miquella's crosses across the Land of Shadow are modeled after the Stations of the Cross, which — if you want to push the institutional-design reading — the Stations are themselves a devotional technology, a ritual framework designed to process participants through a specific emotional and theological sequence. Miquella is using institutional design to try to fix an institutional failure. He's building a better pipeline. And the pipeline still fails because the variable he can't control is the player character, who has no investment in his process and every incentive to break it.)

Gideon figured all this out. That's his tragedy. "The all-knowing knows. No one can become Elden Lord." He's not saying the task is too hard. He's saying the system is broken at a level that completing the pipeline cannot fix. The credentialing process was designed to legitimate a usurpation, and completing a process designed to legitimate a fraud just produces — a more elaborately credentialed fraud. He's looked at the institutional mechanics and concluded that the institution itself is the problem, and his response is to try to prevent anyone from completing the pipeline, which is — I mean, that's a institutional-reform position. It's a terrible one. "Shut down the pipeline" is not the same as "build a better pipeline." But at least he's asking the right question, which is more than Goldmask ever does.

Goldmask's proposed fix — remove the fickleness of the gods from the Golden Order — is the most institutional-design-brained response in the entire setting and also the most terrifying one. He looked at Marika's system and said "the problem isn't the rules, it's that the rulemaker has discretion." Which, sure, eliminating discretionary authority from a governance system is a recognizable reform impulse. But the thing he's proposing is essentially: keep the pipeline, keep the credentialing process, keep the entire institutional architecture that was built to launder a usurpation, but make it run on automatic so no individual god can break it again. He's not fixing the foundation. He's pouring concrete over the cracks so they can't be seen. That's not reform. That's — well, the Tarnished Archaeologist calls it fascism, and I don't think that's wrong exactly, but I think the more precise term is something like "totalitarian proceduralism." The system is perfect; only the operators are flawed; remove the operators. The trains will run on time because there will be no one left to delay them.

The Dung Eater's ending is the one that actually breaks the institutional logic, and I think that's why people find it so viscerally upsetting beyond the obvious body-horror dimension. If everyone is an Omen, the category "Omen" ceases to function as a sorting mechanism. The entire apparatus of exclusion that the Golden Order built — Omen underground, Misbegotten in chains, Albinaurics discarded, Those Who Live in Death hunted — all of it depends on the existence of a "normal" category against which deviance is measured. Universalize the deviance and the measurement collapses. It's not that the pipeline gets fixed. The pipeline becomes meaningless because there's no longer a population that can be sorted through it.

Which brings me back to the original point about meritocracy. The Tarnished who becomes Elden Lord doesn't win because they're the strongest. They win because they're the one who happened to complete a credentialing sequence that was designed by a usurper to retroactively justify a theft, managed by bureaucratic intermediaries who may have lost contact with their principal, fueled by the immolation of women who were not told what they were signing up for, and opposed at every stage by competing candidates, institutional gatekeepers, and at least three other cosmic entities running their own pipelines. The player character is "of no renown" — the least likely candidate — and that's not a meritocratic triumph. That's a system so degraded that its own selection criteria no longer predict outcomes.

Same as it ever was, honestly.
