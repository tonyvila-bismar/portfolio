The Gloam-Eyed Queen's Last Apostle

by Oneila

I wore a god once.

Not metaphorically. The robes are skin. Divine skin, flayed and cured and stitched into vestments that stretch when I stretch and breathe when I breathe and remember, in the fibers, what it felt like to be worn by something holy. The Apostles do not discuss the source. The Nobles do not discuss the source. The skin is the office. The office is the skin. You put it on and you become what it makes you, and what it makes you is a thing that can channel black flame, which is the flame that kills.

Not the way a sword kills. Not the way rot kills. The way death kills. Final. Absolute. The soul extinguished. The body returned to nothing. No Erdtree cycle. No golden rebirth. No coming back.

That was the world before Marika sealed it away. That was the world when Destined Death was loose.

You want to know what that world looked like? Everyone does. The Golden Order erased the memory so thoroughly that people imagine it must have been hell — a world of constant extinction, of terror, of souls winking out like candles. The Fundamentalists teach that Marika's great mercy was locking death in Maliketh's blade and giving the Lands Between the gift of the cycle: die, return to the Erdtree, be reborn.

Here is what they don't teach: when death was real, so was everything else.

A marriage meant something when the people in it could die. A child meant something when the child could be lost. A harvest meant something when starvation was not a temporary inconvenience remedied by golden grace but an ending — a real ending, for real people, who would not come back.

The Gloam-Eyed Queen understood this. She was an Empyrean — chosen by the Greater Will, or by something older, the accounts disagree — and her domain was Destined Death. Not as a weapon. As a principle. She wielded the black flame the way a physician wields a scalpel: precisely, with knowledge of what it cuts and what it preserves. Death was her instrument and her sacrament and her gift.

Gift. I use the word deliberately. The Golden Order calls Destined Death a curse. The Fundamentalists call it the great enemy. Marika had it sealed because — why? Because she feared it? Because she wanted to control who died and who didn't? Because a god who removes death from the world makes herself indispensable, and indispensability is the purest form of power?

Yes. All of those. But also: because Destined Death was competition. The Gloam-Eyed Queen offered an alternative theology. Her Godskin Apostles — of whom I am the last, or near enough — preached that death was sacred. That the ending was the meaning. That a life without death was not eternal life but eternal postponement, and postponement is not the same as living.

Marika sent Maliketh to defeat the Queen. He did. He took the black flame. He sealed Destined Death in his blade and buried it in Farum Azula, in the crumbling temple where time does not work, where the storm holds everything in permanent almost-falling.

The Queen vanished. The Apostles were hunted. The robes — the god-skin vestments — were declared abomination. We were driven underground, into the catacombs and the forgotten places, and we have been there ever since, tending a flame that the world has decided should not exist.

I am old. I don't know how old. Time does not treat the Apostles kindly — the skin we wear interacts with our biology in ways that the Queen never fully explained, and one of those ways is that we persist. Not immortality. Persistence. My joints ache. My vision blurs. My hands, which once channeled black flame with the precision of a calligrapher, now tremble. But I do not die, because the thing that would kill me — Destined Death — is locked in a blade in a storm, and without it, my body does not know how to stop.

This is the irony that the Fundamentalists will never appreciate: Marika's sealing of Destined Death made her enemies immortal. We cannot die because she took death away. The Apostles she wanted destroyed are the hardest things in the Lands Between to destroy, because destruction requires the tool she confiscated.

I have watched the Golden Order rise. I have watched it claim the Erdtree. I have watched it declare the Crucible impure and the Omen accursed and the Fire Giants extinct and the Nox banished and the merchants buried alive. I have watched it build a civilization on the premise that death is the enemy, and I have watched that civilization produce a world where nothing ends and nothing is risked and nothing means what it should mean because the consequence has been removed.

A knight who cannot truly die is not brave. He is performing.

A sacrifice that will be reversed by the Erdtree's cycle is not sacrifice. It is theater.

A love that cannot be lost to death is not — I want to say it is not love, but that would be unfair. It is love. But it is love with the bottom cut out. Love without the abyss underneath it. Love that knows, in its marrow, that the worst thing cannot happen, and therefore the best thing cannot fully arrive.

The Queen knew this. The Queen's theology was simple: death makes life real. Remove death and you remove reality. What remains is a golden dream — beautiful, luminous, and empty.

Marika shattered the Elden Ring. I do not know why. The theories multiply — guilt, strategy, madness, a plan so complex it looks like destruction. But I know this: when the Ring shattered, death cracked loose. The Rune of Death leaked through the Greattree's roots. Those Who Live in Death appeared — the half-dead, the improperly killed, the consequences of a system that removed consequences and then broke.

And the Night of the Black Knives happened. Numen women stole fragments of Destined Death from Maliketh and killed Godwyn the Golden. The first true death in an age. Except it went wrong — Godwyn's soul died but his body persisted, and Ranni's body died but her soul persisted, and the cursemark that should have been a clean circle broke in two, and now the world has death back but it's the wrong death. Broken death. Death that doesn't finish the job.

I could have told them. I could have told anyone who asked that Destined Death is not a weapon you aim. It is a force you channel, and channeling requires training, and the training was the Queen's gift to the Apostles, and the Apostles have been hiding in catacombs for centuries because the civilization that needed us declared us monsters.

The black flame is not gone. I can still summon it. A candle-flicker, where once I held an inferno. Enough to light a room. Enough to remind my hands what they were made for. Not enough to do what needs doing — not enough to give Godwyn the death his body deserves, not enough to finish what the Black Knives started, not enough to show the Lands Between what a clean death looks like so they can remember what a clean life felt like.

The Queen is gone. The skin remembers her. When the wind shifts in the catacombs and the air moves across the vestment's surface, the fibers contract — a reflex, autonomic, the skin responding to a stimulus that its original owner would have recognized. A shiver. A memory of being alive.

I sit in the dark and I tend my candle-flame and I wait. Not for the Queen's return — I am not sentimental. I wait because the alternative is to stop waiting, and stopping is the one thing my body cannot do, because the woman who took death also took my ability to choose it.

Somewhere above me, the Erdtree glows. Golden and spectral and empty. A ghost worshipped by a civilization that forgot what ghosts are.

I remember.

The flame remembers.

Whether that is enough — whether remembering a thing is the same as the thing itself, whether the memory of real death can do the work of real death in a world that has forgotten both — I do not know.

The black flame flickers on my palm. It is warm. It is final. It is very small.

It is still lit.
