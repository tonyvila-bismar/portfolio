The Tuning

by Pellegrin

The first spirit ash Roderika tuned was a soldier who had died at Stormveil Castle. She did not know his name. The ash had been brought to her by a Tarnished who had collected it from a corpse near the gatehouse, and the Tarnished had not asked the corpse's name, because corpses in the Lands Between were not people but resources.

The process of spirit tuning is straightforward to describe and difficult to understand. The tuner holds the spirit ash — a small, dense pellet of calcified essence, cool to the touch, slightly luminous in darkness — and reaches into the pattern encoded within it. The pattern is the spirit. Not a representation of the spirit, not a recording, not a symbolic encoding. The pattern is, in every way that matters, the person who died. Their reflexes, their training, their habitual grip on a weapon, the specific angle at which they held their shield. All of it persists in the ash the way a melody persists in notation.

To tune a spirit ash is to alter the pattern. Strengthen a reflex. Deepen a trained response. Reinforce the ligature between intention and execution. The spirit, when summoned after tuning, fights more effectively. Its shield holds longer. Its sword finds gaps it previously missed. The tuner does not add anything to the pattern. She clarifies what is already there.

Roderika learned this from no one. There was no school of spirit tuning, no curriculum, no tradition of masters passing technique to students. The ability appeared in her the way language appears in children — suddenly, irrevocably, as if a door had been opened that could not be closed. She had arrived at the Roundtable Hold as a noblewoman with no useful skills, wearing a red hood that marked her as someone her family wished to forget. She had been sent to Stormveil with a group of other disposables, and she had survived, and the survival had cracked something open.

The soldier's ash responded immediately to her attention. She could feel the pattern beneath her fingers — not physically, but in the way that a musician feels the structure of a chord before striking it. The soldier had been right-handed. He had favored a straight thrust over a lateral cut. His shield work was competent but unimaginative, the product of drill rather than instinct. She strengthened the thrust. She tightened the coordination between his shield arm and his sword arm by a fraction that she could not have expressed numerically but that she knew, with absolute certainty, was correct.

The Tarnished summoned the soldier in a fight the next day and reported that the spirit had performed better.

Roderika did not ask what "better" meant. She knew. She had felt the improvement in the pattern before it manifested in the field. The tuning had been correct. The soldier's ash now contained a version of the soldier that was more effective than the soldier had been alive.

This was the first indication that something was wrong, though she did not recognize it as such.

She tuned a second ash. A jellyfish. The pattern was simpler than the soldier's — fewer variables, fewer trained responses, a narrower range of behavior. But within that narrowness she found something unexpected. The jellyfish's pattern contained a component that was not functional. It did not contribute to combat effectiveness. It did not improve summoning reliability or duration. It was, as far as she could determine, vestigial.

She examined it. The component was a repetitive oscillation in the pattern, a rhythmic fluctuation that served no purpose she could identify. It was not damage. It was not noise. It was structured, intentional, and completely useless.

She left it alone.

The third ash was a pack of wolves. The pack had a collective pattern, which was itself composed of individual patterns, which were themselves composed of sub-patterns governing specific behaviors. The complexity was staggering. She spent three days simply observing the architecture before she attempted any modification.

Within the pack pattern she found the same vestigial component. Each wolf carried it. The oscillation was slightly different in each — different frequency, different amplitude — but the structure was identical. A rhythmic fluctuation that contributed nothing to function.

She left those alone too.

By the tenth ash, she had developed a provisional taxonomy. The vestigial component appeared in every spirit she examined. It varied in complexity — the soldier's was relatively simple, a single oscillation; a sorcerer's was an elaborate polyrhythmic structure that she spent a full day mapping. But the basic architecture was consistent. A pattern within the pattern. Structured. Intentional. Useless.

She began to call it the remainder.

The word was imprecise, but precision required understanding, and understanding had not yet arrived. The remainder was what was left over after she accounted for everything functional in the pattern — every combat reflex, every trained response, every habitual motion. It was the part of the person that had nothing to do with fighting.

She mentioned this to the spirit tuner's assistant, a quiet man named Gideon who maintained the workspace and organized the ashes. He asked if the remainder affected performance.

"No," she said.

"Then why does it matter?"

She did not have an answer.

The fifteenth ash was a woman who had been a healer. Her functional components were minimal — a dodge reflex, an instinct to position herself behind allies, a rudimentary knowledge of how to swing a short blade without cutting herself. She had not been a fighter. She had been collected because she was there, because the Tarnished who found her ash did not distinguish between spirits who had been warriors and spirits who had been something else.

Roderika tuned the dodge reflex and the positioning instinct. There was almost nothing else to improve.

The healer's remainder was the largest she had encountered relative to her functional core. It was as if the woman's entire life had been non-functional — not in the sense of being purposeless, but in the sense that nothing she had spent her years doing was relevant to combat. Her skills, her knowledge, her accumulated understanding of wounds and medicines and the particular way a body communicates distress through posture and breathing — all of it sat in the remainder, intact and inaccessible, while the summoning process called up only her ability to duck.

The Tarnished summoned her once, watched her dodge ineffectually around the edges of a fight, and never summoned her again.

The ash sat on Roderika's shelf. The healer's remainder continued its slow, complex oscillation. The dodge reflex waited, unused, to be called.

She tuned a knight of the Crucible. The pattern was ancient — older than the Golden Order, older than the Erdtree's current form. The knight's reflexes were built on principles that predated modern combat doctrine by centuries. His shield technique was not a technique at all but an instinct, the way an animal knows to duck before a blow lands. She strengthened what she could. She left what she couldn't understand.

The remainder in the Crucible knight's pattern was enormous. It dwarfed the functional components. If the combat reflexes were a house, the remainder was the city the house sat in — sprawling, interconnected, vastly larger than the structure it surrounded.

She spent a week inside the pattern, mapping the remainder's topology. It was not random. It had regions, borders, areas of high density and areas of near-emptiness. The dense areas clustered around what she came to think of as nodes — points where multiple threads of the remainder converged and interacted. The interactions were not mechanical. They did not produce outputs. They simply occurred, the way weather occurs, without purpose or product.

She emerged from the mapping exhausted and asked herself a question she had been avoiding.

What was the remainder for?

The question was malformed. "For" implies function, and the remainder had no function. A better question: what was the remainder? Not what it did — it did nothing — but what it was.

She returned to the soldier's ash. She had tuned it weeks ago. The Tarnished had been using it regularly, summoning the spirit in fights across the Lands Between. She examined the pattern again.

The functional components had degraded slightly, which was expected. Repeated summoning wore at the pattern the way repeated use wears at any tool. The degradation was minor and easily repaired.

The remainder was unchanged.

This should not have been the case. If the remainder was part of the pattern, it should have degraded alongside the functional components. Summoning stress did not discriminate. It affected everything in the ash equally, the way heat affects everything in a room. The functional components had worn. The remainder had not.

She checked the jellyfish. Same result. She checked three wolves from the pack. Same result. She checked the healer.

The healer's remainder had not degraded because the healer had never been summoned again. This was a different kind of confirmation. The functional components had also not degraded, because they had not been used. But the remainder had not degraded even in the absence of summoning stress — which meant the remainder's stability was not a consequence of being shielded from use. It was a property of the remainder itself.

She designed a test. She took the soldier's ash and the healer's ash and subjected both to the same artificial stress — a sustained application of the tuning frequency at maximum intensity, the equivalent of summoning a spirit continuously for hours. The functional components in both ashes degraded. The soldier's sword technique lost precision. The healer's dodge reflex blurred.

The remainders did not change.

She increased the stress. She pushed the tuning frequency beyond what any normal summoning would produce. The functional components degraded further. The soldier's pattern lost its grip on the straight thrust that had been its foundation. The healer's positioning instinct dissolved into noise.

The remainders did not change.

She stopped. The functional damage was repairable but the experiment had answered the question. The remainder was not a component of the pattern. It was substrate. It was the material the pattern was written on. The combat reflexes, the trained responses, the habitual motions — all of these were inscriptions on a surface, and the surface was the person.

The remainder was not degrading because it was not being summoned. When the Tarnished called the spirit into the field, the functional components manifested — the soldier's sword arm, his shield technique, his trained responses — and the remainder stayed in the ash. It was not part of the summoning. It was not accessed, not activated, not used. It sat in the pattern the way sediment sits at the bottom of a glass of water that has been poured carefully.

She understood then what the remainder was.

It was everything about the person that was not useful.

The soldier's preference for a particular song. The jellyfish's — she hesitated, because attributing preferences to a jellyfish required a framework she did not possess, but the oscillation was there, structured and intentional, and she did not know what else to call it. The wolves' individual variations — the way one wolf in a pack has a habit of circling left, not because circling left is tactically superior but because that wolf has always circled left, for reasons that are not reasons but simply the shape of a particular life.

The remainder was personality. Not the personality that manifests in combat — aggressiveness, caution, creativity — but the personality that has nothing to do with combat. The part of a person that exists between fights. The part that hums while walking. The part that prefers rain to sun, or sun to rain, or is indifferent to both but notices the quality of light at a particular hour and feels something that does not have a name.

She had been strengthening soldiers and discarding people.

The realization arrived without drama. She was sitting at her workbench, holding the Crucible knight's ash, and the thought formed itself the way her tuning ability had formed itself — complete, irrevocable, a door that could not be closed. She had taken patterns that contained entire lives and stripped them for parts. She had kept the sword arm and left the song.

The Tarnished did not know. They brought ashes and asked for improvements and received them. The spirits fought better. The Tarnished were satisfied. The transaction was clean.

But the remainder sat in every ash she had tuned, untouched and untouchable, a sealed room in a house that had been renovated around it. The soldier's song was still in the ash. The jellyfish's oscillation was still in the ash. Every preference, every habit, every purposeless inclination that had once been part of a living being was preserved in calcified form, ignored by the summoning process, invisible to the Tarnished, visible only to her.

She could not tune the remainder. She had tried. The components did not respond to the same techniques that worked on functional patterns. They were not strengthened by reinforcement. They were not clarified by attention. They existed in a register that her ability could perceive but not modify, the way a person can hear a frequency of sound without being able to produce it.

She could not remove the remainder either. She had considered it — not seriously, not as a plan, but as a theoretical exercise. What would happen if she excised the non-functional components from a pattern? The spirit would still fight. The Tarnished would notice no difference. The ash would weigh the same, glow the same, summon the same.

But the person would be gone. What remained would be a weapon that remembered being a person but no longer was one.

She did not do this.

She continued tuning. The Tarnished continued bringing ashes. The spirits continued fighting. The remainders continued sitting in their sealed rooms, preserved and purposeless, carrying the songs and preferences and habits of people who had died and been collected and brought to a woman in a floating building who could see what they had been but could only improve what they were used for.

She mentioned the remainder to no one else after Gideon. Not because it was a secret, but because she understood, with the same certainty that governed her tuning, that explaining the remainder to someone who could not perceive it would require her to translate it into functional terms. "It doesn't affect performance" would be the summary. And the summary would be accurate. And the accuracy would be the problem.

The Crucible knight's ash sat on her workbench for longer than any other. She returned to it in the evenings when the Tarnished had gone and the Hold was quiet. She did not tune it. She sat with the pattern open before her — the enormous remainder, the small functional core — and she listened to whatever it was that the remainder was doing, the weather of a person who had lived before the Golden Order, who had fought for something older than the Erdtree, who had carried a set of preferences and inclinations and purposeless habits that had survived his death and his calcification and his collection and his delivery to her workbench.

The oscillation was different from the others. More complex. Slower. She could not have described it in language. It was not a song. It was not a memory. It was the residue of a way of being in the world that no longer existed anywhere else — a way of noticing light, of holding stillness, of standing in a particular posture that meant nothing and was irreplaceable.

She closed the pattern. She set the ash on the shelf. She did not tune it.

The Tarnished who had brought it never came back for it. This happened sometimes. The ashes accumulated on her shelves — tuned and untuned, functional and remaindered, each one containing a sealed room that no one would ever open.

One evening she took the healer's ash down from the shelf. She had not looked at it in weeks. The functional components were intact — the dodge, the positioning — preserved by disuse. The remainder was exactly as she had left it.

She opened the pattern and examined the remainder carefully. The healer's oscillation was warm and arrhythmic, lacking the soldier's precision or the Crucible knight's depth. It moved the way hands move when they are working without thinking — adjusting a bandage, testing a temperature, performing tasks that the body has learned so thoroughly that the mind is free to be elsewhere.

She wondered where the healer's mind had been, in those moments. She wondered what the healer had thought about while her hands worked. She wondered whether the healer had liked her work, or endured it, or performed it out of obligation, or performed it out of love, and she understood that the answer was in the remainder and that the remainder would not translate.

She put the ash back on the shelf, next to the Crucible knight's. The two remainders oscillated in their separate patterns, unaware of each other, unaware of her, unaware of the shelf or the Hold or the world outside. They were not waiting. They were not suffering. They were simply persisting, the way a stone persists, or a stain, or the shape of a hand pressed into wet clay and fired.

Roderika adjusted her red hood. The hood had been a gift from her family, given when they sent her away. It marked her as disposable. She had kept it because she had arrived with nothing else, and then she had kept it because it was hers, and then she had kept it because keeping a thing that was meant to discard you is its own kind of remainder.

She picked up the next ash. A foot soldier. Young, by the density of the pattern. Trained recently. The remainder was small — a brief, simple oscillation, barely distinguishable from noise.

She strengthened his shield arm.
