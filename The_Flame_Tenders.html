The Flame Tenders

by Tess Jyanssen

The first thing she noticed about the Monastery of Fire was that the furnaces were wrong.

Not wrong in the catastrophic sense. The furnaces were lit, the monks were tending them, the containment seals along the stone channels were holding within what Tess estimated (from thirty feet away, through a window she wasn't supposed to be looking through) to be acceptable tolerances. Everything was functional. Everything was maintained. Everything was also, very clearly, running on institutional memory rather than engineering knowledge, and Tess had spent enough years around institutional memory to know exactly how that story ends.

It ends with the furnace.

She had come to the Mountaintops of the Giants for the timber. Specifically, for the ironwood that grew in the passes between the snowline and the forge-wastes, a species of conifer so dense that a six-inch plank could stop a crossbow bolt and so fire-resistant that the monks used it for scaffolding inside the flame halls themselves. Ironwood grows slowly. One inch of radial growth per decade, which means a tree wide enough to yield useful lumber is a tree that has been standing since before the Golden Order was established. You don't cut ironwood. You harvest a historical record.

The properties are extraordinary. The resin crystallizes under sustained heat rather than combusting, which means ironwood actually gets harder when you put it near flame. This is, as far as Tess could determine, unique among tree species in the Lands Between and possibly unique in the broader world. The monks used it for furnace scaffolding, for tool handles, for the great wooden frames that held the containment seals in place during replacement. Every other material in the forge-halls degraded. The ironwood endured. It was the single most important structural material in the monastery and nobody outside the monastery had any idea, because the monks did not publish supply-chain analyses.

The ironwood trade was technically unrestricted. Technically. In practice, every logging crew that tried to operate above the snowline ran into the same problem: the Fire Monks controlled the roads, the Fire Monks controlled the passes, and the Fire Monks had a set of permissions and prohibitions so tangled that even they couldn't tell you which activities were forbidden and which were merely discouraged.

Tess had obtained a logging permit from the Church of the Flame, which was the wrong church. She had then obtained a transit permit from the Guardians of the Flamepeak, who were the right authority but used the wrong form. She had then bribed a quartermaster at the outer garrison to issue her a materials-transport writ that covered ironwood specifically but not the tools needed to cut it, which meant she legally possessed permission to move timber she could not legally fell.

She had then hired a local felling crew out of the last settlement below the snowline, paying them in advance because nobody who works at that altitude works on credit, and equipped them with axes she'd purchased two valleys over and brought in under a separate household-goods exemption that covered personal tools.

Total cost of the logging operation itself: four hundred runes. Total cost of the permits, bribes, transit fees, and creative legal interpretation required to make the logging operation possible: eleven hundred runes.

She loved it. She absolutely loved it.

This was her favorite kind of institutional nonsense: the kind that had clearly started as a security measure (don't let anyone near the Giants' Flame who doesn't have authorization) and had, over centuries of accretion, become a system so ornate that the security function was buried under seventeen layers of procedural sediment. The monks at the gate didn't check her permits to see whether she was a threat. They checked her permits to see whether they were filled out correctly. A woman carrying enough alchemical fire to blow the entire monastery off the mountain would have been waved through if her paperwork was in order. A woman carrying a logging axe with the wrong stamp would be turned away at the gate.

Tess was waved through. Her paperwork was impeccable. Her actual intentions were somewhat more flexible than the paperwork suggested.

The Monastery of Fire had been built in the Age of the Erdtree on the bones of something much older, and it showed. The lower halls were Giant-work: massive stone blocks fitted without mortar, each one taller than Tess and weighing more than her entire logging operation. The upper halls were human-scale additions, built on top of and around the Giant foundations the way a vine grows through a wall. The proportions were wrong everywhere. Doorways that should have been eight feet tall were twelve. Corridors that should have been wide enough for two people were wide enough for six. The monks had filled the extra space with scaffolding, shelving, storage, and an impressive collection of devotional objects that Tess recognized as repurposed Giant artifacts. A carved stone that had probably been a Giant's hearthstone was serving as a prayer altar. A section of what looked like a Giant's chain link was being used as a boot scraper.

The forge-halls were the heart of the operation. Tess counted seven primary furnaces, each one occupying a chamber roughly the size of a decent tavern, each one fed by a channel system that drew heat from somewhere below the monastery's foundations. The monks called it the "remnant flame." What it actually was, as near as Tess could determine from asking questions that the monks found suspiciously specific, was a controlled bleed from the Giants' Flame itself.

This is where the engineering got interesting.

The Giants' Flame was the last weapon capable of burning the Erdtree. Marika had waged the War against the Giants specifically to neutralize this threat, and the Fire Monks existed to ensure the flame was contained, maintained, and absolutely never used. This was, in Tess's professional assessment, the single most important engineering job in the Lands Between, and it was being performed by an order of monks who had been doing it for so long that they had forgotten why.

Not forgotten the history. They knew the history. They could recite it. They had hymns about it, prayers about it, a liturgical calendar organized around the anniversary of the War and the sealing of the flame and the binding of the last Giant. What they had forgotten was the engineering.

The seven furnaces were supposed to serve as pressure bleeds. Tess worked this out from the channel geometry. The Giants' Flame burned underground, sealed by the Erdtree's power and the physical mass of the mountain. But sealed flame still generates heat, and heat generates pressure, and pressure needs to go somewhere or it goes everywhere at once. The channel system drew controlled amounts of heat from the sealed flame and vented it through the seven furnaces, which the monks kept burning at what they believed was the correct temperature.

"Believed" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

The monks maintained the furnaces by ritual. Every six hours, a team of three added fuel (ironwood, naturally, which is why the ironwood supply mattered more than anyone outside the monastery understood), adjusted the dampers according to a pattern they had memorized, and checked the containment seals for cracks. The pattern had been handed down for generations. The original engineering specifications, if they had ever existed in written form, were gone. What remained was a set of instructions that told the monks what to do but not why, which meant that when something went wrong (a seal cracked, a damper jammed, the flame ran hotter than expected) the monks' only diagnostic tool was "do the thing we always do, but harder."

Tess spent three days in the monastery. She was supposed to be coordinating her ironwood shipment, and she did coordinate her ironwood shipment, but she also spent every spare hour in or near the forge-halls, watching the monks work, asking questions, and doing math on the back of her transit permit.

The math was not encouraging.

The containment seals were limestone. Limestone is a fine construction material for most applications. It is not a fine construction material for applications involving sustained temperatures above six hundred degrees, because limestone undergoes thermal decomposition at that threshold. It turns to calcium oxide. It becomes powder. The monks were replacing cracked seals every three to four months, which they considered routine maintenance. Tess considered it evidence that the seals were failing faster than they should, which meant the flame was burning hotter than the system was designed to handle, which meant the pressure bleeds weren't bleeding enough pressure, which meant one of the seven furnaces was probably obstructed or miscalibrated, which meant the monks needed an engineer.

She checked the replacement logs. Brother Urdeth kept meticulous records, she'd give him that. Every seal replacement, dated, noting which furnace and which position. The dates told a story the monks hadn't noticed because they were too close to see it.

Furnace Three was eating seals twice as fast as the others.

Furnace Three was also the furnace closest to the mountain's northeast face, which Tess thought was probably significant because the northeast face was where the Giant's chain had been anchored during the War. If the chain had cracked the bedrock (and it would have, you don't chain a god to a mountain without cracking the bedrock), then the sealed flame below might have a secondary vent path through the fissure. A secondary vent path that nobody knew about because nobody had surveyed the subsurface geology in living memory, if anyone ever had.

They did not want an engineer.

Tess raised the issue with Brother Urdeth, who was the closest thing the monastery had to a technical authority. Urdeth was a large, patient man who had been tending the furnaces for thirty-one years and who regarded the flame with the steady devotion of a man who has organized his entire life around a single task. He heard Tess's concerns about the limestone, the thermal decomposition threshold, the accelerating replacement schedule. He nodded. He thanked her.

Then he told her that the flame burned as the flame willed, and that the monks' role was to tend, not to engineer.

Tess, to her credit, did not scream.

She wanted to. She wanted to grab Brother Urdeth by the shoulders and explain that flame is a chemical process governed by fuel, oxygen, and temperature, and that his entire monastery was one undiagnosed subsurface fissure away from venting the Giants' Flame directly into the lower halls. She wanted to tell him that the hum in Furnace Four had a wobble in it and that the wobble meant something and that nobody was listening.

She did not do any of these things. She had learned, over a long career of encountering people who maintained dangerous systems through faith rather than knowledge, that shouting does not help. It makes them close ranks. It makes the next person who tries to bring engineering into the conversation even less welcome.

What she did instead was find the oldest monk in the monastery, a woman named Sister Velisse who was ninety-three years old and had been tending the furnaces since before Urdeth was born. Velisse didn't talk much. She sat in a chair near Furnace Four, which she said ran quieter than the others, and she watched the flame through a viewing slit that had been cut into the stone at some point in the distant past by someone who, Tess noted with professional interest, had understood exactly where to place a viewing slit to give you maximum diagnostic visibility of the flame's color and behavior.

"Someone built this slit for an engineer," Tess said.

"Someone built this whole place for an engineer," Velisse said. "We just lost the engineer."

Velisse remembered things the other monks didn't. She remembered that when she was young, there had been a set of calibration marks on the furnace walls. Small notches at specific heights that told you where the flame should reach at each stage of the tending cycle. The notches had worn away over the decades. Nobody had re-cut them because nobody remembered what the correct heights were. The ritual (add fuel, adjust dampers, check seals) had replaced the measurements. The monks were flying blind and had been for at least fifty years.

She also remembered that Furnace Three had always run hotter than the others. "Even when I was a girl," she said. "Three was the angry one."

"Did anyone ever investigate why?"

"Brother Kolm wanted to. He was going to open the floor channels and trace the feed line back to the source. They told him it was forbidden. Direct observation of the sealed flame's substrate was a violation of the Containment Protocols, Article Twelve, which I think was written by someone who'd never seen a furnace from closer than a hundred yards."

"What happened to Brother Kolm?"

"Transferred to the outer garrison. Spent twenty years checking transit permits."

Tess filed that away in the part of her mind where she kept evidence of institutions punishing the people who tried to save them.

"How do you know the flame is at the right temperature?" Tess asked.

"It feels right," Velisse said.

"And when it doesn't feel right?"

"Then I adjust."

"Adjust how?"

Velisse gave her a look that Tess recognized. It was the look of a practitioner who has internalized so much tacit knowledge that they cannot articulate it, and who knows that the inability to articulate it is exactly the problem. Velisse could feel when Furnace Four was running hot. She could not explain how she could feel it. She could not teach it. When she died, the feeling would die with her.

"The color," Velisse said, after a long pause. "And the sound. When Four is right, it hums. When it's running hot, the hum gets a wobble in it. Like a wheel that's not quite true."

That was something. That was data, even if Velisse didn't know it was data. A wobble in the acoustic signature meant turbulent airflow, which meant uneven fuel combustion, which meant the damper positions could be correlated to flame stability through sound alone if someone bothered to build a reference index. Someone with instruments. Someone with time. Someone the monks would allow to sit next to the furnaces for long enough to map the acoustic profile across a full tending cycle.

Someone who was not going to be Tess, because Tess was a timber merchant with a three-day transit permit and no authority whatsoever.

Tess sat with that for a while.

She sat with it the way she sat with every beautiful, doomed system she'd ever encountered: with admiration for what it was, with fury at the regulations that had made it fragile, and with the grim practical certainty that she was going to have to do something about it whether anyone asked her to or not.

The ironwood shipment left on schedule. Twelve planks, each one eight feet long and dense enough to sink in water. The felling crew had done good work. The trees had come down clean, the cuts were straight, and the resin had already begun crystallizing along the exposed grain in that particular way that meant the wood would be functionally fireproof within a season.

Tess ran her hand along the top plank as the cart pulled away from the monastery gates. Two hundred years of growth under her palm. Two centuries of snow and wind and the distant heat of a god's flame seeping up through the roots. The wood was warm. Ironwood is always warm near a flame source. It drinks heat the way other wood drinks water, and it holds it.

She would sell eleven of the twelve planks at market in the Altus Plateau. The twelfth she would keep. She always kept one piece of the best material from every job. Her quarters at the circus, back when the circus was still running, had been lined with samples: a strip of Liurnian glintstone-infused cedar, a plank of Caelid red oak (harvested before the rot, worth more than gold now), a section of Haligtree root-wood that Miquella's people had grown into a living arch. She collected the materials of the world the way other people collected paintings. The material was the painting. You just had to know how to read it.

Tess left with the shipment. She had her timber, her transit permits, her materials-transport writ.

She also had, tucked into the bottom of her travel pack, a set of measurements she'd taken at all seven furnaces over three days. Flame color at twelve-hour intervals. Seal-crack locations mapped to furnace geometry. Heat readings estimated from the expansion rate of ironwood offcuts she'd placed at measured distances from each furnace mouth. An acoustic log of Furnace Four's hum, noted in the margin of her transit permit in a notation system she'd borrowed from a Carian lute-tuner.

Nobody had asked her to do this. Nobody had authorized it. The Fire Monks' regulations specifically prohibited unauthorized measurement of flame-related phenomena by non-monastic personnel, which Tess considered the most dangerous sentence in the entire Monastery of Fire, because it meant that the only people allowed to understand the flame were the people who had forgotten how.

She would write up her findings. She would send them to the monastery. Brother Urdeth would thank her and file them somewhere they'd never be read.

But Velisse would read them. Tess was almost sure of that. And Velisse would know what they meant, even if she couldn't explain how she knew, and maybe that was enough.

It probably wasn't enough.

But the ironwood was excellent, and the view from the Mountaintops was something Tess would remember for a long time: the Erdtree glowing gold in the distance, and below it the sealed flame burning in the dark, tended by monks who had forgotten everything about fire except that it was holy. Which is, if you think about it, the most dangerous thing you can forget.
