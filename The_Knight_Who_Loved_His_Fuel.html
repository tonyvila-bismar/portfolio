The Knight Who Loved His Fuel

by Sidonei ne Sangbelles

They called him the closest.

Closest to becoming Elden Lord. Closest to fixing everything. Closest, which is a word institutions use for the person who almost succeeded so they can study what went wrong without examining whether the thing he was supposed to succeed at was worth doing.

Vyke. Knight of the fingerprint. Armor singed and blistered where the Three Fingers touched him. Dragon-loved — Lansseax herself favored him, which tells you something about his bearing or his smell or whatever it is that an ancient dragon finds attractive in a mortal, and I am not going to pretend I understand the criteria because I have been exiled from one society and I see no reason to attempt comprehension of another's courtship rituals when my own proved fatal.

He was the best Tarnished candidate in a generation. The pipeline loved him. The Two Fingers probably twitched with something adjacent to enthusiasm, insofar as divine appendages experience enthusiasm, which I suspect they don't because enthusiasm requires a nervous system and the Fingers appear to have outsourced theirs to the Finger Readers approximately three theological ages ago.

Vyke had a maiden. Her name was Lireth.

This is the part where I am supposed to describe her with the clinical detachment of a man who has seen enough scapegoating to recognize the machinery. I have seen enough scapegoating to recognize the machinery. The detachment — the detachment is harder today. I don't know why.

Lireth was recruited at fifteen. Grace-sensitive, which meant her body responded to the golden light in ways that the people running the Maiden Assignment pipeline found useful. Useful. I want to hold that word up to the light and turn it slowly, the way you'd examine a knife for blood. Useful. A fifteen-year-old girl was useful. Her usefulness consisted of walking alongside a man in armor toward a destination she had been told was the foot of the Erdtree, which was technically accurate in the way that telling someone they're going to a barbecue is technically accurate when they are the pig.

The Forge of the Giants is at the foot of the Erdtree. The Forge of the Giants is where the maiden burns. That's the theological pipeline: a woman walks a champion to the door, and at the door she catches fire, and the fire opens the way, and the champion walks through, and the woman is ash, and the system produces an Elden Lord, and somewhere in the ecclesiastical paperwork there is a line item for fuel consumption.

Lireth was a line item.

I know about line items. I was one. Different ledger, different institution, same principle: a person reduced to a function, and the function reduced to a cost, and the cost weighed against the output, and the output — the Elden Lord, the political stability, the theological continuity — the output always justifies the cost because the people calculating the cost are the people who benefit from the output.

(My mother calculated costs. She was good at it. She calculated the cost of keeping me versus the cost of exile and she — I don't want to talk about my mother. I was talking about Vyke.)

Vyke reached the Erdtree threshold. The closest. He had fought his way across the Lands Between, collected Great Runes, impressed the Two Fingers, earned grace and kept earning it, done everything the pipeline required of a candidate — and at the last gate, at the moment when Lireth would have walked to the Forge and become fuel, Vyke descended beneath the capital.

He went underground. He went to the Three Fingers.

I should explain the Three Fingers. The Two Fingers are the Greater Will's emissaries — trembling divine appendages that sit in back rooms and twitch and get interpreted by old women who may or may not be making the whole thing up, which is a theological arrangement I find both contemptible and deeply familiar. The Three Fingers are the alternative. A rival set of fingers, underground, associated with the Frenzied Flame. More fingers, different agenda, same communication method: touch and silence and let the interpreter do the rest.

The Frenzied Flame is — imagine setting fire to the concept of individuality. Imagine a flame that doesn't burn bodies but burns the boundaries between them. The Frenzied Flame wants everything to melt into everything else, all suffering dissolved by dissolving the selves that suffer, which is a solution to the problem of pain in the way that destroying the patient is a solution to the problem of disease. Not wrong, exactly. Just so far past the point that wrongness has become irrelevant.

The Three Fingers offered Vyke a different path. Become Lord through frenzy instead of faith. No Forge. No burning maiden. Just the small matter of incinerating the boundary between yourself and every other conscious being in existence.

Vyke took it. He was scorched by it. His fingerprint armor — the armor that identified him, that carried the pattern of his skin, the thing that said "this is Vyke and no one else" — his armor blistered.

The official account says he was corrupted. Seduced by an alien cosmic force. Lured away from his duty by a power that offered him an escape he was too weak to refuse.

The official account is garbage.

I know because Lireth wrote a letter. She wrote it to her mother. In the letter — which survived because a soldier kept it, because soldiers keep things, because the machinery of war is maintained by individual acts of tenderness that no requisition form accounts for — in the letter, Lireth said that Vyke found out.

He found out what the Forge required. He found out that Lireth was fuel. He found out that the entire pilgrimage, the entire quest, the entire theological apparatus of Tarnished and maiden and grace and Erdtree, terminated in a woman's body catching fire so a man could walk through a door.

And he chose the fire for himself.

He went to the Three Fingers and he took the Frenzied Flame because the Frenzied Flame offered a way to become Elden Lord without burning his maiden. A worse way. A way that involved the destruction of everything the Golden Order stood for, the dissolution of all identity, the end of individual suffering through the end of individuality — but a way. A door that didn't require Lireth as kindling.

He loved her.

I need to stop here for a moment because this is the place where the story is supposed to become beautiful and I am not a person who does beautiful well. I do acid. I do dissection. I do the clinical identification of exactly which suture the institution used to stitch the wound shut so no one would notice the wound was institutional. Beautiful is — beautiful is for people who got to stay in their gardens, who got to watch the vines and the water and the slow green patience of growing things, who were not expelled from their — I was talking about Vyke.

He loved her. He took the fire. And then the fire took him.

The Frenzied Flame drove Vyke mad. This is what the Frenzied Flame does — it burns the boundaries of the self, and a self without boundaries is not a self, it is a conflagration, it is the experience of being everyone and no one simultaneously, and the human mind is not built for that and breaks.

Vyke went mad and attacked Lireth.

I have seen this before. Not this specifically — I have never watched a man dissolve into divine psychosis while the person he saved stood in front of him — but the shape. The shape I know. You make a sacrifice for someone. The sacrifice costs you everything. And then the thing you became through the sacrifice turns on the person you made it for, because the sacrifice changed you into something that doesn't remember why it was made.

My mother did this. Different fire. She sacrificed her standing, her political capital, her relationship with the other Sangbelles matriarchs, to protect me from — and then she —

I was talking about Vyke.

Read the sequence again in your head. Not because the sentence is complicated. Because the sequence matters. He loved her. He chose damnation to save her. The damnation destroyed his mind. His destroyed mind attacked the person he had damned himself to protect.

The system won.

Not through justice. Not through theology. Not through the moral superiority of the Golden Order or the righteous punishment of a man who defied the divine plan. The system won through the same mechanism every system wins through, which is that the alternatives to compliance are designed to be worse than compliance. You can follow the pipeline and your maiden burns. You can refuse the pipeline and you burn. There is no door where nobody burns. The architecture doesn't include that door. The architects didn't build it because the architects need the burning.

Lireth survived. I think. The sources are unclear on this point, which means either she died in a way that no one recorded or she lived in a way that no one considered worth recording, and I genuinely don't know which of those possibilities makes me angrier.

(The anger is — I know what it's about. It's not about Lireth specifically. It's about the fifteen-year-old and the pipeline and the word "useful" and the distance between being valued and being consumed, which is the distance between a garden and a fire, which is the distance I have been trying to walk for twenty years without arriving at either end.)

They display Vyke's armor at the Roundtable Hold. What's left of it. The fingerprints blistered beyond recognition — his identity literally burned off the surface. Someone propped it up in a corner the way you'd display a trophy or a warning or a piece of institutional furniture whose function is to remind every other candidate what happens when you love your fuel more than your outcome.

I stood in front of it once. The blisters form a pattern if you look long enough — concentric rings spreading from each fingertip, the way heat radiates from a point source. Physics. Just physics. The Three Fingers touched him and the touch was hot and the heat spread and the pattern it left is exactly the pattern heat always leaves, and there is nothing theological about it, and I don't know why I stood there for twenty minutes looking at a dead man's armor except that the pattern was the shape of a hand, and the hand was reaching, and the reaching had burned through the steel, and nobody reaches that hard for anything except — except —

The metaphor got away from me there.

The armor says: do not love. The armor says: walk through the door. The armor says: she is a line item. She is a cost. She is the price of the thing you are becoming, and the price is not optional, and the man who tried to pay it differently is a cautionary tale propped up in a corner gathering dust.

The closest.

He was the closest, and what he was closest to was a system that required a woman to burn, and he said no, and the no destroyed him, and the system found someone else, and the someone else walked through the door, and the maiden — Melina, the manufactured one, the backup fuel the institution had been hiding the whole time — the maiden burned, and an Elden Lord was crowned, and Vyke's singed armor sat in its corner and nobody asked whether the whole apparatus could have been different.

Nobody asks. That's the trick. The fire is always assumed. The burning is always the baseline. And the man who tried to stop it is always — always — the cautionary tale.

I should know. I was one. Different fire. Same corner.
