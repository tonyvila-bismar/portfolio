The Appraisal

by Tess Jyanssen

Lienne of the Altus Plateau made Crucible talismans for forty-one years, and in that time she produced somewhere between six hundred and eight hundred objects (the records disagree, and Lienne herself kept books the way a river keeps its banks: approximately, and only when it felt like it). She worked in horn, bone, scale, and a material she called veinstone, which was calcite threaded with traces of Erdtree amber that formed naturally in the limestone caverns east of the capital. The veinstone was the signature. Nobody else used it. Nobody else could get it.

Lienne had an arrangement with the cave-tenders of the eastern Altus, and arrangements with Lienne were not things you renegotiated, because Lienne did not renegotiate. She set a price, the price held, and if you didn't like the price you were welcome to find your own caverns and your own tenders and spend your own twelve years learning which veins yielded amber and which yielded calcium dust that crumbled in your hands.

(She was, in other words, my kind of woman.)

Her talismans were not decorative. That is the first thing to understand, because the word "talisman" in the Lands Between has drifted since the reclassification into meaning something between "jewelry" and "charm," and Lienne would have put her fist through a wall if you'd called her work either of those things. A Crucible talisman was a functional object. It captured a specific aspect of the Crucible's primordial life and held it in stable form, so that the bearer could channel that aspect without the biological cost of manifesting it directly.

You wanted a tail sweep without growing a tail? You wore the Aspect of the Crucible: Tail, and the talisman did the intermediary work. Your body stayed human. The aspect flowed through the object. Clean, contained, elegant.

The engineering was extraordinary. Not because the end product was complex (most talismans were palm-sized, smooth, warm to the touch) but because the process required the artisan to understand the Crucible at a material level that made most theologians uncomfortable. You had to know what a horn actually was. Not symbolically. Not liturgically. You had to know the mineral composition, the growth patterns, the electromagnetic resonance that linked a living horn to the Erdtree's root system. Lienne knew. She knew the way an engineer knows load-bearing tolerances: practically, specifically, without mysticism, and with enormous respect for the forces involved.

She was not a priestess. She was not a theologian. She was a craftsman. The distinction mattered to her and it should matter to you.

Her workshop occupied two rooms above a tanner's shop on the Outer Wall of Leyndell, which tells you something about both her status and her economics. Crucible artisans were respected but not wealthy. The comparison (forgive me, I cannot help myself) is to gunsmiths in a pre-industrial military state: everyone agreed the product was essential, nobody wanted to pay what it actually cost to produce, and the artisan's social position hovered in that awkward zone between "valued" and "compensated."

Lienne charged by the aspect. A horn talisman ran about four hundred runes in the late Crucible period. A tail ran six hundred (the aspect geometry was more complex; tails required a dynamic joint system that horns, being structurally rigid, did not). Wings were the most expensive. Lienne made exactly three wing talismans in her career and charged two thousand runes each, and even at that price she was undervaluing her own labor by a factor of roughly ten.

I know this because her ledgers survived. Eleven of them. They are in a private collection now (I will get to that), and I have seen six, and they are the most meticulous financial records I have ever encountered outside of a Gabrielite counting-house. Every transaction dated. Every material cost itemized. Every client named. The handwriting is small and even and consistent and slopes slightly leftward, which in a right-handed person indicates either fatigue or a deliberate attempt to slow down, and Lienne was deliberate about everything.

The ledgers span the full forty-one years. They begin in the late Crucible period, when Lienne was twenty-three and had just finished her apprenticeship under a horn-specialist named Torvald, and they end in the fourteenth year of the Golden Order's consolidation, when Lienne was sixty-four, and they document, entry by meticulous entry, the complete destruction of a market.

I need to explain how the market worked before I can explain how it died.

In the Crucible period, aspect talismans were standard military and liturgical equipment. The Crucible Knights carried them. The temple attendants used them in ceremony. Ordinary citizens wore minor talismans (single-aspect, limited channel capacity) the way you or I might carry a ward against weather or a compass keyed to the nearest grace. The market was stable, demand was consistent, supply was constrained by the number of artisans with the skill to do the work (never more than about forty active practitioners in the greater Leyndell region at any given time), and prices reflected the genuine cost of materials and expertise.

Lienne's revenue in a typical year of the late Crucible period: between twelve and fifteen thousand runes. Comfortable. Not lavish. Enough to keep the workshop, pay the tanner for the space, maintain the arrangement with the cave-tenders, and set aside a small reserve for the years when the veinstone veins ran thin.

Then the Golden Order established itself, and the Erdtree matured, and the Crucible became "a prior state of the sacred tree, now surpassed," and the aspects became "vestiges," and the vestiges became "impurities," and the impurities became contraband.

It didn't happen overnight. That is the thing people get wrong when they talk about the reclassification. They imagine a single decree, a single moment, a line drawn in the golden sand. It wasn't like that. It was a price curve.

Year one of the consolidation: no change. Lienne's ledger shows twelve thousand, four hundred runes in revenue. Same clients. Same orders. Same margins.

Year two: eleven thousand, eight hundred. A slight dip. Two regular clients did not return. One of them (a Crucible Knight named Aldren, who had commissioned a horn talisman annually for eleven years) was reassigned to a garrison in the Weeping Peninsula and simply fell out of the supply chain. The other stopped ordering without explanation.

Year three: ten thousand, two hundred. Lienne notes in the margin (the only non-financial notation in any of the eleven surviving ledgers): "Temple order cancelled. No reason given."

Year four: seven thousand. The temple orders stopped entirely. The Crucible Knights were still ordering, but at reduced volume. The liturgical market had evaporated. Lienne raised her prices for the first time in nineteen years, not because her costs had increased but because fewer clients meant each remaining client had to carry more of the fixed overhead. This is standard small-workshop economics and Lienne understood it instinctively.

Year five: four thousand, three hundred. Two more Crucible Knights reassigned. One client (a minor noble whose family had carried aspect talismans for four generations) sent a servant to return three talismans and request a refund. Lienne records the refund in full: twelve hundred runes. She does not comment.

Year six: two thousand, nine hundred. Lienne moved out of the workshop above the tanner's shop. She relocated to a single room in the Outer Wall's lower quarter. The ledger notes the new rent: forty runes per month, down from one hundred and ten.

Year seven: one thousand, four hundred. Four clients. All private. No institutional orders of any kind. The Fundaments of Sacred Morphology was published this year. Lienne does not mention it in the ledger. But the prices she charges her remaining clients are now double what she charged in the Crucible period. The market had inverted: fewer buyers, but the buyers who remained wanted the objects badly enough to pay a scarcity premium. This is what happens to every prohibited good. The demand does not disappear. It goes underground, and the price goes up, and the artisan who once worked in daylight now works in a back room with the shutters drawn.

Year eight: nine hundred runes. Three clients.

Year nine: six hundred. Two clients. One of them was purchasing a wing talisman. Lienne's fourth. She charged three thousand runes, which is why the yearly total is six hundred: the wing sale happened in arrears, paid across two years, and the ledger records it scrupulously. The other client bought a single horn talisman for two hundred runes, which was half what Lienne had charged in the Crucible period, because this client was young and terrified and Lienne (I am reading between the lines here, but I am confident) did not have it in her to charge full price to someone who flinched when the door opened.

I should tell you what I think happened in year nine. I think the young client was an Omen. I think the talisman was not for channeling an aspect but for something else entirely: for holding the memory of what the aspect had been before the Golden Order declared it a disease. I have no evidence for this. The ledger says "horn, standard, single-aspect, 200r, client K."

But the price is wrong. Lienne never sold a horn talisman for two hundred runes. Not once in forty-one years. The only explanation that makes sense to me (and I have spent time with these numbers, and I know what a discount looks like when someone cannot bear to charge what the work is worth) is that she looked at client K and saw something that made the price irrelevant.

Years ten through thirteen are thin. Annual revenue between three hundred and seven hundred runes. The clients are unnamed after year ten. The ledger simply records the object, the aspect, the price, and a single initial. The handwriting does not change. The margins do not deteriorate. Lienne's discipline held.

Year fourteen. The last entry.

One talisman. Aspect: unspecified. Client: none. Price: none.

That is the entry. In its entirety. Object made, aspect not named, client not recorded, price not charged. Lienne's ledger, which had maintained a perfect accounting of every transaction for forty-one years, which had recorded refunds and rent payments and the cost of cave-tender arrangements down to the single rune, ends with an object she made for no one, or for herself, or for something she could not bring herself to write down.

The ledger closes after that entry. The remaining pages are blank.

Here is what I know about the object itself, because it survived, and because I held it.

It is palm-sized. Veinstone, threaded with Erdtree amber so dense the whole piece glows faintly gold in low light. The surface is carved in a pattern I did not recognize until I turned it in my hands three times and then held it very still.

It is a horn formation. Specifically: two horns curving toward each other from opposite sides, reaching upward, almost meeting at the crown. The gap between the tips is barely visible. You have to look to see it.

I did not know what the formation meant when I first held it. I learned later. Others have written about it. The open-crown formation: devotion that reaches and reaches and never arrives. An Omen's horns growing toward each other above the skull, four inches short of closure.

She made a talisman of it.

Not to channel. Not to sell. She made an aspect-talisman of an Omen's horns, carved in veinstone, in the fourteenth year of the order that had declared those horns a defilement, in a back room with the shutters drawn, for no client and no price, and the aspect she captured was not horn, not tail, not wing. The aspect was the gap.

This is the part where I am supposed to tell you what the object is worth.

I am very good at telling you what things are worth. It is, arguably, the only thing I am genuinely good at (that and running a circus and negotiating port tariffs and identifying load-bearing lies in trade agreements, but those are all variations on the same skill, which is: knowing what things cost). I have appraised Jyanssen antiquities for three foreign governments. I have priced siege engines. I once put a fair market value on a live gryphon. I can price anything.

I cannot price this.

The collection where Lienne's ledgers and her final talisman now reside belongs to a family in the Altus foothills who acquired them through a chain of custody I will not detail here because some of the links are technically illegal and I am fond of the current owners. They let me examine the objects over two days. I sat in their parlor with the eleven ledgers spread across a table built for six, and I read the numbers, and I watched a market die.

It is a strange thing, reading a market's death in someone else's handwriting. You see the curve before the person living it did. You watch year three's margin note ("Temple order cancelled. No reason given.") and you know what she did not know yet: that the temple orders were never coming back. That the Crucible Knights would be next. That the polite noble sending a servant to return three talismans was not an anomaly but a leading indicator. You can see the whole collapse from above, the way you can see a river's course from a hilltop, and Lienne was in the river, and her handwriting never wavered.

She kept making things. That is the part I cannot get past.

The market was dying. Her revenue dropped from fifteen thousand to nine hundred to three hundred. Her workshop shrank from two rooms to one room to (I suspect, though the ledger does not confirm it) a corner of someone else's room. And she kept making things. Horn talismans for unnamed clients at prices that would not cover her materials. A wing talisman paid in installments by someone who might not live long enough to finish paying. And then, at the end, an object for no one.

A craftsman who keeps working after the market has declared her craft worthless is making an argument. Not a political argument. Not a theological one. Lienne was not a protester. She was not Morgott, standing in the rain for a principle. She was making the argument that her hands made: that the thing she knew how to do was real, that the aspects were real, that the horn and the tail and the wing and the gap were real, and that the Golden Order's reclassification of them as impurities was a pricing error.

A pricing error.

I know how that sounds. I know it sounds reductive, even offensive, to describe the systematic persecution of everyone born with Crucible traits as a "pricing error." But I am asking you to take me seriously for a moment, because I think this is actually the most precise way to say it, and precision matters when you are talking about what things cost.

The Golden Order looked at the Crucible and decided its aspects had no value. Less than no value: negative value, contamination, something that had to be removed at the cost of a child's pain and an eight-percent mortality rate. They didn't just refuse to buy what Lienne was selling. They reclassified the entire asset class.

Sacred became profane. Divine became pathological. The eleven words for scale transition became "dermal anomaly, partial." And Lienne's talismans, which had been essential military and liturgical technology, became evidence of a crime.

That is not theology. That is a market correction driven by a change in the regulatory environment, and I have seen it before (I have

lived

it before, when Tower Jyanssen fell and every object with our name on it went from heirloom to liability overnight), and it is always the same: the thing does not change, the price changes, and everyone pretends the price was always this, that the thing was always worthless, that nobody ever valued it.

Lienne's ledgers are proof that they did. Twelve thousand runes a year. Forty active practitioners in the Leyndell region. Crucible Knights carrying her work into battle. Temples ordering her talismans for ceremony. The demand was real. The value was real. And then the demand evaporated, not because the product failed, but because the institution that had been the primary buyer decided to reclassify the product as poison.

I sat in that parlor for two days. I read the numbers. I held the final talisman in my hands and turned it until the veinstone caught the light and the amber veins glowed gold and the two horns reached toward each other and did not touch, and I thought: she captured it.

Forty-one years of watching a market die, and at the end, she made the one thing that could not be sold. She made the aspect of the gap. She made the shape of everything the Golden Order threw away, held it in calcite and amber, and she did not write down the price because the price was not the point, and Lienne of the Altus Plateau, who had kept perfect books for four decades, knew the difference.

The talisman sits in a glass case in the parlor in the foothills. The family does not know what it is. They think it is decorative. They think the horns are abstract, stylized, purely ornamental. They have never looked closely enough to see the gap.

I looked. I saw it. I did not tell them what it was worth, because I do not know, and because some things are not improved by being priced, and because the last honest entry in Lienne's ledger is the one with no number in the margin.

Aspect: unspecified. Client: none. Price: none.

She knew exactly what she was making. She just couldn't find the words for it in any language the market still spoke.
