The Certification Problem

by Kontextmaschine

The thing that's been bugging me about the Radahn Festival is not the part where a bunch of strangers agree to fight a god who's been eating corpses in a swamp for however long it's been since Caelid turned red. That part is straightforward — people do insane things for glory, always have, the ballads practically write themselves. The part that's been bugging me is the logistics. Specifically: who told them about it? How did word get out? There's no post office. The Roundtable Hold is a pocket dimension that only some Tarnished can access and half of them are trying to kill the other half. The erdtree glows but it doesn't have a bulletin board. Jerren — one guy, a guest commander whose authority derives entirely from a personal oath to a man who no longer recognizes him — somehow organized a multinational invitational combat tournament in the middle of a wasteland where the rain gives you cancer.

That's not a funeral rite. That's event planning.

And event planning requires infrastructure that the Lands Between conspicuously does not have, which means somebody improvised, and the improvisation is the interesting part — because what Jerren actually built, without apparently intending to, is a credentialing institution.

Think about the Tarnished economy for a second. You die. You come back. Grace points you in a direction. You fight things. If you're good enough, you fight bigger things. If you're extraordinarily good, you fight a demigod and take their Great Rune, which is itself a fragment of a cosmic operating system that was shattered by the god who built it. At no point in this process does anyone check your qualifications.

There's no licensing board. The Two Fingers don't issue certificates of competency — they can barely communicate at all, and the Finger Readers who interpret their twitches have a reliability rate that the seminary itself prefers not to quantify too precisely. Your Finger Maiden, if you even have one, is a chaplain-slash-power-supply, not a supervisor. Nobody is evaluating your readiness. Nobody is tracking your progress. You just keep killing things until you either become Elden Lord or stop coming back.

This is, to put it in the gentlest possible terms, an accountability vacuum.

The Roundtable Hold tries to fill it — Gideon collects intelligence, the Two Fingers nominally approve candidates, Hewg keeps your weapons sharp — but the Hold is less a governing body than a coworking space with a weapons rack. Nobody in the Hold can actually tell you whether you're ready to take on Radahn or Malenia or Morgott. They can give you information. They cannot give you a credential. And without credentials, you have a labor market where every participant's only proof of competence is the fact that they're still alive, which is a pretty low bar in a world where death is temporary.

Enter Jerren.

What Jerren did — and I genuinely don't think he understood this at the time — is create the first performance-based credentialing event in the post-Shattering Lands Between. The Festival isn't a funeral. It isn't even primarily a mercy killing, though that's how Jerren frames it, because Jerren is a man of honor and men of honor need noble reasons for the things they organize. What it actually is, functionally, structurally, is a sorting mechanism.

You show up at Redmane Castle. You walk through a gate into Caelid's most actively hostile combat zone. You fight General Radahn — a demigod so powerful that he held back the stars themselves, now reduced to a feral colossus crawling through rot-swamp eating the dead, which honestly makes him more dangerous, not less, because a Radahn with tactical intelligence might spare you but a Radahn running on hunger and muscle memory will not.

If you survive: congratulations. You now have a credential.

Not a diploma, not a title, not a formal certification — but something more valuable in a world without institutions: a story that other people can verify. "I fought at the Radahn Festival" is a sentence that means something to anyone who knows what the Radahn Festival is, and Jerren made sure everyone knows what the Radahn Festival is, because that's what the event planning was for. The word-of-mouth, the invitations, the spectacle of the thing — it's not marketing, or rather, it is marketing, but the product being marketed is the credential itself. The Festival's reputation is what makes surviving it meaningful.

This is not a new invention. This is how credentialing has always worked when institutions don't exist or have collapsed.

Medieval tournaments — the actual ones, not the jousting-for-a-lady's-favor nonsense — were credentialing events for landless knights. You showed up. You fought. If you won, you took the loser's horse and armor (literal prize money) and you got a reputation. The reputation was the credential. A knight who'd won at three tournaments was hireable. A knight who'd won at none was not. The tournaments were organized by individual nobles — not by any central authority — and the credentialing function emerged from the spectacle, not from any deliberate institutional design.

Jerren is William Marshal. He probably doesn't know it.

The deeper structural joke is that the Festival solves a problem the Golden Order created and never acknowledged: the Tarnished have no institutional support. They were exiled. They died in foreign wars. They were resurrected and called back and pointed at demigods and told "become Elden Lord or don't, we genuinely do not have a process for this." The Two Fingers' guidance amounts to "go to the erdtree," which is roughly as useful as telling a med school applicant "go to the hospital."

The entire Tarnished pipeline is built on the assumption that the worthy will self-select through survival, which is the same logic that says the free market doesn't need regulation because bad companies will just go bankrupt. It works in theory. In practice it produces a lot of corpses and no useful information about who's actually qualified.

The Festival introduces information. That's its real function. After the Festival, the surviving Tarnished are no longer undifferentiated — they're Tarnished who fought Radahn. The ones who didn't attend are, by implication, Tarnished who didn't. The credential creates a hierarchy where none existed, and hierarchies, whatever else you want to say about them, produce legibility. Suddenly you can tell who's who. Suddenly the market has grades.

And then Radahn dies, and the credential becomes even more interesting, because now nobody can ever earn it again.

Jerren's Festival is a one-time event by design — the oath was to give Radahn a warrior's death, and once that death occurs, the oath is fulfilled and the institution dissolves. But the credential persists. Everyone who fought at the Festival carries that credential forever. Everyone who didn't can never get it. You've got a finite, unreproducible, permanently closed credentialing event, which means the Festival veterans become a de facto aristocracy — not because anyone intended it, not because Jerren wanted to create a new noble class, but because that's what happens when you create a credential and then destroy the mechanism for issuing it.

Same thing happened with every first-generation revolutionary movement in human history. The guys who were at the Continental Congress? Permanent aristocracy. The guys who stormed the Bastille? Permanent aristocracy. The credential is "I was there," and "there" no longer exists, so nobody born after can ever qualify. Jerren accidentally invented the Lands Between's equivalent of the Society of the Cincinnati, except instead of a gold eagle pin you get "I fought a god and lived" as your calling card at every social function for the rest of your undying life.

And this is where it gets weird, because the Tarnished are undying.

The credential never expires because the credential-holder never expires. In a mortal society, first-generation credentials naturally dilute — the revolutionary generation dies, their children inherit the prestige but not the experience, and within three generations the credential is a family name and a portrait in the hallway. But Tarnished don't die. A Festival veteran is a Festival veteran forever. The credential doesn't just not expire — it appreciates, because as Radahn recedes into history, "I fought him" becomes increasingly rare and increasingly unverifiable, which means the claim itself becomes the credential, which means we're back to a world where proof of competence is "trust me."

Jerren solved the accountability vacuum for about forty-five minutes and then recreated it in a form that's even harder to challenge, because now it's wrapped in glory.

Nobody set out to build this. Jerren wanted to honor an oath. The Tarnished wanted to fight something big. The Festival emerged from individual motivations and produced institutional consequences that nobody designed and nobody controls and nobody can undo, because you can't un-credential someone whose credential is that they survived.

Who credentials the credentialer? Jerren's authority comes from his oath. His oath comes from Radahn. Radahn is dead. The authority chain terminates in a corpse — which, in the Lands Between, is less of a problem than it sounds, but still.
