The Consolidator Of Limgrave

by Eitan

The Consolidator of Limgrave

K.J. Parker

I was re-stitching a gambeson sleeve when they brought me the contract for the walking tomb.

Re-stitching, I should say, because the first time I did it I used a herringbone that pulled apart under any lateral stress worth mentioning, and the second time I used a backstitch that held beautifully but puckered the linen so badly it wouldn't lie flat under a breastplate. Third time I was trying a couching stitch I'd seen a Carian seamster use once, years ago, holding the padding in place with a second thread laid perpendicular. Very elegant, if you can keep your tension even. Which, on that particular morning, I could not, because a man I'd never met was standing in my doorway telling me I'd been requisitioned.

"Requisitioned," I said.

"By Kenneth Haight."

"Who is Kenneth Haight?"

The man looked at me the way you look at someone who has just asked what rain is. "He's the rightful lord of Limgrave."

"Is he."

"He says so."

"And what does Kenneth Haight want with a consolidator?"

"There's a problem with one of the walking tombs."

I put down the gambeson, because my hands had gone cold. The walking tombs — Wandering Mausoleums, if you want the proper term, which nobody does — are something I had spent my entire professional career very carefully not being near. A consolidator deals in stone. Specifically, a consolidator deals in stone that is falling apart: foundations, retaining walls, load-bearing columns that have developed the kind of cracks that make you lie awake at night calculating distributed loads on your fingers. I am, by most reasonable accounts, the best consolidator currently practicing in southern Limgrave, and by most reasonable accounts this means I am the fourteenth or fifteenth best in the Lands Between, assuming seven of the people ahead of me are still alive. Which, given the Shattering, is optimistic.

(The Shattering was hard on the building trades. Not because buildings stopped needing repair — quite the opposite — but because the people who paid for repairs stopped existing in any organized sense. A lord with a crumbling curtain wall used to send to the Studium or the relevant guild chapter for an assessed contractor. Now a lord with a crumbling curtain wall is, nine times out of ten, a corpse or a madman or a Tarnished who wandered in last Tuesday and doesn't know what a curtain wall is. The work is there. The institutional framework that turns work into pay is not.)

The walking tomb had stopped walking. This, the man explained with studied patience as we rode north along the road — and I should mention that the horse he'd brought for me was a sway-backed chestnut with a gait like a butter churn — had created something of a civic problem, because it had stopped walking directly on top of what had been the main track between the Church of Elleh and the gatefront. Traffic, such as it was, had to detour through a stretch of low ground that was crawling with the rotting soldiers that Godrick's people had dumped there and that had, in the way of things, refused to stay properly dead.

"Why doesn't your lord just go around?"

"Lord Haight says the roads must be restored. Civilization depends on infrastructure." He said this with the weary precision of a man who has repeated it many times. "Also, the thing's shedding."

"Shedding what?"

"Stone."

That got my attention. Walking tombs are, structurally, some of the most remarkable things in the Lands Between — and I say this as a man who has spent forty years staring at remarkable structures. The basic engineering is, or should be, impossible. A bell-shaped mass of fitted stone the size of a castle, supported on four legs, moving under its own volition across terrain that would challenge a moderately determined goat. The stone itself is a dense grey limestone of a type I've never seen quarried anywhere in Limgrave or Liurnia. The joints are dry-fitted — no mortar, no pins — and yet the thing walks, and has been walking for centuries, and the joints hold.

Or held. Until now, apparently.

I arrived at the site around noon. The walking tomb was exactly where they'd said: planted in the middle of the road like an enormous stone turnip, its four legs folded underneath it, its bell rising maybe sixty feet to the flat summit where the mausoleum chamber sat. Around its base, a ring of debris — fallen facing stones, crumbled mouldings, chunks of what had once been decorative blind arcading. The headless knights who guard these things were still circling, their armored feet crunching through their own detritus.

Kenneth Haight was a tall man with excellent posture and inadequate footwear. He had the bearing of someone who had been told from birth that he was important and had never encountered sufficient evidence to the contrary. He was also, I noticed, genuinely distressed.

"There she is," he said, gesturing at the tomb the way you'd gesture at a sick relative. "She just — stopped. Three days ago. And the stones keep falling."

"She?"

"One must call them something."

I walked a circuit. The headless knights ignored me, which I took as a good sign. Their wing-shaped pauldrons caught the light as they walked, and I tried not to look at the stumps above their gorgets where heads had once been. I've seen worse, working in the catacombs. But catacomb spirits don't actively patrol.

The damage was extensive and, to my professional eye, deeply strange. The outer facing stones — a fine-grained ashlar, beautifully dressed, close-jointed — were delaminating from the core. Not crumbling. Delaminating. The surface was peeling away in sheets, the way certain schists do under frost action, except this wasn't schist and there hadn't been frost. Underneath, the structural core was a rubble fill bonded with something that wasn't mortar. It was darker, almost organic-looking, and where it was exposed to the air it wept a yellowish fluid that smelled faintly of temple incense.

I touched it. Cold. Not cold the way stone is cold — cold the way a living thing is cold when it shouldn't be. Cold with intent.

"How long have you been the rightful lord of Limgrave?" I asked, not because I cared but because I needed to think about something else for a moment.

"Since birth. The Haight family has administered these lands since before the Shattering."

"And in that time, has anyone ever successfully repaired a walking tomb?"

He blinked. "I assumed — you're the consolidator."

"I consolidate buildings. Things that sit still. Things with foundations." I pointed at the nearest leg, which was thicker than a mature oak and appeared to be a single piece of stone, though I knew that was impossible. "This is not a building."

"It's made of stone."

"A river is made of water. That doesn't make it a well."

He looked at me with the particular expression of a man who is paying for expertise and receiving philosophy. I sympathized. I have worn that expression myself, usually while some engineer explains why my proposed repair will not work and his alternative will cost three times as much.

I examined the delamination more closely. The ashlar facing was separating not at the joints but through the body of the stone itself. Each block was splitting along a plane roughly an eighth of an inch in from the outer face. Consistent depth. Consistent across every block I checked. That's not natural weathering. Natural weathering is irregular, preferential along bedding planes and frost lines. This was systematic, as though something inside the tomb had decided it no longer wanted its skin.

(I should explain about the skin of a building. In proper masonry construction, the outer face is not decorative. Or rather, it is decorative, but it is also functional — the dressed surface sheds water, resists wind, and protects the structural core from thermal cycling. The relationship between face and core is symbiotic. When the face fails, the core deteriorates rapidly. When the core shifts, the face cracks. They are married, in the old term. A consolidator's job is essentially marriage counseling for stone.)

I am not a coward. I want to be clear about that. But I appreciate the value of fear the way I appreciate the value of a plumb line — it tells you when something is wrong. And standing at the base of that tomb, looking at the weeping core and the peeling ashlar and the headless knights walking their mindless circuit, I was afraid in a way that had nothing to do with physical danger and everything to do with the suspicion that I was looking at something I did not understand and that my not understanding it was the point.

"I can stabilize the facing," I told Haight. "Temporarily. You'll need bronze cramps — do you have a smith?"

"I have Hewg. Well, I don't have him. Nobody has him. But I can send to the Hold."

"Hewg's at the Roundtable Hold. I need someone here."

"There's a smithing hut near the Gatefront. Unmanned, but equipped."

"I'll need help. Someone who can work a forge."

"I'll find someone."

He found me a Tarnished.

I want to say this clearly, because I've heard various opinions and most of them are wrong: I have nothing against the Tarnished as a class of people. They were called back. They didn't ask to be here any more than I did. Most of the ones I've met have been polite enough, in the stunned, slightly concussed way of people who died and woke up in a place they don't recognize. But working with them is like working with a man who has had a very bad head wound. They're present, they're functional, they just seem slightly displaced from the moment, as though they're hearing music you can't.

This one was a woman. Short, wide in the shoulder, hands like a bricklayer's. She said her name was Threnn and that she'd been a farrier before she died, which was better than nothing. She could work a forge. She could draw bronze rod to the gauge I needed. She asked surprisingly few questions.

The cramps were straightforward. Bronze, because bronze doesn't rust and because it has enough give to accommodate minor movement without cracking. You drill into the ashlar on either side of a delamination line, seat the cramp in lead, and tamp it home. A child could do it. The drilling is the hard part — you need to match the angle of the bedding plane so the cramp pulls the face back against the core without introducing a shear stress that will crack the block. I've done it a thousand times. On buildings that don't walk.

The first day's work went well. Threnn handed me tools with the quiet efficiency of someone accustomed to working alongside another person's hands, and I drilled and set forty-seven cramps along the tomb's eastern face, which was the worst affected. The headless knights watched but did not interfere. By evening, the delamination on the eastern side had arrested.

By morning, it had migrated to the western side.

I stood looking at it for a while. The eastern face was holding — my cramps were good, the lead seating was solid, the ashlar was pulled tight. But the western face, which had been merely cracked the day before, was now peeling in the same systematic way, at the same depth, with the same yellowish weeping from the core.

"It's moving," Threnn said.

"Yes."

"Not the tomb. The — the coming-apart. It's moving."

"I know."

She was quiet for a moment. "In the village where I worked, before, there was a farrier's horse that got colic. Every time the horse doctor treated the stomach, the symptoms moved to the bowel. When he treated the bowel, they moved to the lungs. The horse died."

"This is a building."

"It's not, though. Is it."

She was right, and I knew she was right, and I liked her for saying it plainly rather than letting me pretend. The walking tombs are not buildings. They are containers for something — call it a purpose, call it a will — that I, in my professional capacity, am not qualified to assess. I consolidate stone. I do not consolidate intent.

But Kenneth Haight was paying me — in actual coin, pre-Shattering mint, a luxury I had not enjoyed in some time — and the road needed clearing, and I had said I would do the job. So I consolidated.

Three days. Every morning I'd find the previous day's work intact and a new section delaminating. The pattern was consistent: wherever I stabilized one area, an adjacent area failed. The tomb was shedding its skin in sequence, and I was chasing the damage around the circumference like a man trying to catch a leak in a barrel by plugging holes while new ones opened.

On the evening of the third day, I sat on a broken moulding with a cup of the dreadful ale Haight's people brewed and watched the headless knights walk their circuit. Their armour was old. Very old. The metalwork was a type of articulated plate I'd seen only in manuscript illustrations — long-obsolete, associated with the era before the Golden Order standardized military equipment. The eclipsed sun on their shields was hammered in silver and black.

"Do you know what's inside?" I asked Haight. He'd been hovering all day, alternating between enthusiastic encouragement and transparent anxiety.

"A demigod. A soulless one."

"Soulless."

"The body lives. The soul is gone. The mausoleums carry them."

"Why?"

"I don't know. Preservation, perhaps. Or penance. The knights cut off their own heads to serve."

I looked at the nearest knight. The stump of his neck was clean — no ragged flesh, no bone splinter. It looked almost surgical. The wing-shaped ornaments on his pauldrons caught the last light and flashed like a signal.

"The tomb stopped walking," I said, thinking out loud. "And then the facing started failing. Not before. After."

"Yes."

"What if it's the other way round? What if the facing started failing, and that's why it stopped?"

"Does it matter?"

It mattered immensely, but I didn't have the language to explain why. In consolidation, the first question is always: what is the load path? Where does the weight go? In a conventional structure, load passes from the roof through the walls to the foundations to the ground. If the load path is interrupted — by a cracked wall, a subsided foundation — the structure fails above the interruption. You find the interruption, you fix it, the load path is restored.

In the walking tomb, the load path ran through the legs and into the ground. But the legs weren't failing. The legs were perfect — not a crack, not a shift. The core wasn't failing either, not structurally; it was weeping that strange fluid but it was solid, dense, integrated. Only the facing was failing, and the facing bore almost no load. It was cosmetic. A skin.

You don't die of skin failure. Unless the skin is doing something more than you think.

On the fourth morning, I went underneath.

I don't recommend this. The space beneath a walking tomb is dark, cramped, and haunted by the absolute certainty that the thing above you weighs more than you can conceptualize and is held up by legs that have, historically, been known to move. But the underside is where the legs meet the body, and I needed to see the connection.

Threnn held the lantern. The joint where each leg entered the body of the tomb was a marvel — a massive ball-and-socket arrangement, the ball at the top of the leg seated in a socket carved into the base of the bell. The surfaces were ground smooth, smoother than anything I could achieve with my best abrasives. They had the sheen of glintstone. And they were dry.

Every other joint in the tomb wept that yellowish fluid. These were dry.

"It's like a joint in a body," Threnn said. "Needs fluid to move."

"Yes."

"And the fluid's coming out through the walls instead."

"Yes."

I sat under the tomb, in the dark, and thought about what I knew. I knew the facing stones were delaminating at a consistent depth. I knew the core was weeping fluid through the exposed surfaces. I knew the ball-and-socket joints were dry. I knew the tomb had stopped moving before the symptoms appeared, or possibly the symptoms had appeared before the tomb stopped moving, and I knew that every time I sealed one area, the fluid found another path out.

Here is what I understood: the tomb was not failing. It was grieving.

I am aware of how that sounds. I am a consolidator. I deal in lime mortars and bronze cramps and the coefficient of thermal expansion of Liurnian limestone. I do not traffic in metaphor. But the walking tombs carry the soulless bodies of demigods, and those bodies are — or were — sustained by some connection I am not equipped to name. The fluid in the core was part of that sustenance. It circulated, it lubricated the joints, it maintained the bond between face and core. And something had interrupted it. The tomb had stopped walking because the circulation had failed, and the circulation had failed because — because what?

I went to look at the mausoleum chamber.

The climb was inadvisable. The delaminating ashlar was unstable, and the headless knights took exception to my presence above a certain height. Threnn distracted two of them by standing very still near the eastern face while I scrambled up the western side, using my cramp holes as hand- and footholds. Consolidation is hard on the body. I am fifty-three years old and my knees sound like a bag of walnuts being stepped on. By the time I reached the summit I was breathing in a way that suggested my lungs had filed a formal objection.

The chamber at the top was small — a single room, open-sided, with a stone bier at the centre. On the bier lay a body.

I have seen bodies. In the catacombs, in the ruins, on the roads. Bodies are the landscape's most common feature these days. This one was different because it was intact — no decomposition, no rot, no damage. A young man in fine armour, hands folded on his chest, eyes closed. He looked as though he were sleeping, except that he had no presence. A sleeping person radiates something — warmth, breath, the faint electromagnetic hum of a working mind. This body radiated nothing. It was a container with nothing inside.

The soulless demigod.

The bier was cracked.

One single crack, running diagonally from the upper left corner to the lower right, passing directly underneath the body. The crack was fresh — sharp-edged, unweathered. And at the crack's deepest point, maybe three-quarters of an inch wide, I could see that the bier was hollow. Inside the hollow, something glinted.

I am a consolidator. I assess damage. I do not investigate mysteries. But I was up there, and my knees were not going to tolerate a second climb, so I looked.

The bier was a shell over a cavity, and in the cavity was a small rune-inscribed stone, roughly the size and shape of a walnut. It was cracked clean in half. The two pieces sat slightly apart, no longer touching, and between them — nothing. A gap. An interruption.

The tomb's rune was broken.

Not the Great Runes of the demigods — those were claimed in the Shattering. This was something smaller, more specific. A governing rune, perhaps. The instruction set that told the tomb to walk, the joints to circulate, the facing to hold. The thing that made the stone more than stone. And it had cracked, and the crack had propagated outward through every system in the structure, and now the tomb sat on the road weeping its vital fluid through its disintegrating skin.

I could not repair it. I want to be clear about that. I can repair stone. I cannot repair runes. I cannot repair the animating principle of a funeral edifice older than the Golden Order itself. I can put in cramps and I can seal surfaces and I can, given enough time and bronze and lead, hold the facing in place indefinitely. But the tomb will not walk. The joints will remain dry. The body on the bier will continue to lie there with its soulless composure, waiting for a restoration that I am not competent to provide.

I told Kenneth Haight. He took it with more grace than I expected.

"Can you at least get the facing to hold? So it stops shedding onto my road?"

"I can."

"Then do that."

"It won't walk again."

"I know."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "There are people in this land who have lost everything. Their lords, their families, the grace that was supposed to guide them. And they still get up in the morning. They still walk the roads, such as they are. If the tomb can't walk, then it will be a landmark instead. People will use it to give directions. 'Turn left at the mausoleum.' That's a form of service too."

It was the most sensible thing I'd heard since the Shattering.

The work took eleven days. I sealed every exposed surface with a lime-putty render over a galvanized bronze mesh — my own technique, developed for waterproofing cisterns in Liurnia — and cramped the remaining ashlar so thoroughly that nothing short of a direct siege engine hit would shift it. Threnn worked the forge. The headless knights walked their circuit. Kenneth Haight checked on us every morning and said encouraging things that made me want to throw a chisel at him, which is the hallmark of a good client.

On the last day, Threnn asked me about the body.

"Is it really a demigod?"

"So I'm told."

"Which one?"

"I don't know. Could be any of the soulless ones. There are more than you'd think." I was packing my tools — two chisels (tungsten-tipped, irreplaceable), a pointing trowel, a hawk, a lump hammer, a cold chisel, a hand drill with six bits (three of which were worn past the point of usefulness), a plumb bob, a spirit level made of brass and rosewood that my master gave me when I graduated, a ball of twine, a straightedge, and a bag of dry lime putty. The material sum of my professional life, not counting the gambeson I'd never finished re-stitching.

"Do you think it matters? Who it is?"

"To the knights, yes. They cut off their heads for whoever's in there."

"The knights don't have heads to think with."

"No. But they have purpose. That's harder to cut off."

She nodded. She had the look of someone working through a thought that was larger than the conversation. Most Tarnished have that look, sooner or later.

"Before I died," she said, "I shod horses. That was my whole life. Two hundred horses a year, give or take, for twelve years. I was good at it. Not brilliant — I knew a woman in the Altus Plateau who could shoe a draft horse in eighteen minutes, and I never got below twenty-two. But I was good. And when I came back, my hands remembered. Everything else was different, but my hands knew what to do."

"Yes."

"Is that what the tomb is? Something whose body remembers the work, even though the — the thing that made it work is broken?"

I thought about it. The tomb, sitting on the road with its sealed skin and its dry joints and its cracked rune. The headless knights, walking their eternal circuit around a structure that would never move again, guarding a body that would never wake. The facing stones, cramped into place by my bronze and my lead, holding a surface that no longer needed to be held because there was nothing underneath driving it apart.

"Probably," I said. "Yes."

She paid me her half of the ale bill, which I thought was generous, considering I'd drunk most of it. I gathered my tools. I walked south.

The tomb stood on the road behind me, solid and still, a monument to someone whose name I never learned, repaired by a consolidator of no particular distinction using techniques designed for cisterns, in a world where the fundamental laws of reality had been shattered by people with considerably more power than sense. The headless knights circled. The road ran north and south. Kenneth Haight would tell people to turn left at the mausoleum.

I got about a mile before I looked at my hands. There was a faint discoloration on my left palm, where I'd touched the weeping core that first day. Yellowish. Slightly warm.

That was three months ago. It hasn't faded.

But I'm sure it's nothing. The body remembers all kinds of things.
