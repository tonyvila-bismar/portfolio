The Irrigation

by Tess Jyanssen

The tree was fed on blood.

I want to be specific about this because people hear "blood" and they think ritual, they think sacrifice, they think some poor bastard chained to an altar while a priest chants. That is not what happened at the Haligtree. What happened at the Haligtree was irrigation.

Miquella bled himself. Regularly. Into channels cut into the root system of a tree he was growing as an alternative to the Erdtree, which is the kind of sentence that sounds insane until you understand the engineering, and then it sounds worse, because the engineering worked.

Let me back up.

The Erdtree was the dominant theological and biological infrastructure of the Lands Between. I say "theological and biological" because in this particular world those are the same system: the tree defined reality, reality defined the tree, and if you weren't included in the tree's operating parameters you were, functionally, not a person. The Albinaurics whose legs failed on schedule. The Misbegotten who used to be sacred and got reclassified as livestock. The Omen, born with horns the old religion celebrated and the new religion sawed off. All of them excluded. All of them aware of the exclusion. None of them with anywhere to go.

Miquella decided to build them somewhere to go.

This is either the most ambitious public works project in the history of the Lands Between or the most spectacular act of theological hubris since his mother stole divinity from the Hornsent, depending on whether you evaluate projects by their blueprints or their outcomes. I have opinions about this. I always have opinions about this. People who build things interest me more than people who rule things, and Miquella was building.

The Haligtree was grown, not constructed. This distinction matters for the same reason the distinction between a bridge and a ford matters: one of them you can maintain with tools and the other one you maintain by not letting the river change course. Miquella planted a seed.

The seed was not a normal seed (nothing about any of this is normal, I am aware, but specificity is how we avoid drowning in the abnormal). The seed was grown from unalloyed gold, a substance Miquella had developed after abandoning Golden Order Fundamentalism, which had failed to cure his sister Malenia's Scarlet Rot. Unalloyed gold wards away Outer Gods. It creates a space that is theologically neutral. A clearing in the forest of divine influence where something new can grow without being colonized by whatever cosmic entity happens to be paying attention.

He watered the tree with his own blood.

I need you to understand this mechanically, not symbolically. Miquella's blood contained properties derived from his status as an Empyrean (candidate for godhood). The blood functioned as a growth medium. The tree absorbed it through its root system and used the divine properties to accelerate its development and extend its canopy. This is not metaphor. This is horticulture. Weird, theologically loaded, possibly blasphemous horticulture, but horticulture.

The system worked. The Haligtree grew. Its branches extended. Its roots established. And the rejected populations of the Lands Between began to arrive.

Albinaurics came first. Their legs were failing (the legs always fail, that's the design, that's the planned obsolescence built into their bodies by whoever commissioned them, and nobody talks about the commissioning because the purchase order doesn't exist and the people who benefit from Albinauric labor would rather not examine the supply chain too closely).

They came to the Haligtree because Miquella said: you exist and I see you.

That was the sales pitch. That was the entire value proposition. "You exist and I see you." The fact that this was sufficient to command lifelong loyalty from an entire population tells you everything about the market they were coming from.

Misbegotten came. Former divine beings reclassified as beasts of burden. They carried stones. They built walls. They did what they had always done (labor, heavy and uncompensated) except that at the Haligtree someone acknowledged they were doing it. This is the thinnest possible improvement and they took it because thin is better than nothing.

Cleanrot Knights came with Malenia. They knew what service to her meant. The Scarlet Rot would consume them. Their armor would corrode. Their flesh would putrefy. They came anyway because Malenia was Miquella's sister and Miquella's project was her project and the Knights had decided, individually and collectively, that devotion to a cause that would literally decompose their bodies was preferable to serving the Golden Order, which would decompose their bodies metaphorically and call it duty.

The logistics of this settlement were, and I say this as someone who has provisioned caravans across three continents, extraordinary.

Consider the water system. The Haligtree grew in the mountains north of the Consecrated Snowfield. Temperatures below freezing for nine months of the year. Every water source frozen solid except the channels Miquella had carved into the root system, which stayed liquid because divine blood apparently has antifreeze properties (I have checked: nobody studied this, nobody documented the mechanism, nobody asked "why doesn't the blood freeze," because everybody was too busy worshipping or fleeing to do basic materials science, and this is why I drink).

Consider the food. The canopy filtered sunlight into something golden and diffuse, which was beautiful and also meant that standard agriculture was impossible beneath the branches. The settlement ate what grew in the tree's own ecosystem: bracket fungi the size of cart wheels, edible bark from secondary growth, and a fruit that the Albinaurics called "mercy" because it tasted like nothing but kept you alive, which is the most Albinauric name for a food I have ever heard.

Consider the architecture. The Haligtree's branches were load-bearing in ways that conventional timber is not. Structures were grown into the tree rather than built on it. Rooms formed in the spaces between bark layers. Staircases followed the grain. The whole settlement was parasitic in the biological sense: it lived on and in the host organism, drawing sustenance from the same root system that Miquella was feeding.

I have seen parasite architecture before (the clacks towers along the Patchwork Express route are technically parasitic, growing into the ridgeline rather than sitting on it, and Johannes used to say that the best building is one that can't be separated from its site). The Haligtree settlement was the most elegant version of this I have ever encountered. It was also, and this should have been obvious to everyone involved, completely dependent on the continued health of the tree.

The tree's continued health was completely dependent on Miquella.

The Haligtree had a population. It had infrastructure. It had a growing canopy and extending roots and unalloyed gold wards and a theological neutrality that made it the only place in the Lands Between where an Albinauric and a Misbegotten and a Crucible-aspected knight could stand next to each other and all three of them were, for the first time in their lives, legitimate.

It had a single point of failure.

Miquella.

The blood was his. The unalloyed gold was his invention. The theological framework was his design. The wards, the growth medium, the neutrality, the sanctuary: all of it flowed from one Empyrean child who could not age past adolescence and who bled himself into a tree because nobody else could.

When Mohg kidnapped Miquella, the Haligtree stopped growing.

I want to talk about what "stopped growing" looks like in practice because it is not what you think.

A tree that stops growing does not immediately die. It persists. Its existing structure remains sound for years, sometimes decades, depending on the species and the conditions.

The wood holds. The bark thickens. The roots maintain their positions in the soil and continue to draw water and nutrients through capillary action that requires no conscious direction from anyone.

But the new growth stops. The branch tips that were extending, centimeter by centimeter, into new territory: they stop. The root tips that were probing deeper, finding new water tables, establishing new anchoring points: they stop. The canopy that was thickening, filling gaps, creating the continuous cover that makes a tree a habitat rather than a landmark: it stops.

The Haligtree stopped growing the way a city stops growing when the money runs out. The buildings stay. The roads stay. The people stay. But the construction cranes come down and the permits stop being issued and the empty lots stay empty and the cracks in the sidewalk don't get filled and everyone can see, in the stillness, the shape of everything that was supposed to happen next and didn't.

The rot started at the edges. Malenia's Scarlet Rot, which the unalloyed gold had been suppressing, began to creep back in as the gold degraded. The outermost branches softened. The bark turned spongy. The bracket fungi that had been feeding the population began producing spores that smelled wrong, that tasted of copper instead of nothing, that the Albinaurics stopped eating and then started eating again because the alternative was not eating.

That sentence should be the end of the story but it isn't because the Haligtree didn't die. It stopped growing and it didn't die and the difference between those two things is the difference between a project that failed and a project that was abandoned, and I care about this distinction the way I care about all distinctions between things that break and things that are broken.

The tree stood. The canopy held. The roots maintained their positions. The wards thinned but did not collapse entirely. And the population that had gathered beneath the branches waited for Miquella to come back.

He did not come back.

The Cleanrot Knights discovered something about the Haligtree in the years after Miquella's disappearance. If they detonated themselves (and I mean this literally, their bodies had become so saturated with Scarlet Rot that controlled self-destruction was a tactical option, which is a sentence I do not enjoy writing), the flash of their deaths created a beacon. A signal. Something that might, theoretically, reach Miquella wherever Mohg had taken him and guide him home.

They were using their bodies as flares.

I have seen desperate engineering before. I have watched people in the Patchwork Express jury-rig solutions out of materials no sane engineer would approve, and I have admired every one of those solutions because desperation is the mother of innovation and innovation is the thing I love most in the world. But the Cleanrot Knights turning themselves into signal fires is not innovation. It is the endpoint of a supply chain that started with one boy bleeding into a tree and ended with soldiers exploding themselves to call him back.

The single point of failure.

Every engineer knows about single points of failure. Every engineer is taught, on the first day of every course I have ever taken or taught or argued about in the back of a workshop while someone else was supposed to be lecturing, every engineer is taught: redundancy. Backup systems. Failsafes. If one component breaks, the system survives. If one person leaves, the project continues. If one supply line is cut, another feeds the operation.

Miquella built none of this.

He built a tree that only he could feed. A sanctuary that only he could ward. A population whose legitimacy derived entirely from his personal recognition. Every act of inclusion was an act performed by Miquella, for Miquella's project, through Miquella's blood. When Miquella was gone, the inclusion had no institutional structure to sustain it. The Albinaurics were legitimate because Miquella said so. Who says so now?

I keep coming back to this. The Erdtree was a tyranny but it was a distributed tyranny. Kill one priest and another takes his place. Burn one church and the doctrine survives in the next village. The Golden Order was evil and it was resilient because evil systems that depend on single individuals don't last and the Golden Order lasted for ages.

The Haligtree was good and it was fragile because good systems built by exceptional individuals are exactly as durable as those individuals and not one day longer.

The tree is still standing. I checked. Rotting, infested, populated by soldiers who have been waiting so long they've forgotten what waiting feels like and have settled into a state that looks like garrison duty if you squint but is actually grief wearing armor. The canopy is thinning. The wards are failing. The unalloyed gold in the root system is degrading without fresh applications.

Nobody is bleeding into the channels.

The blood channels are the part that gets me. They're still there. Cut into the root system with precision that tells you someone (Miquella, presumably, or someone he directed) understood fluid dynamics and vascular geometry and the specific rate at which divine blood needs to flow through living wood to maintain growth. The channels are dry now. They look like irrigation ditches after a drought: the infrastructure of plenty, empty, waiting for a supply that isn't coming.

I have seen irrigation ditches after a drought. I have walked the Patchwork Express through regions where the canals built by better governments than the current one stood dry and cracking in the sun while the fields around them turned to dust. The ditches don't know the water stopped. The ditches are ready. The ditches will be ready forever. That is the nature of infrastructure: it outlasts intent.

The refugees stayed. Where else would they go? Back to a world that classified them as livestock, as obsolete, as theologically inconvenient? The Haligtree is rotting and the Haligtree is still better than the alternative, and the fact that a dying tree built by a kidnapped child is the best option available to the Albinaurics and the Misbegotten and the Cleanrot Knights tells you everything you need to know about the quality of the other options.

Miquella's mistake was not ambition. Ambition I understand, ambition I respect, ambition is the thing that makes a person look at a broken world and say "I can build something better" instead of "somebody should do something."

Miquella's mistake was being irreplaceable.

He built a system that required a god and then he disappeared and the system discovered what every system eventually discovers: that the difference between a founder and a foundation is that foundations don't get kidnapped. You want your project to survive you? Make yourself unnecessary. Distribute the knowledge. Train the replacements. Document the blood ratios and the channel geometries and the warding frequencies so that when you are dragged away by a delusional Omen lord with a god complex, somebody else can pick up the watering can.

The tree is still standing. The blood channels are dry. The Cleanrot Knights are still detonating.

Somebody should have taught that boy about redundancy. Somebody should have sat him down before the first blood-letting, before the first ward, before the first Albinauric walked through the gate and looked up at branches that said "you belong here," and told him: you are mortal. You are an Empyrean who cannot age past adolescence and you are still mortal, which means you can be taken, and when you are taken, everything you built with your blood will starve.

He wouldn't have listened. Builders never do.
