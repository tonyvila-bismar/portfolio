Concerning the Last Festival of Forms, and the Question of Whether It Was Known to Be Such at the Ti

by Paarfi

Concerning the Last Festival of Forms, and the Question of Whether It Was Known to Be Such at the Time

It is a peculiarity of festivals that the last one is never announced. We have observed this in our study of a great many historical celebrations, carnivals, and public rituals, and we can state with confidence earned through considerable labor that no crier has ever stood at the gates of a city and proclaimed: "Come one, come all, to the final instance of a beloved tradition which will never be repeated, on account of the world changing underneath it in ways we are too busy celebrating to notice."

This does not happen.

Festivals end the way rivers change course — slowly, then all at once, and by the time anyone thinks to mark the old channel, the water is somewhere else entirely and the map is a curiosity.

We mention this because the Festival of Forms, which is the subject of this account, was held in the city of Thornreach on the second day of the month that the old Crucible calendar calls the Month of Deepening — a name we confess we find evocative, though we have been unable to determine what, precisely, was thought to deepen in that season, and we suspect the original meaning has been lost beneath so many centuries of subsequent interpretation that the word has become a kind of archaeological site in itself, with each generation's assumptions layered atop the last like so much decorative plaster.

The Festival of Forms was an annual occasion. It had been held, by the accounts we have been able to consult (and we will address the reliability of these accounts presently, to the satisfaction of all but those critics who would not be satisfied if we presented them with the original tablets signed in the hand of the Crucible itself), for no fewer than four hundred years, and possibly as many as six hundred.

The discrepancy arises from the fact that the earliest references may describe a related but distinct ceremony involving the blessing of livestock, which the reader will agree is not quite the same thing, though the distinction is perhaps less clear than one might wish in a civilization that considered the boundary between human and livestock to be a suggestion rather than a rule.

The purpose of the Festival was this: those individuals who had, in the preceding year, manifested new aspects of the Crucible — horns, tails, wings, scales, dense bone, altered musculature, or any of the several dozen other variations catalogued in the Litany of Forms — would present themselves before the assembled priesthood and the public, and their aspects would be named, blessed, and recorded. It was, in short, a census conducted as a celebration, or a celebration conducted as a census, depending upon whether one consulted the temple or the garrison.

(We are aware that a certain school of historians has argued that the Festival was primarily a military recruitment exercise disguised as a religious observance. We have considered this argument. We find it reductive, though not without a grain of truth so small that it might be mistaken for sand, and we will address it at a more appropriate juncture, if, indeed, any juncture in a narrative can be said to be appropriate for addressing arguments one finds reductive.)

But we were speaking of a specific Festival, and we must arrive at it eventually, or risk losing the reader's patience, which we value more than we perhaps demonstrate.

The Festival we intend to describe is the one held in the four hundred and — let us say sixty-third year of the tradition, though we must confess that the precise numbering depends upon whether one counts from the first Festival or from the first Festival that was called a Festival, and the difference is, as the reader may have surmised, the subject of a scholarly dispute that we have no intention of resolving here, having resolved quite enough disputes for one career and emerged from each resolution with fewer friends than we entered it.

The city of Thornreach occupied a position of some significance in the geography of the Crucible's domain. It sat at the confluence of two rivers whose names we will not attempt to render in the modern tongue, as the original language employed consonant clusters that would, we fear, cause the reader physical discomfort, and we consider ourselves bound by the historian's oath to do no harm, or at any rate no more harm than is strictly necessary for the advancement of knowledge.

The city was not large by the standards of the great temple complexes to the south, but it was prosperous, and it possessed two features that made it a natural site for the Festival. The first was a public square of considerable dimensions — large enough, our sources tell us, to accommodate six hundred standing observers and still leave room for a mounted procession, though we suspect this figure has been inflated by civic pride, a force we have found to be more powerful than gravity and considerably less predictable.

The second was a temple whose architectural distinction lay in its open roof — or rather, in the deliberate absence of a roof, the temple having been designed so that those who worshiped within it did so beneath the open sky, exposed to the branches of a young Erdtree that had been planted in the temple's courtyard some two centuries prior.

The tree had, by the time of our Festival, grown to a height that — but we are getting ahead of ourselves, which is a fault we have been accused of before and to which we readily confess, as it is a fault born of enthusiasm rather than negligence.

Let us speak of the morning.

The Festival began at dawn, as most Crucible ceremonies did, because the priesthood believed that the light of early morning revealed aspects in their truest form — a belief that was, so far as we can determine, not entirely without physiological basis, as several of the aspected traits described in the Litany do in fact present differently under different lighting conditions, a detail that strikes us as extraordinary and that the priesthood, to their credit, had noticed long before anyone thought to investigate it systematically.

The participants gathered in the square before the temple. Our sources describe them with a specificity that we find touching, and we will reproduce several of these descriptions here, not because each is strictly necessary to the narrative, but because we believe the reader deserves to see what the crowd looked like, on the grounds that history which does not show you the people is not history but bookkeeping.

In the front rank of participants — for there were ranks, the order of presentation being determined by the severity and complexity of the manifestation, with the most dramatic aspects presented last, in the manner of a theatrical program that builds toward its most impressive act — stood a girl of perhaps sixteen years whose aspect was a pair of small, curved horns emerging from her temples.

They were, by the standards of the Crucible, modest. They curved backward in the classic logarithmic spiral that the Litany classifies as Primary Cranial, Type II, and they were polished to a high shine, which was customary. The girl wore a white robe — white being the ceremonial color for new manifestants, indicating that they had not yet received their formal classification — and she stood very straight, in the manner of a person who has been told to stand very straight and is thinking about nothing else.

Behind her stood a man of middle years whose left arm, from the elbow to the fingertips, was covered in scales of a deep amber color. He had manifested these scales over a period of some three months, beginning at the wrist and progressing upward, and he stood in the ranks with the patient weariness of a person who has already explained his arm to every neighbor, relative, and passing stranger in his village and would very much like to stop explaining it.

Behind him — but we must exercise some restraint, or we will describe every participant in the square and arrive at the ceremony itself sometime next year, which we cannot afford, as our publisher has expressed certain views on the subject of deadlines that, while unreasonable, are backed by contractual provisions we would prefer not to test.

Let us say only that there were, in total, forty-seven participants. Twenty-three had manifested cranial aspects of various kinds — horns being by far the most common, with the occasional bony ridge or crest. Eleven had manifested integumentary changes — scales, thickened skin, or in one remarkable case, a fine down of feathers along both arms that the Litany would have struggled to classify, as it fell between two existing categories in a manner that, we suspect, gave some overworked clerk a very difficult afternoon.

Eight had manifested appendicular changes — tails, wings, or elongated digits. Five had manifested changes to bone density or musculature that were not visible but were detectable by the temple assessors through methods we will not describe, as they involved a degree of physical examination that the reader may find indelicate, and we are, above all things, delicate.

The ceremony itself was conducted by the Senior Liturgist, a woman whose name our sources record as Thessaly — a name we find suspicious, as it appears to be borrowed from a geographical region rather than bestowed in the customary manner, but we have no evidence to the contrary and will therefore accept it, with the caveat that we accept many things about this period with a degree of resigned uncertainty that our critics mistake for carelessness.

Thessaly opened the ceremony with the Invocation of the Unformed — a prayer that is, so far as we know, unique to the Crucible tradition, in that it addresses not a god, not a saint, not an ancestor, but the state of being that existed before any of these categories applied. The Crucible, in its theological imagination, was the moment before differentiation. The Invocation asked the assembled to remember that they were, each of them, fragments of that original unity, and that the aspects they bore were not additions to their nature but revelations of what had always been present.

We find this beautiful. We also find it theologically audacious, and we mention our reaction because we believe the historian who conceals his own responses to his material is not practicing objectivity but cowardice, and we have been called many things — verbose, digressive, unreliable, and on one memorable occasion "a menace to the Hawk's reputation for intellectual sobriety" — but never a coward.

We pause here to note that the Invocation has not survived in any complete form. What we have are fragments, quoted in secondary sources, and our own reconstruction based on liturgical patterns from the period. We are confident in the broad outlines. We are less confident in the specific wording, and we ask the reader to extend to us the courtesy of trusting our judgment while remaining skeptical of our precision, which is, we believe, the appropriate stance toward any historian, and especially toward this one.

Each participant was then brought forward.

The procedure was as follows. The participant approached the stone at the center of the square — a flat slab of granite that our sources describe as "the Stating Stone," a name whose etymology is either straightforward (a stone upon which one states one's aspect) or deeply symbolic (a stone that states what you are), and we confess we prefer the second interpretation though we have no grounds for it beyond aesthetic preference, which is, we are told, an insufficient basis for historical claims, a position with which we disagree.

Upon the Stating Stone, the participant was examined by Thessaly and two assistant liturgists. The aspect was measured, described in liturgical terminology, and classified according to the Litany. The classification was then announced to the crowd by a crier whose voice, we are told, could be heard across the full extent of the square, which was, as we mentioned, of considerable dimensions.

After the classification, the participant was asked a question.

This is the part of the ceremony that interests us most, and we ask the reader's patience as we describe it, because the question was always the same, and the answers were always different, and between the sameness and the difference lies everything that matters about the Crucible as a living faith rather than a system of classification.

The question was: "What does the form teach you?"

We will note that this question is not "What is the form?" That was the liturgist's business — the measuring, the classifying, the recording. Nor is it "What is the form for?" — which would imply utility, purpose, design. It is "What does the form teach you?" — which implies that the aspect is not merely a thing that happened to your body but a communication, a lesson, a word spoken by the Crucible in a language that only the bearer can hear.

The girl with the horns, when asked, said: "It teaches me that my head is heavier than I thought."

There was, our sources record, laughter. We find this appropriate. We also find it, upon reflection, rather profound, though we are aware that finding profundity in a sixteen-year-old's joke about the weight of her own skull may be an act of interpretive overreach, and we accept that risk.

Thessaly, we are told, smiled. This is the only time the sources record her smiling during the entire ceremony, and we consider it worth noting.

The man with the scaled arm, when asked, said — but here we encounter a difficulty, as our primary source becomes illegible for several lines, the parchment having suffered water damage at precisely the most inconvenient juncture, which is, in our experience, what parchment does, having a malicious intelligence that historians underestimate at their peril.

We can say with confidence that forty-seven participants were presented, classified, and asked the question. We can say that the ceremony lasted from dawn until the middle of the afternoon, which strikes us as a very long time to stand in a public square, though the crowd appears to have remained throughout, which suggests either extraordinary civic devotion or insufficient entertainment options in Thornreach, and we suspect both.

What we cannot say — what no source tells us and no reconstruction can supply — is whether anyone present understood that this was the last time.

There are indications. Our secondary sources, writing from a later period, describe changes in the years following this Festival that suggest the institution was already under pressure. A faction within the priesthood had begun to argue that certain aspects were more sacred than others — that the classic forms (horns, tails, dense bone) represented the Crucible's true intention, while the rarer and stranger manifestations (the feathered arms, the asymmetric growths, the aspects that defied classification) were errors, impurities, deviations from a pattern that the Crucible had intended but imperfectly executed.

We will not belabor the implications of this argument, as the reader is, we trust, capable of perceiving where a theology that divides the sacred into the properly sacred and the improperly sacred eventually leads.

We will say only this. The girl with the horns — Type II, Primary Cranial, polished to a shine — would have been, in the framework that was emerging, acceptable. The man with the feathered arms — unclassifiable, falling between categories, giving some clerk a difficult afternoon — would not.

The Festival of Forms was not abolished. It was not banned or suppressed or formally discontinued. It simply was not held the following year, or the year after that, and by the time anyone thought to ask why, the answer was that it had always been a local custom of no particular significance, and the resources it consumed could be better allocated to the temple's primary mission, which had, somewhere in the intervening years, been redefined in terms that the founders of the Festival would not have recognized.

We have searched for a record of the forty-eighth participant — the one who would have been first at the next Festival, had there been one. We have not found it. This does not surprise us. The aspects did not stop manifesting. The Festival stopped noticing.

This is how a beloved tradition dies. Not with a decree, but with an absence.

We have spent, we confess, more time on this account than our publisher would consider reasonable, and more emotion than our critics would consider scholarly. We do not apologize for either expenditure. Some subjects deserve unreasonable attention, and some histories deserve to be told by someone who cares about them, even if caring makes the telling less precise and considerably less brief.

Here the record, such as it is, ends. We have done what we can with it. If the reader finds our account incomplete, we can only say that incompleteness is the natural condition of history, and that the historian who claims otherwise is either a liar or has not looked carefully enough at the gaps, which are, in our experience, where the most important things are hiding, patiently, waiting for someone to come along with a lantern and the good sense to look down.

The question was: "What does the form teach you?"

We do not know what the girl with the horns said after that first Festival. We suspect she went home. We suspect the horns kept growing. We suspect she polished them every morning, because that is what you do with something you have been told is sacred, even after the people who told you stop saying it. We suspect she did this for years. We have no evidence. We have only the conviction, born of a career spent studying people who lived and died and left too few records, that this is what people do.

But that is speculation, and we are, after all, a historian.
