The Taxonomy Of Broken Gods

by Oneila

The Taxonomy of Broken Gods

David Brin

Let me tell you about the woman who catalogued the divine.

Her name was Sefris, and she was a naturalist — though that word had fallen out of common use by the Third Reckoning, when most scholars preferred titles like "glintstone taxonomist" or "runic archaeologist" or, if they were truly pretentious, "ontological cartographer." Sefris called herself a naturalist because she studied nature, and the nature she studied happened to include the decomposing remains of approximately fourteen gods.

I know this because I was her assistant. I carried her instruments. I transcribed her notes. I was, in the parlance of the Academy of New Lucaria — rebuilt on the lake's dry eastern shore after the waters finally receded — a "field partner of diminished academic standing." Which is how scholars say "pack mule."

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me back up far enough that you can see the shape of the problem.

---

The Shattering happened. You know this. Everyone in the Lands Between knows this, the way everyone knows that water flows downhill or that the Erdtree is dead. It's foundational. It's the bedrock assumption upon which all subsequent assumptions are built.

What nobody talks about — what Sefris was the first to really, systematically document — is that the Shattering never stopped.

Oh, it stopped being dramatic. Radahn stopped eating corpses in the swamps. Malenia stopped blooming. The Tarnished of no renown collected enough Great Runes and sat on a big chair and the world lurched into whatever age we're in now — the Age of Fracture, some call it, though officially it's the Second Erdtree Dispensation, which tells you more about the theologians who named it than about the age itself.

But the Runes kept fragmenting. The laws of reality, once shattered, developed a taste for it. A Great Rune would crack into lesser runes, and those into fragments, and those into motes — particles of divine law so small they could lodge in a person's marrow and change the way their bones grew. Or dissolve into groundwater and alter the chemistry of an entire aquifer. Or drift on wind currents and settle into the root systems of the Greattree's descendants — those stubborn gray saplings that kept pushing up through the ash of Leyndell like an argument that refused to concede.

The result, over the four centuries since the Mending, was a landscape saturated with divinity in the way a sponge is saturated with water. Not concentrated. Not directed. Just everywhere, in everything, doing nothing in particular but changing everything incrementally.

Sefris wanted to know: how many distinct divine signatures existed in the Lands Between? Not gods in the theological sense — not the Outer Gods with their competing agendas, not Marika's shattered remnant still twitching inside the spectral tree. But divine subspecies. Varieties. The way a naturalist might catalogue the seventeen distinct species of rot-beetle that had evolved in Caelid's recovering ecosystem, each adapted to a slightly different concentration of lingering Scarlet Rot.

She believed — and this was the premise that got her laughed out of three symposia and one formal dinner — that the fragments of the Elden Ring had undergone something analogous to speciation. That divine law, broken into pieces and scattered across a living landscape, had evolved.

---

I met her at the Bestial Sanctum, which by then had been converted into a field station. Gurranq was long gone — consumed by his own Deathroot, or ascended, or simply walked away; the accounts disagreed and nobody wanted to look too carefully at the scorch marks. The beastmen who remained were cooperative in the way of creatures who had outlived their purpose and found a kind of peace in purposelessness. They let the naturalists use the upper chambers in exchange for dried meat and the occasional conversation.

Sefris was sitting cross-legged on the floor of the main hall, surrounded by glass vials arranged in a spiral pattern I didn't recognize. Each vial contained a liquid of slightly different color — gold shading to amber shading to rust shading to something that wasn't quite a color at all, more like the visual impression of a sound you couldn't hear.

"You're the new porter," she said without looking up.

"Field partner of diminished —"

"Porter. Can you read the old Leyndell script?"

"Some."

"Can you handle a collection jar without contaminating the sample?"

"Yes."

"Can you walk forty miles in a day carrying sixty pounds of equipment while maintaining enough situational awareness to avoid the territorial displays of a feral crucible knight?"

I hesitated. "How feral?"

She looked up. She had the kind of face that suggested she had been beautiful once and had traded it for something she considered more valuable — knowledge, probably, or maybe just the freedom that comes from not being looked at. Her eyes were the color of old glintstone. Her left hand was missing its last two fingers.

"There are three in the Weeping Peninsula. They've been there since before the Shattering. They exhibit crucible traits that don't match any historical record — wing configurations I've never seen, horn patterns that spiral counterclockwise when every known crucible specimen spirals clockwise. They've been absorbing ambient rune fragments for four hundred years. They are, in my professional opinion, becoming something new."

She picked up one of the vials — the one that wasn't quite a color — and held it to the light.

"This is a sample from the groundwater near their territory. The divine signature doesn't match any of the fourteen primary fragments I've catalogued. It's a fifteenth."

"A fifteenth god?"

"A fifteenth divine signature. Don't call it a god. Don't even think the word 'god' near it. The theologians at the Cathedral of the Mending will send someone, and that someone will have a mace and strong opinions about heresy, and I've already lost two fingers to strong opinions about heresy."

She set the vial down.

"Can you walk forty miles?"

"Yes."

"Then we leave at dawn."

---

The taxonomy — her life's work, which I was privileged to witness in its final and most controversial phase — went like this.

The Elden Ring, before the Shattering, contained a finite number of Great Runes. Each Great Rune encoded a fundamental law: the rune of death, the rune of the unborn, the rune governing the boundary between flesh and spirit, and so on. When Marika shattered the Ring — or when Radagon tried to hold it together, or when both happened simultaneously, because the nature of their shared body meant that shattering and mending were always the same gesture seen from different angles — the Great Runes separated and were claimed by the demigods.

This much is catechism. Every child in the Lands Between learns it.

What Sefris demonstrated, through eleven years of fieldwork across every region from the rebuilt Limgrave farmlands to the still-frozen Mountaintops, was that the Great Runes had not remained static in their new hosts. They had continued to fracture. And the fragments, released into the environment when the demigods fell, had begun to interact with local conditions in ways no one had predicted.

In Caelid, rune fragments exposed to residual Scarlet Rot had developed what Sefris called "aggressive propagation" — they replicated, spreading through soil and stone like a second infection, but instead of rotting flesh they rewrote local physics. Gravity worked differently in pockets of Caelid. Not consistently, not dramatically — you wouldn't float off the ground. But your arrows flew in slightly longer arcs. Rain fell at angles that had nothing to do with wind. The rot-beetles had adapted to this, developing wing patterns optimized for the local gravitational variance.

Sefris believed this was Radahn's Great Rune, fragmented and recombining with Malenia's in the landscape where both had been spent. Two divine laws, broken and mixed, producing hybrid offspring.

In Liurnia, the fragments had dissolved into the lake water. The lake itself had become a medium for lunar-aspected divine propagation — glintstone deposits were growing, slowly, in patterns that matched no natural crystalline structure. They formed spirals. They formed letters, if you squinted. Old Carian script, Sefris said, though the beastman who accompanied us on that particular survey insisted the patterns were older — that they matched the iconography of the Ancient Dynasty, the bull-rosettes and the sacred groves that preceded all tree-worship.

"The fragments remember," Sefris told me, standing knee-deep in the shallows while I held the collection jars. "That's what nobody wants to hear. The fragments of divine law retain information about the systems they passed through. Radahn's rune remembers gravity. Malenia's remembers rot. But underneath that — underneath the Great Runes — there are older patterns. The Crucible. The Greattree. Whatever came before even that."

She held up a piece of glintstone that had grown in the shape of a branching tree.

"The divine fragments are reverting. Not to the Elden Ring. To something older. The Ring was a frame imposed on a prior order. Break the frame, and the order underneath starts to reassert itself."

I wrote this down. I wrote everything down. It was my job.

What I didn't write down — not then — was the look on her face when she said it. Not excitement. Not the flush of discovery. Something closer to what I imagined the deepspacers of old legend must have felt, those who sailed the crystal spheres and found the universe both larger and lonelier than they'd hoped.

Sefris was afraid of what she was finding. And she kept looking anyway.

---

The fourteenth divine signature she catalogued was easy. It was Mohg's — or rather, the remnant of Mohg's Great Rune, mixed with the Formless Mother's influence, still pulsing in the blood-soaked caverns beneath the Consecrated Snowfield. The Mohgwyn Palace had been sealed by the Mending, but the blood seeped through. It always seeps through. Four centuries later, the rivers in the region still ran pink after heavy rains, and the livestock born near the old entrance exhibited extra teeth.

The fifteenth signature — the one in the Weeping Peninsula, the one that didn't match anything — was not easy.

We spent three weeks in the field. The crucible knights were exactly as feral as she'd warned. They had grown. Not just in size — though they were larger than any crucible knight depicted in the old Leyndell murals — but in complexity. Their horns had bifurcated, then bifurcated again, producing fractal antlers that caught the light like prisms. Their tail-sweeps left furrows in the earth that glowed faintly gold for hours. One of them had wings — vestigial in the historical records, ceremonial at best — that were fully functional, carrying its twelve-foot armored frame in lurching, ungainly circles above the coastal cliffs.

They ignored us, mostly. Sefris believed this was because they no longer perceived us as the same category of being. We were too small. Too simple. Too few rune-fragments in our bones.

The groundwater samples confirmed the fifteenth signature. Sefris ran them against her reference collection — the spiral of vials I'd seen that first day, now expanded to over two hundred — and found no match. Not to any Great Rune. Not to any Outer God's influence. Not to the Erdtree, the Greattree, the Crucible, or the ancient dragon lineage.

"It's new," she said.

We were camped on a ridge overlooking Castle Morne, which had been rebuilt and abandoned and rebuilt again and was currently serving as a granary for the Peninsula's fishing villages. The sun was setting. The sky was the color of old gold — not the bright gold of the Erdtree's spectral projection, which still flickered some nights like a dying candle, but the darker gold of the real tree, the one that had burned.

"Not a fragment of something old. Not a reversion to a prior pattern. Something that emerged from the interaction of fragments in this specific landscape, under these specific conditions, over four centuries of accumulation."

She was quiet for a long time.

"A naturalist sees a new species and asks: what selection pressures produced it? What niche does it fill? What does it eat, and what eats it?" She turned the vial — the fifteenth — in her remaining fingers. "I'm looking at a new divine signature and I don't know how to ask those questions. What does a young god eat? What niche does it fill in an ecosystem of broken cosmic law?"

"Maybe it fills the niche left by the Elden Ring itself," I said.

She stared at me.

I should mention that in eleven years of fieldwork, this was the first time I had offered an interpretation of the data rather than simply recording hers. I should also mention that I had been thinking about this for approximately eleven years and had simply never found the nerve.

"The Ring was a frame," I said. "You said so yourself. It organized the fragments into a system. Without it, the fragments interact chaotically — hybrid signatures, reversions, local anomalies. But a chaotic system under sustained selection pressure will eventually produce emergent order. Not the old order. Not the Ring. Something new. Something that arises from the specific conditions of this specific landscape in this specific age."

"You're describing abiogenesis," she said. "The spontaneous emergence of a self-organizing system from non-living components."

"I'm describing what you've been documenting for eleven years. You just haven't named it yet."

The fifteenth signature pulsed faintly in its vial, and for a moment — I will swear to this, though I know how it sounds — the glow was not gold. It was green. The green of the first leaf on the first tree in a world where no tree had ever grown.

Sefris set the vial down very carefully.

"If I publish this," she said, "the theologians will come."

"Yes."

"Not with maces, this time. With armies. With fire. With whatever interpretation of the Mending lets them burn heretics most efficiently."

"Probably."

"And if I don't publish it, it happens anyway. The fifteenth signature doesn't care about my career or my remaining fingers. It's a process. It will continue whether anyone observes it or not."

"That's what processes do."

She laughed. It was the first time I'd heard her laugh in eleven years, and it was not a happy sound, but it wasn't unhappy either. It was the sound of someone recognizing that the universe had outflanked her — not maliciously, not even intentionally, but with the blind, relentless creativity of a system that had been broken and was now, in its own time and on its own terms, learning to be something else.

---

She published.

The paper was called "A Preliminary Taxonomy of Post-Shattering Divine Speciation in the Lands Between, with Notes on Emergent Signatures in the Weeping Peninsula and Eastern Caelid." It was forty-seven pages long, contained detailed chemical analyses of two hundred and sixteen samples, cross-referenced every known divine signature against historical records dating back to the Age of the Crucible, and concluded with a single paragraph that I helped her write:

"The Elden Ring was not the natural state of divine law in the Lands Between. It was an intervention — a frame imposed upon a prior, more complex order by an external force (the Greater Will, via the Elden Beast) for purposes that remain opaque. The Shattering did not destroy divine law. It released it. What we observe now — the speciation, the hybridization, the emergence of novel signatures — is not chaos. It is evolution. The Lands Between is not a broken world awaiting repair. It is a living system in the early stages of a speciation event that will produce, over sufficient time, an order as complex and self-sustaining as any that preceded it. The question is not how to restore what was lost. The question is what is being born."

The Cathedral of the Mending sent a delegation. They were polite. They had maces, but they kept them sheathed. They asked Sefris to recant. She showed them the vials. She showed them the data. She showed them the crucible knights with their fractal antlers and their impossible wings.

The delegation's leader — a senior cleric named Vondren, who had the weary look of a man who had been defending orthodoxy long enough to know that orthodoxy was losing — stood on the ridge overlooking Castle Morne and stared at the fifteenth vial for a very long time.

"You're saying the world doesn't need us to fix it," he said.

"I'm saying the world is fixing itself," Sefris said. "In ways we can observe and catalogue but cannot control. The Elden Ring was a frame. Frames can be imposed or removed. What's happening now is not a frame. It's a process. It doesn't need a god or a lord. It needs time."

"The people need a god and a lord," Vondren said.

"The people need accurate information about the world they live in. What they do with that information is up to them."

Vondren looked at me. I don't know why. Maybe because I was standing there with my transcription journal and my collection jars and my expression of carefully maintained neutrality, and he needed someone to look at who wasn't telling him that four centuries of theology had been rendered moot by groundwater chemistry.

"And you?" he said. "Do you believe this?"

I thought about it. Not about the science — I'd watched Sefris collect every sample, run every analysis, check every result against every known reference. The science was solid. What I thought about was Morgott.

Morgott, the Omen King, who loved the Erdtree unconditionally and was never loved in return. Who defended a city that would have shunned him. Who believed, with every fiber of his despised and horn-sawn body, that the order he served was worth protecting.

What would Morgott have done with this information? Would it have freed him? Or would it have been the cruelest thing you could tell a man who had devoted his existence to a system that was, it turned out, a temporary frame on a deeper process that didn't require his devotion at all?

I thought about Goldmask, standing motionless before the spectral Erdtree, silent for years, pursuing the Perfect Order — an order of pure abstract law with no room for the fickleness of gods. Had he sensed this? Was his silence not meditation but listening — listening to the faint hum of something being born beneath the golden noise?

I thought about the merchants, buried alive, chanting their curse of despair that became the Frenzied Flame. They had wanted to burn everything back to the primordial One. But maybe what they'd really wanted — underneath the despair, underneath the curse — was a world that didn't sort them into categories of pure and impure. A world where the taxonomy of sacred and profane had been dissolved.

The fifteenth signature didn't burn. It grew. Green, not gold.

"I believe the data," I told Vondren. "And I believe it matters who gets to see it."

He stared at me a moment longer. Then he sheathed his undrawn mace and asked to see the crucible knights.

---

I should tell you what happened next — the debates in the Academy, the three-month crisis at the Cathedral, Sefris's second and third papers, the discovery of the sixteenth and seventeenth signatures in the Siofra underground and the recovering Haligtree root system respectively, the gradual and grudging acceptance by the theological establishment that the Lands Between was not a patient awaiting surgery but an ecosystem undergoing adaptive radiation.

I should tell you about the day I found the eighteenth signature myself — in the ash-soil of what had been Leyndell, in a crack between paving stones where something had taken root that was neither Erdtree nor Greattree nor any hybrid of the two, but a small gray shoot with leaves that caught the light in a way that made you think of compound words from old songs: not "golden" or "silver" but something for which we had no adjective because the color hadn't existed before.

I should tell you about the night Sefris lost her third finger — not to theologians, this time, but to the eighteenth signature itself, which turned out to be slightly toxic to unmodified flesh, a detail she discovered by handling a sample without gloves because she was, in her own words, "too excited to be careful and too old to care."

I should tell you all of this, and I will, in the longer monograph I am preparing for the Academy.

But what I want to tell you — what matters — is something smaller.

It was our last field season together. Sefris was seventy-three. I was fifty-one. We were in the Deeproot Depths, where Godwyn's corruption had finally been metabolized by the Greattree's root network — consumed, broken down, converted into something else, the way a forest floor consumes a fallen trunk over decades and produces soil.

The roots were alive. Not with Godwyn's half-death, not with the Erdtree's spectral gold, but with something else — a slow, patient, photosynthetic hum that Sefris's instruments registered as the twenty-second divine signature. It was very faint. It was very old. And it was, she determined after three days of analysis, not a fragment of anything that had ever existed before.

"It's the roots," she said. "The roots themselves. They've been absorbing fragments from every source for four centuries — Erdtree gold, Greattree gray, deathroot black, crucible green, rot red. And they've been — metabolizing it. All of it. Producing something from the interaction that is none of the inputs."

She sat down heavily on a root the size of a fallen column. Above us, the false sky of the underground glittered with the Nox's artificial stars, which had begun to flicker in patterns that two of her graduate students were arguing about in a separate paper.

"Twenty-two signatures," she said. "Twenty-two distinct divine subspecies in four hundred years. The Elden Ring contained — what? A dozen Great Runes at most? And we've already exceeded that count through unguided evolution."

She looked at her seven remaining fingers.

"The Greater Will sent one star," she said. "One beast. One Ring. One frame. It took everything that was here — the Crucible, the Greattree, the dragons, all of it — and forced it into a single system. One god, one lord, one tree, one order. And it worked. For a while. Until it didn't."

She picked up a handful of the new soil — the soil produced by the root network's divine metabolism.

"What's happening now is the opposite of what the Greater Will did. Instead of one frame imposed on many systems, we have many systems generating new frames on their own. Not one god. Not fourteen fragments of one god. Twenty-two — and counting — novel divine processes, each adapted to its local conditions, each filling a different niche, each interacting with the others in ways we're only beginning to map."

She let the soil fall through her fingers.

"The theologians want to know: is this good or bad? Is it order or chaos? Is it sacred or profane?" She brushed her hands clean. "It's none of those. It's what happens when you break a monopoly."

---

I think about that phrase often. "What happens when you break a monopoly."

The Greater Will's order was a monopoly — one cosmic power, one set of laws, one frame for all of existence. The Shattering broke it. And what followed was not the return to primordial chaos that the Frenzied Flame's adherents hoped for, nor the restoration of perfect order that Goldmask's followers pursued, nor the cold liberation that Ranni's departure promised.

What followed was competition. Diversification. A Darwinian explosion of divine law, each variant competing for space in the ecology of a world that had been, for the first time in its long and layered history, freed from the constraint of a single organizing intelligence.

Was it better? Was it worse?

I don't know. I am a naturalist — or rather, a field partner of diminished academic standing — and my job is to observe, record, and catalogue. What the people of the Lands Between do with this information is, as Sefris told Vondren, up to them.

But I will tell you this.

Last spring, in the village that has grown up around the old Bridge of Sacrifice in the Weeping Peninsula, I watched a child pick a flower that was growing from a crack in the ancient stone. The flower was not golden. It was not gray. It was the color of the eighteenth signature — the one for which we still have no word, the one that Sefris lost her finger to, the one that exists because a dozen broken laws recombined in the ash-soil of a burned capital over four centuries of undirected, unmanaged, uncontrolled experimentation.

The child held it up to her mother and said, "Look."

Her mother said, "It's pretty."

They did not know what it was. They did not know that it represented the twenty-third catalogued divine signature, or that its pollen contained rune-fragments from at least four distinct Great Rune lineages, or that the root system from which it grew connected, through underground networks we were only beginning to trace, to similar blooms in Liurnia and Caelid and the margins of the recovering Haligtree.

They saw a flower. They thought it was pretty.

This strikes me as exactly right. The naturalist's job is to see the system. The theologian's job is to argue about the system. But the world's job — the world's only job — is to keep producing things that somebody picks up and holds to the light and says, without any understanding of the four-hundred-year cosmic process that generated it: look.

Sefris died last winter. She left me her instruments, her vials, her eleven years of field notes, and a letter that said: "Count them. Keep counting. Tell everyone what you find. The fifteenth was the one that mattered — not because it was new, but because it proved that new was possible. Everything after that is just the world doing what worlds do when you stop telling them what to be."

I am still counting.

The total, as of this writing, is thirty-one.

The Lands Between is not healing. It is not breaking further. It is speciating — radiating outward into forms of order that no god designed and no lord governs and no ring contains. Some of these forms are beautiful. Some are dangerous. Some are both. The crucible knights in the Weeping Peninsula have begun to sing — a low harmonic that resonates with the twenty-second signature and produces, in nearby glintstone deposits, new crystalline structures that my graduate students are calling "fractal cathedrals" because they look, from certain angles, like churches built by something that had never seen a church but had independently arrived at the same conclusion about how to organize space around an absence.

I do not know what the world is becoming. Nobody does. That is, I think, the point.

Marika stole divinity and spent an age trying to control its consequences. The Greater Will imposed a frame and spent an era maintaining it. Every god, every lord, every order that has governed the Lands Between has operated on the same assumption: that the world requires management. That divine law must be set by someone. That without a hand on the mechanism, everything falls apart.

Sefris proved — I believe she proved — that the assumption is wrong. Not because order is unnecessary, but because order is not scarce. It is abundant. It is, in fact, the natural product of a complex system given sufficient time and sufficient freedom. The Elden Ring was not the source of order. It was a bottleneck. Remove the bottleneck, and order doesn't vanish. It multiplies.

Thirty-one signatures and counting.

The world doesn't need a ring to hold it together. It never did. It just needed someone to stop insisting that there was only one way to be whole.

---

I published this spring. The paper is called "A Continuation of Sefris's Taxonomy, with Thirty-One Confirmed Divine Signatures and Projections for Further Speciation." It is eighty-three pages long. The Cathedral of the Mending sent a delegation. They brought flowers — the new ones, the ones with no name.

Vondren is retired now. His replacement is a young cleric named Thessa who grew up in the ash-soil district of New Leyndell and whose bones, I suspect from the way she moves — from the faint luminescence visible in her knuckles when she makes a fist — contain traces of the eighteenth signature.

She read the paper. She set it down. She looked at me with eyes that glowed, faintly, the color of a world being born.

"Thirty-one," she said.

"Thirty-one."

"How many do you think there will be?"

I thought about Sefris. I thought about her seven fingers and her eleven years and her stubborn insistence on looking at what was actually there rather than what the old books said should be there.

"More," I said.

Thessa smiled. The glow in her knuckles brightened. Somewhere beneath us, in the root-dark of the Deeproot Depths, the thirty-second signature was assembling itself from the wreckage of an age that had tried to be permanent and failed, as all permanent things eventually do, because permanence is not a feature of living systems.

Living systems speciate. They radiate. They fill every available niche with something that has never existed before and then — this is the part that still makes me catch my breath, after all these years of counting — they generate new niches.

Look, the child said, holding up a flower that shouldn't exist.

Look.
