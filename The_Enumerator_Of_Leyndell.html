The Enumerator Of Leyndell

by Eitan

The Enumerator of Leyndell

K.J. Parker

I was counting bricks when the order came through.

Not because anyone had asked me to count bricks, you understand. I count things because that is what I do. The way a dog licks itself, or a priest clears his throat before lying. I am an enumerator — senior grade, third class, seconded to the Office of the Erdtree Fiscal under the Seal of the Golden Order, which is a very long way of saying I count things for the government and am paid barely enough to justify the ink.

The specific bricks I was counting belonged to the east curtain wall of the lower granary adjacent to the Fortified Manor, and I was counting them because I had noticed — during a routine assessment of taxable structures in the Altus ward — that the wall contained forty-three fewer bricks than the corresponding wall on the opposite side. This discrepancy was either (a) an error in the original construction, (b) evidence of unlicensed demolition and material theft, or (c) my own mistake. I was recounting to eliminate option (c), because a senior enumerator, third class, does not make errors with bricks. Bricks are the easy part. It's people that trip you up.

The messenger was a boy of about twelve wearing the gold sash of the Erdtree Chancellery, which on him looked like someone had draped a curtain over a broom handle. He waited for me to finish my row — which showed a professionalism rare in his office — and then handed me a folded sheet sealed with the Channeled Sun.

"You're to come at once," he said.

"I'm in the middle of a wall."

"Yes sir. At once, sir."

The summons was from the Archivist-Conservator of the Royal Census, a woman named Ystrild, whom I had met exactly twice. The first time she told me my quarterly report on warehouse capacities in the lower city was the most thorough document she had read in eleven years of service. The second time she told me I had miscounted the warehouses. Both statements were true. There are nine warehouses. I had written eight, because the ninth had burned down three months before my survey, and I did not count buildings that no longer existed. She maintained that a warehouse which had burned down was still a warehouse; it was simply a warehouse with a value of zero. We argued about this for an hour. I lost, of course. She outranked me. But I was right, and she knew it, and I knew she knew it, which is the only victory available to a man in my position.

Her offices were on the fourth tier of the administrative quarter, in a building that had once been a chantry dedicated to the Erdtree's Grace and was now dedicated to the rather less luminous grace of municipal record-keeping. The conversion had not been kind. Sacred niches that once held golden reliquaries now held ledgers. The altar had been replaced with a desk. There was a faint smell of old parchment and regret, which is the characteristic scent of any government office in Leyndell, or anywhere else for that matter.

Ystrild was a lean woman with the kind of face that had been good-looking once and was now merely precise — everything in its right place, nothing extra, like a well-drafted survey map. She did not look up when I entered, which was either a power move or genuine distraction. With Ystrild it was hard to tell. She had that quality some administrators develop, where rudeness and competence become so thoroughly fused you can't separate them without breaking both.

"Aldwyn," she said, still not looking up. "Sit."

I sat. The chair was uncomfortable, which I assume was deliberate.

"You've heard about the census."

"I've heard rumors about a census."

"Then you've heard correctly. The Elden Lord" — she paused on the title, the way people had taken to doing since the new one was crowned, as though they weren't quite sure the words still meant what they used to — "has ordered a full enumeration. Every living soul in the capital and its dependencies. Every building, every granary, every cistern, every head of livestock, every weapon in every armory, every rune of taxable value. He wants to know what he has."

"That's sensible."

"It's insane. The last full census was conducted under Morgott" — she said this name without flinching, which told me something about her — "and it took four years, sixty enumerators, and an armed escort for the team surveying the Subterranean Shunning-Grounds. We lost two men down there. Not to violence. They simply got lost and were never found. The passages aren't mapped."

"They were enumerators," I said. "They should have been counting their steps."

She looked up at me then, and I saw something I hadn't expected: she was frightened.

Not of me, obviously. I have been described as many things in my career — thorough, pedantic, an intolerable bore who would count the hairs on your head if left unsupervised — but frightening is not among them. She was frightened of the task. Or rather, she was frightened of what the task would reveal.

That's the thing about counting. People think it's the simplest skill in the world. One, two, three — a child can do it, a dog can nearly do it. But counting is not a neutral act. When you count something, you define it. You draw a line around it and say: this is one thing, and that is another, and the boundary between them is here. A warehouse that has burned down: is it a warehouse or isn't it? A citizen who died in the Shattering but whose body walks the streets at night, sustained by deathroot corruption: is he a living soul or a dead one? An Omen, born with horns and tusked jaw, imprisoned since birth in the pipes beneath the city: does he count?

This is why enumerators drink.

"You want me on the census," I said.

"I want you to lead a section. The lower city. Specifically, the Subterranean Shunning-Grounds and the connected catacombs. Every passage, every chamber, every occupant."

"The Shunning-Grounds."

"Yes."

"Where you lost two men last time."

"Yes."

"And you want me to go down there and count what's in them."

"That is literally your job, Aldwyn."

She wasn't wrong. A senior enumerator goes where the counting needs doing. I have counted grain stores in siege conditions, tallied the dead after a Misbegotten riot in the lower wards, and once spent three weeks enumerating the contents of a library where every fourth book was trapped with a golden-order ward that would discharge a bolt of light into the face of anyone who opened it without authorization. I still have a scar on my left eyebrow from that one. The librarian apologized profusely and then asked me to count the wards as well.

But the Shunning-Grounds were different. Everyone knew that. They were the bowels of Leyndell — literally, in some sections, where the sewer channels ran — and they had been used for centuries as a dumping ground for everything the Golden Order preferred not to look at. The Omen went down there. The mad went down there. The heretical, the deformed, the inconvenient. And since the Shattering, worse things had taken up residence. You heard stories. Revenant clusters, those horrible many-armed things that used to be people, crawling through the pipes in packs. Servants of the fell curse. Giant lobsters in the waterways, which is an absurd thing to be afraid of until you've seen what a giant lobster can do to a man in half-plate.

"I'll need equipment," I said. "Lanterns. Good ones — Carian crystal if you can get them. A surveyor's chain, the sixty-foot kind. Two assistants minimum. Chalk. A great deal of chalk."

"You'll have it."

"And an escort."

"I'll assign you two soldiers from the garrison."

"Make it four. And I want them armed with something better than standard-issue halberds. Those tunnels are close quarters. Short swords, shields. And at least one of them needs to be able to cast a basic golden vow. I don't care if it's just the prayer book version. I want someone who can produce enough sacred light to make a revenant think twice."

She wrote all of this down without comment, which told me she had already anticipated the requests. Ystrild was like that. She let you think you were negotiating when in fact she had made all the decisions before you walked in. A useful trait in an administrator. Infuriating in a human being.

"One more thing," I said. "The Omen."

She stopped writing.

"There are Omen living in the Shunning-Grounds. Have been for generations. Under Morgott's census they were enumerated as — and I'm quoting from the archive here — 'accursed chattel, classified non-citizen, not subject to the protections or obligations of the Golden Ordinance.' Which means they were counted, but counted as things rather than people. It affected the totals. If you count a man as livestock, your population figures drop and your livestock figures rise, which makes the city look less populated and better fed than it actually is. Morgott allowed it because—"

I stopped. Because the obvious answer — because Morgott, himself an Omen, allowed the classification of his own kind as chattel — was something I didn't understand and wasn't sure I wanted to.

"How does the new Lord want them counted?"

Ystrild set down her pen. "The order says every living soul. I interpret that literally. Count them as people."

"That's going to change the numbers considerably."

"Yes. It is."

We looked at each other. She was still frightened, but there was something else there too — a kind of grim satisfaction. The numbers were going to change. After centuries of careful falsification, of counting the way the Golden Order wanted things counted, the numbers were going to tell the truth.

Which is a terrifying prospect if you've spent your career making them lie.

I should explain something about my profession, since you're going to be hearing a great deal about it and I'd rather you understood the principles before we get to the part where I'm crawling through a sewer with a chalk stick in my teeth.

An enumerator is not a mere counter. A counter adds things up. An enumerator determines what is to be added. This is the crucial distinction, and it is the source of all our power and all our misery.

Consider a room. A counter enters a room and counts everything in it: four walls, one door, one window, six chairs, a table, a rug, a lamp. Simple. But what about the nails holding the chairs together? Are they items in the room or components of items? What about the dust on the rug? The air? The light coming through the window — is the light in the room, or does it merely pass through? What about the shadow the lamp casts on the wall — the shadow is certainly in the room, but is it a thing?

You think I'm being absurd. I am not being absurd. I once spent three days arguing with a colleague about whether the rainwater that had leaked into a granary through a cracked roof constituted part of the granary's contents. He said yes, water is a thing, it is in the granary, count it. I said no, the water is damage, not inventory. We referred the matter up the chain. The ruling came back: count the water, but in a separate column, and annotate it as "involuntary accretion." Three days of my life for that. Seven pages of documentation.

The point — and I do have one — is that enumeration is an act of philosophy disguised as arithmetic. When you count something, you are making a claim about what exists. And in Leyndell, where the fundamental nature of existence was defined by a Ring that is now broken and reforged by new hands, that claim has consequences that go well beyond bookkeeping.

Before the Shattering, the census was theological. You counted what the Golden Order said existed. The living were citizens. The dead returned to the Erdtree and were not counted because they were no longer individuated — they were part of the tree, the way a drop of water is part of the sea. Those Who Live in Death were not counted because they should not exist; to count them would be to legitimize their condition. The Omen were counted as chattel, which was a way of simultaneously acknowledging their presence and denying their personhood. The Misbegotten were listed under "livestock and beasts of labor," which at least had the decency to be straightforwardly cruel rather than theologically creative.

This system worked — in the sense that it produced numbers that confirmed what the Order already believed. The city was wealthy, well-populated, blessed by the Erdtree's grace. That the wealth was concentrated in seven families, the population included thousands of beings classified as furniture, and the Erdtree's grace was a golden phantom projected over a burned stump — these were not the enumerator's concern. We counted what we were told to count, the way we were told to count it. And the numbers, having been produced by rigorous professional method, were true.

This is the great secret of my profession: the numbers are always true. It is the categories that lie.

The new Lord changed the categories.

I don't know much about him, personally. A Tarnished of no particular reputation, which is what they always say, and which always turns out to mean something more complicated. He'd been crowned perhaps six months before the census order came through. The city was still adjusting. The ash had been cleared from the main thoroughfares but still clogged the side streets. The wax seals on doors were cracking. People were venturing out, blinking, finding that the world was different but also not different enough — the same shops, the same neighbors, the same leaky roofs, just with a new name at the top of the administrative chain.

He'd chosen some mending rune or other — I confess I don't follow the theology closely enough to say which. Something about death being woven back into the order. Fia's rune, they called it, or the Prince of Death's. What it meant in practice, for people like me, was that the old categories were dissolving. If death was no longer a one-way door to the Erdtree's roots — if Those Who Live in Death were no longer aberrations but simply people in a different state — then the census had to reflect that.

Hence my assignment. Go underground. Count what's there. Count it honestly.

The trouble with counting honestly is that honesty has a habit of being more complicated than anyone anticipated.

We descended on a Middas morning — myself, two assistants named Ferl and Gladdis, and four soldiers from the garrison, one of whom could indeed produce a passable golden vow, though he told me privately that since the Ring's reforging his incantations felt "different, like trying to play a familiar song on a retuned lute." I filed this observation away. It was not my area of expertise, but I am an enumerator; I file everything away.

The entrance to the Shunning-Grounds was through an access shaft behind the Avenue of the Baleful Shadow, down a ladder that had been repaired so many times it was more patch than original — a ship of Theseus in iron rungs. At the bottom, the smell hit us: damp stone, old rot, and something chemical and sharp that I later identified as the residue of alchemical reagents. Someone had been distilling down here. Whether it was medicine or poison was, as usual, a question of categories.

The first few chambers were straightforward. Storage rooms, mostly empty, with the kind of detritus that accumulates anywhere people have lived without cleaning: broken pottery, soiled cloth, the bones of small animals. I counted and recorded everything. Ferl held the lantern. Gladdis took notes. The soldiers stood at the doorways and tried to look alert, which is the primary function of a soldier in non-combat situations.

The difficulty began at the first junction, where the passage split into three. The left branch descended sharply. The right branch was partially flooded. The center branch was blocked by what appeared to be a mass of organic matter — roots, possibly, or some fungal growth — that had pushed through the stonework and filled the corridor to a height of about five feet.

I approached the obstruction and examined it with my lantern. The surface was fibrous and pale, with a texture somewhere between wood and flesh. It was warm to the touch. There were tiny golden filaments running through it, catching the light.

"Erdtree root," said one of the soldiers. A man named Brevald, older than the others, with the weathered look of someone who had served through the Shattering and found the experience neither glorious nor interesting.

"I know what it is," I said. "The question is whether to count it."

"Count it as what?"

"Exactly."

This was a genuine professional dilemma. The Erdtree root network extends throughout the foundations of Leyndell. Under the old census protocol, the roots were not enumerated — they were classified as a feature of the natural or divine landscape, like the sky or the golden light of grace, and therefore not subject to inventory. But the Erdtree was dead. The golden phantom projected above the city was, by theological consensus, no longer operational. And these roots — this warm, pulsing, fibrous mass blocking my corridor — were either part of the dead Erdtree, in which case they were a natural feature, or they were part of the Greattree, the older root system that predated the Golden Order, in which case they were technically an archaeological artifact.

Or — and this was the possibility that made my chalk hand tremble slightly — they were something new. Something growing in the changed conditions of the reforged Ring. Life pushing through the old channels in a form no one had categorized yet.

I made a note: ROOT OBSTRUCTION, CORRIDOR 3-C, APPROX. 5 FT HEIGHT, 8 FT DEPTH, LIVING TISSUE OF INDETERMINATE CLASSIFICATION. REQUIRES SPECIALIST ASSESSMENT. ENUMERATED PROVISIONALLY AS STRUCTURAL FEATURE.

Ferl, reading over my shoulder, said: "That's a lot of words for 'I don't know.'"

"Yes," I said. "Welcome to the profession."

We took the left branch, which descended into what the old maps called the Omen Quarter — though "quarter" implied a degree of urban planning that was generous, given the reality. What we found was a shantytown built into the sewer infrastructure. The Omen had taken the drainage channels, the maintenance alcoves, the junction chambers, and turned them into living spaces through the application of ingenuity, desperation, and an astonishing quantity of stolen material.

I say "stolen" because I am an enumerator and I am precise about these things. Every brick, every timber, every scrap of cloth in the Omen Quarter had come from somewhere in the city above, transported down through access shafts and drainage grates over what must have been decades or centuries. There were bedframes made from repurposed scaffolding. Curtains cut from temple banners. A cooking hearth constructed from interlocking pieces of a ceremonial fountain that I recognized from the Avenue of Prayer — it had been reported as "storm damage" in the records. The Omen had, in effect, built their city from the leavings of the one that rejected them.

I began counting.

The first Omen we encountered was a woman — if that is the right word, and I am going to use it because the new Lord's order says living souls and a soul does not have a species — approximately sixty years old, with a pair of vestigial horns curving back from her temples and a lower jaw that protruded into short, blunt tusks. She was mending a shoe. The shoe was human-sized, not Omen-sized, which meant she was mending it for sale or trade to someone in the city above.

She looked at us the way a cat looks at a dog that has wandered into the wrong yard.

"Census," I said.

"What?"

"We're conducting a census. On behalf of the Elden Lord. A count of all living persons and assets within the capital boundaries."

"Persons," she repeated, with the particular emphasis of someone who has not previously been included in that category.

"Yes."

She considered this. Then she called over her shoulder in a language I didn't recognize — guttural, resonant, probably a dialect that had evolved in isolation down here over generations. Three more Omen emerged from the alcove behind her. A man with a full crown of horns like a stag's rack. A younger woman with scales along her arms and neck, iridescent in the lantern light, a crucible vestige that would have been beautiful if anyone in the city above had the courage to call it that. A child, perhaps four years old, with a single small horn budding from the center of its forehead.

I counted them. I wrote down what I saw. Name (given in their language, transliterated as best I could), approximate age, distinguishing features, occupation if any, dependents. The same categories I would use for any citizen of Leyndell. The form didn't have a box for horn configuration or tusk length, so I added them in the margins, because I am thorough and because these details mattered — not to the census, but to the people being counted. Someone had looked at them and written down what they were. That might have been the first time.

The old woman watched me write. When I finished, she said: "They used to come down with saws."

I didn't ask what the saws were for. I knew. Everyone in Leyndell knew, in the way you know about the drains that carry away your sewage — the knowledge is there, you just choose not to think about where the pipe goes.

"We're not here for that," I said.

"What are you here for?"

"I told you. A count."

"And then what?"

This was the question, of course. The one I couldn't answer. I could count the Omen. I could record their names, their ages, the pathetic inventories of their stolen possessions. I could send the numbers up the chain to Ystrild, who would compile them into the grand census, which would be presented to the Elden Lord, who would look at the total and know: there are this many people living under my city, in conditions that would shame a kennel, because three hundred years ago someone decided that horns made you less than human.

And then what?

Numbers don't fix things. I know that. I've spent twenty-two years producing numbers, and in all that time I have never once seen a number repair a road, feed a child, or unsay a cruelty. Numbers describe. They do not act. The acting is someone else's department, and in my experience, that department is perpetually understaffed.

But there's a thing about numbers that administrators and theologians consistently underestimate: they persist. You can change the categories. You can burn the records. You can reclassify people as livestock and livestock as furniture and furniture as decoration and decoration as nothing. But the numbers, once written, have a weight of their own. They sit in the archive and they wait. And someday, someone pulls the ledger off the shelf and reads: in this year, in this place, there were this many people living in the dark, and no one did anything about it. The number becomes an accusation.

That's what enumeration really is. Not counting. Witnessing.

We spent eleven days in the Shunning-Grounds. I will not describe all of it, because much of it was exactly the tedious work you'd expect — measuring corridors, counting bricks (I could not help myself), cataloguing the contents of storage chambers that had been sealed for so long the air inside had turned to something barely breathable. Ferl developed a persistent cough. One of the soldiers, a young man named Tolven, walked into a tripwire left by some previous occupant and took a rusty crossbow bolt through the meat of his thigh. It was not a serious wound, but it slowed us considerably, and I lost half a day's counting while Brevald applied poultices and Tolven swore in languages I hadn't known he spoke.

But I will tell you about two things, because they are the things that stayed with me.

The first was the room of records.

We found it on the seventh day, behind a door that had been locked with a seal I recognized — Golden Order administrative, mid-period, the kind of lock used on municipal record offices. The seal was cracked, whether by age or force I couldn't tell. Inside was a room approximately twenty feet square, lined with shelves, containing ledgers.

Census ledgers.

Hundreds of them, dating back — I checked the oldest — nearly four hundred years. This was not a discovery. This was an ambush. Someone had been counting the Omen all along.

I pulled ledgers at random. The entries were in a variety of hands, some precise, some barely legible, but the format was consistent: name, age, physical description, horn type, location within the Shunning-Grounds. No occupation, no dependents, no category for "distinguishing features" because the horns were the only feature that mattered. The purpose was not enumeration. It was tracking. The Golden Order had maintained a registry of every Omen in the underground, updated regularly, for centuries.

The most recent entries were dated to just before the Shattering. The final note, in a hand I didn't recognize, read: DISCONTINUED BY ORDER OF THE OMEN KING. HE WILL NOT HAVE THEM COUNTED THUS.

Morgott. The Omen who loved the Erdtree that despised him, who ruled Leyndell in secret while his own people rotted in the dark beneath it. He had found this room, read these ledgers, and ordered them sealed.

I sat on the floor of that room for a long time. Ferl and Gladdis waited. The soldiers shifted their weight. Nobody spoke.

Here is what I thought about, sitting in that room: I thought about the enumerators who had come before me. Professional men, presumably. Thorough. They had walked these same corridors, spoken to these same people — or their parents, their grandparents — and written down what they saw. They had used different categories. Different forms. But they had done the same work. They had counted.

And the counting had not been neutral. It had not been witnessing. It had been surveillance. The numbers in these ledgers were not a record of lives. They were a record of control. Every name, every horn measurement, every location — it was all data for the saws. When the Order decided an Omen's horns had grown too large, or too numerous, or too visible, they consulted the registry, sent a team, and cut.

The same skill. The same tools. The same professional thoroughness. In the service of something entirely different.

I picked up my chalk and my notebook and I counted the ledgers. Seven hundred and forty-three. I recorded the date range, the approximate number of entries, the condition of the paper, and the name of every enumerator I could identify from the handwriting or the occasional initialed margin note. Then I noted the room's location, dimensions, and contents in my survey, cross-referenced it with the corridor map, and moved on.

What else could I do? I am an enumerator. I count things.

The second thing happened on the ninth day.

We had reached the lowest accessible level of the Shunning-Grounds, where the sewer channels connected to a natural cave system that ran — according to the old maps, which I trusted about as far as I could throw the yellowed, crumbling things — toward the Deeproot Depths. We were not going to the Deeproot Depths. Our mandate was the capital boundaries, and the Depths were well outside any plausible interpretation of those limits, even by someone with my flexible approach to architectural classification.

But at the junction, where the constructed tunnel met the natural cave, we found something.

A man. Sitting on a stone ledge. He was perhaps thirty, dressed in the remains of what had once been the uniform of a Golden Order fundamentalist — the white robes, the golden sun-disc medallion, the particular sandals with the thong between the big toe and the second toe that I had always found unreasonably uncomfortable on behalf of other people. The robes were filthy and torn. The medallion was tarnished to black. The sandals were gone.

He was not an Omen. He had no horns, no tusks, no scales. He was, as far as I could tell, an ordinary human man who had been sitting in the dark for a very long time.

He looked at us. His eyes were wrong. Not absent — present, but present the way the light is present inside a sealed room: there, but with nowhere to go.

"Census," I said, because that was my line and I did not have a better one.

He smiled. It was not a reassuring smile. "You're counting the dead too?"

"Living souls. You appear to be alive."

"Do I?" He looked down at his hands. "That's interesting. I was fairly sure I'd died. It was — let me think — it was during the siege, or the burning, or one of the other things that happened. They do blur together. But I distinctly remember dying. There was a flash, and then cold, and then I was here."

"Here."

"In the dark. For — how long has it been?"

"Since the burning of the Erdtree? About eight months."

"Ah." He processed this. "Eight months. I've been sitting here for eight months?"

"It appears so."

"Without eating or drinking."

"Apparently."

He nodded, as though this confirmed something he'd been suspecting. "Then I am dead. Or something close enough that the distinction is academic."

This was, technically, outside my area of expertise. But I have been an enumerator for twenty-two years, and in that time I have learned that the distinction between life and death is far less clear-cut than the theologians would have you believe. Particularly now. Particularly since the Ring was reforged with death woven back into it — or alongside it, or however the new theology phrases the arrangement. The old system was clean: you lived, you died, you returned to the Erdtree, you were reborn. The new system was less clean. Death was present again, real death, the destined kind, but it sat alongside the old cycle in ways that even the theologians were still working out.

In practical terms, this meant I had to make a decision. Was this man alive? He was speaking, which suggested yes. He had not eaten in eight months, which suggested no. He was sitting in a sewer talking to an enumerator about the nature of his own existence, which could go either way.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Corell. Brother Corell, if the title still applies. Fundamentalist third grade, assigned to the Cathedral of the Forsaken for — well, I suppose 'for my sins' is the honest answer, though they called it 'supplementary ministry to the marginalized.'"

"The Omen."

"Yes. I was supposed to minister to them. Bring them the word of the Golden Order. The word being, more or less, 'you are accursed and your existence is an affront to the natural law, but the Erdtree's grace is theoretically infinite, so there's hope for you yet.' Can you imagine delivering that message? To people chained in the dark? I delivered it every Tirdas for seven years."

"And then?"

"And then the world ended and I died. Or didn't die. Or something." He looked at me with those sealed-room eyes. "What happens now? Am I on your census?"

I thought about it.

Under the old categories, he was easy: if dead, not counted. If undead, hunted and destroyed by the Order's death-hunters. If alive, counted as a citizen, ranked by his fundamentalist grade, assigned a value.

Under the new categories — the categories I was helping to build, one chalk mark at a time — I had no idea.

So I did what any competent enumerator does when faced with an uncategorizable entity. I created a new column. In the margin of my notebook, next to the standard fields for name, age, occupation, and assets, I wrote: STATUS: INDETERMINATE. REQUIRES FURTHER ASSESSMENT. ALIVE BY CONVERSATIONAL EVIDENCE; DEAD BY METABOLIC EVIDENCE. RECOMMEND SPECIALIST EVALUATION.

Brother Corell watched me write. "What category did you put me in?"

"You're in the margins."

He laughed. It was a real laugh — surprised, genuine, slightly unhinged. "The margins. Yes. That's where I've always been."

I wrote down his name. I gave him a number. The same kind of number I gave the Omen woman and her family, the same kind I gave the warehouse contents and the root obstruction and the seven hundred and forty-three ledgers of horrors in the records room.

It was not enough. Numbers are never enough. But it was what I had.

We returned to the surface on the eleventh day, blinking in the pale gold light — or what passes for gold these days, a thinner color than I remember, as though the spectrum itself had been adjusted along with the Ring. I had forty-seven pages of notes, three complete corridor surveys, a partial map of the lower levels, and a population count of the Shunning-Grounds that was going to change the city's demographic profile in ways the administrative quarter was not remotely prepared for.

Ystrild took my report without comment. She read the first page, then the second, then flipped to the summary figures at the back. Her expression did not change. Ystrild's expression never changes. I think she cultivates this the way some people cultivate roses: with sustained effort and a deep commitment to keeping things pruned.

"These numbers," she said.

"Are accurate."

"I know they're accurate. You counted them." She set the report down. "The Omen population alone is three times the previous estimate."

"The previous estimate was based on the surveillance registry, which was discontinued. The actual population has been growing uncounted for — I can't say exactly. The birth records in the underground are oral, not written. But I'd estimate at least two generations of uncounted growth."

"And the — what did you call them? Indeterminate status?"

"Brother Corell. And there were others. Seven that I could identify in the lower levels. People who appear to have died during the burning and continued to exist in some form. They're conscious, communicative, and stationary. They seem to be waiting for something, though none of them could tell me what."

"Seven."

"In the sections I surveyed. The unsurveyed sections are extensive. I'd estimate" — and here I paused, because an enumerator should never estimate when he can count, but the unsurveyed tunnels were dangerous and our time was limited and sometimes you have to betray your principles in the service of a larger accuracy — "between thirty and fifty, in the Shunning-Grounds alone."

"The Order would have destroyed them."

"Yes."

"The new Lord's order says living souls."

"Yes."

"Are they living?"

"They are souls. I can confirm that much. The living part is above my pay grade."

She almost smiled. Almost. "I'll include them. Annotated. Let the theologians argue about it."

"The theologians will argue about it regardless. That's their job. Ours is to give them something accurate to argue about."

This is what I believe, if you want to know. I don't talk about beliefs much. An enumerator is supposed to be objective — a measuring instrument, not a philosopher. But twenty-two years of counting things has given me one conviction, and it is this: the most powerful thing in the world is not a sword, not a spell, not a rune of cosmic law. It is a number written down.

A number written down is a promise. It says: this was here. This existed. This mattered enough for someone to stop, and look, and make a mark. You can burn books. I know that. People have burned very important books in the history of this city, and the world changed or didn't change as a result. You can deface sculptures, tear down monuments, rewrite the prayers. You can shatter the very Ring that defines the laws of nature and reforge it to your liking.

But a number, once written in a census ledger and filed in a municipal archive, has a persistence that borders on the miraculous. It sits there. It waits. And a hundred years from now, or three hundred, some junior enumerator will pull the ledger off the shelf and read: in this year, in the Shunning-Grounds of Leyndell, there were one thousand, two hundred and fourteen Omen, and they were counted as people, and their names were these.

It's not justice. It's not even close to justice. But it is the thing that comes before justice — the acknowledgment that there is something to be just about.

I filed my report. I cleaned my chalk. I went home.

My home is a single room on the third tier, with a window that looks out toward where the Erdtree used to be. Or still is, or isn't, depending on your theology. What I see is a trunk of pale gold light, thinner than before, and at its base a mass of something dark and knotted that might be the original wood or might be new growth. Nobody knows yet. The scholars are arguing. The fundamentalists are praying. The enumerators are, presumably, trying to measure it.

I sat in my chair — the same chair I've sat in every evening for twelve years, its left rear leg shimmed with a folded piece of parchment that I should replace but never will because the shim has become part of the chair, and to remove it would change the total — and I looked at the tree, or the memory of the tree, or whatever it is now.

Then I took out my notebook and I counted my fingers. Ten. Same as yesterday. Same as every day.

It's a habit I've had since childhood. My mother thought it was charming. My colleagues think it's mad. I think it's the only sensible thing a man can do in a world where the laws of nature are subject to amendment: check the basics. Make sure the count hasn't changed. Make sure you're still here.

Ten fingers. One enumerator. One thousand, two hundred and fourteen names in the margins, waiting for someone to do something about them.

That was three months ago. The census is still in progress. The arguments about categories have, if anything, intensified. There is a faction in the Chancellery that wants to count the root-obstruction as a building. There is another faction that wants to classify Brother Corell and his six indeterminate companions as "natural phenomena." Last week, someone proposed counting the golden phantom of the Erdtree as an asset of the municipal treasury, on the grounds that it provides ambient light and therefore constitutes infrastructure.

I am staying out of these debates. I have done my counting. The numbers are filed. They will sit in the archive, in their careful columns, and they will wait.

That's what numbers do. That's all they have to do.
