The Stonemason

by Kontextmaschine

The thing about the Golden Order is it was a standard-issue colonial theocracy that happened to be run by an actual god, which sounds like it would make things simpler but actually makes them much worse, because the entity establishing the ideological framework for the ruling class also literally defines the physical laws of reality. Like, in most empires the priesthood is at best claiming divine sanction for their temporal arrangements, and the gap between claim and reality is where you get your political flexibility – your reformations, your schisms, your plausible reinterpretations. When the god is right there and the laws of nature are literally editable parameters in her operating system, the theology isn't just an ideology, it's physics, and dissent isn't heresy, it's trying to violate thermodynamics.

Except Marika shattered her own system. Which is – okay.

So there's this knight. Havel. Not his real name. I'm going to call him Havel because his armor looked like the stuff they quarry out of the cliffs south of Stormveil and because he refused to tell anyone at the Hold what he was actually called, which at the time everyone interpreted as dramatic Tarnished mystique but in retrospect was probably because his name was something embarrassing from wherever-the-hell beyond the Sea of Fog the Long March originally went.

Havel had been back in the Lands Between for – it's hard to know, time gets weird when death doesn't stick, but call it long enough to have died at Margit twice, died in the Stormveil knife-bird hallway once (everyone dies in the knife-bird hallway once), and made it through to Liurnia before he decided to stop, which is actually a respectable showing for a Tarnished who wasn't one of the big names.

He stopped at one of the churches.

The churches are interesting structurally – these little roadside chapels scattered absolutely everywhere, built to collect the golden sap that used to drip from the Erdtree's boughs. Used to. The Erdtree the player sees is a spectral projection of the original, which burned (the ash all over Leyndell is from this), so the sap collection infrastructure is vestigial. The churches are like gas stations on a decommissioned highway. But Tarnished keep using them as rest stops anyway because they're sited at natural travel chokepoints and because grace tends to pool there, and the Sites of Grace are the real infrastructure the Tarnished care about.

Havel sat in this church – the one at the north end of the lake, with the stripped roof and the view across the water toward Raya Lucaria – and he looked at the Academy and decided: no.

Not a crisis of faith. More like – have you ever had the experience of being sent to do something by an authority you don't particularly respect, and partway through you realize the task itself is fine but the framing is wrong? Like your boss tells you to write a report justifying a decision they already made, and you go along because the report needs writing anyway and you need the paycheck, but somewhere around page ten you look up and think, "wait, do I actually care whether this guy's pre-existing conclusion is vindicated?"

The Two Fingers told the Tarnished: go collect Great Runes and become Elden Lord.

Havel did the math. The Great Runes are held by demigods. The weakest demigod, Godrick – the embarrassing one, the one who compensates for dynastic inadequacy by literally grafting other people's arms onto himself, a grotesque literalization of "fake it till you make it" – took the combined strength of a small army to drive into his castle, and then Havel still died twice to his personal security. The remaining demigods include the greatest swordswoman who ever lived (who destroyed an entire region of the continent as a side effect of ONE FIGHT), and a gravity sorcerer so powerful he's holding the stars in place (but has gone insane and eats corpses in a swamp, which, Havel thought, was probably still worse to fight than the sane version because at least the sane version had self-preservation instincts).

And the reward for clearing this gauntlet is you get to go fix the thing the actual god broke on purpose.

Havel looked at this incentive structure and thought: I fought my way out of whatever afterlife the Fog dumped me in, crossed an ocean, killed things that should not be killable, all because some trembling elderly fingers told me to. The fingers that are, according to everyone's best understanding, the local branch office of a cosmic entity whose motives are never stated directly and whose communication with its own representatives has slowed to the point where interpreting their twitches is a specialized profession.

This is – and I'm not being flip here – this is exactly the Whiskey Rebellion problem. The central authority issues a mandate. The mandate requires enormous personal sacrifice from the people tasked with carrying it out. The institutional capacity to actually support those people is essentially nonexistent (the Roundtable Hold is a waystation outside physical reality that contains: a blacksmith, a weird doll lady, a man who wants to defile your corpse, and an arms dealer who is also a giant tortoise). And the people doing the actual work on the ground look around and go, "the infrastructure here does not match the ambition of the mission statement."

So Havel stopped.

He did not go hollow. He did not turn to the Frenzied Flame. He did not join the Recusants at Volcano Manor (assassination guild, cool aesthetic, but still oriented around a guy who fed himself to a snake, and Havel had a general policy against organizations whose founder had been eaten). He just... stayed at the church.

He fixed the roof.

This sounds trivial but it actually wasn't. The churches were designed to collect Erdtree sap, and the architecture is specialized – these low stone walls with channels cut into the capstones to direct flow toward a central basin. When the roof goes, rain gets in the channels and the whole collection system becomes a drainage problem instead. Havel had been a stoneworker in whatever life preceded his tarnishing (or between deaths, it's unclear, the biographical details get hazy), and he looked at the structural situation and thought: I can do something about this.

He sourced timber from the woods south of the church. He killed the wolves that objected to this. He died once to wolves, which was humiliating, but they were the big Liurnia wolves and one of them got behind him while he was carrying a log, so.

The Albinaurics noticed.

The Albinaurics are an artificial race – created by human hands, first generation with proper bodies that eventually lose mobility, second generation cruder and used as disposable labor, both rejected by the Golden Order – and there was a small settlement of them in the marshes west of the lake. They had been, in the technical terminology, getting absolutely fucked on by the Cuckoo soldiers from Raya Lucaria, who were given essentially free rein to do whatever they wanted militarily and used this latitude in the way that garrisoned soldiers with no real enemy and no oversight always have.

A Tarnished who was not passing through but actually staying put and fixing things was a genuinely novel phenomenon for them.

This is – okay, here's the real point. There's this widespread Tarnished sense that "if I'm not collecting Great Runes, I'm not doing the thing," but the thing the Two Fingers mandated and the thing the Lands Between actually needs are not the same thing. The Lands Between needs – well, it needs what any post-collapse region needs: someone to fix the roof, someone to keep the soldiers from shaking down the civilians, someone to figure out what the local economy runs on now that the old one is gone.

The Golden Order was a comprehensive system. It handled death (which cycled through the Erdtree), it handled law (through the theology), it handled legitimacy (through grace). When it broke, everything broke, and the Two Fingers' proposed solution was "find a very strong person to put it back together," which is the exact response you'd expect from an institution that has only ever understood power as top-down.

Havel fixed the roof. Then he fixed the wall where it had collapsed on the east side. An Albinauric whose legs were failing asked if Havel could build a chair that would let her sit at a workable height, and he did, and then he built three more because she wasn't the only one. A merchant came through – one of the Great Caravan survivors, the ones whose entire people were buried alive for heresy, and who now travel the roads selling their wares without discussing what happened – and Havel traded some wolf pelts for tools and the merchant mentioned that the bridge two miles east had lost its keystone.

Havel fixed the bridge.

The Cuckoo soldiers came by once. They saw a Tarnished with a big hammer and the look of someone who had killed a demigod's worth of creatures on the way here and they decided the Albinauric village was not worth the hassle today.

This is the thing about state incapacity in a post-theological environment: the formal power structure (the Two Fingers, the demigods, the Roundtable Hold) is oriented toward the grand succession question – who gets to be Elden Lord, who gets to define reality's parameters next – and meanwhile on the ground, in the actual territory where actual people live, authority devolves to whoever shows up and does something useful. Which historically is how you get – feudalism, basically. Local strongmen filling the vacuum. The Tarnished were supposed to be temporary – pass through, collect runes, ascend to lordship – but the ones who stick around become the local power whether they meant to or not.

(This is also how you get Volcano Manor, incidentally – Rykard offered an alternative institutional framework for people who correctly identified that the existing one was broken, but his alternative was "I will eat everything," which is charismatic in the way cult leaders are charismatic but is not a governing philosophy.)

Havel didn't become Elden Lord.

There's no ending for that. No cutscene. No rune to collect. Grace still glowed faintly at the church and he ignored it the way you ignore a notification from an app you stopped using but haven't uninstalled.

The Albinauric woman – the one with the chair – her name was Pidia, or something close to it (the Albinauric naming conventions are a whole thing, involving the circumstances of their creation rather than parentage, because they were made, not born, and this is the kind of detail the Golden Order's record-keeping never bothered with because the Golden Order didn't consider them real people). She asked him once why he stopped.

"The fingers told me to go kill gods," Havel said. "I'm a stonemason."

And she said – and this is the part that matters – she said, "The Erdtree told us we weren't people and we believed it for a hundred years."

The god who defines reality can make you believe anything about yourself. Especially if the evidence is literally built into the physics of the world. The Albinaurics were rejected by the Erdtree's grace, which in a world where grace is a physical phenomenon (visible golden light, tangible guidance) means their metaphysical status as non-persons was empirically verifiable. And they believed it. Until the system broke, and grace stopped working reliably for anyone, and it turned out the infrastructure of cosmic legitimacy was no more durable than a church roof.

Havel kept fixing things. More Tarnished came through, most of them passing toward the Academy or north to the plateau, and a few of them stopped for a night and then moved on, and a very few of them looked at the repaired church and the functional bridge and the Albinaurics going about their business unmolested and thought: oh. You can just do this?

The answer to "who gets to define the parameters of reality" turned out to be, for one very small area around one stripped-roof church on the north shore of a flooded lake in a continent whose god had broken herself in half: nobody. Nobody defines the parameters. You fix the roof, and the roof is fixed, and that's the whole theology.

Not the worst ending.
