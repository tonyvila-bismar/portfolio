The Tithe Of Forms

by Bartuk Codex

The Tithe of Forms

For as long as anyone in the district could remember, the Temple of the Aspect Gracious had charged a tithe of one-ninth.

This was, by the standards of Crucible-era religious institutions, moderate. The temple at Stormhill charged a flat annual assessment regardless of income. The southern sanctuaries operated on a gift economy that somehow always produced gifts of exactly the right size. The eastern provinces had experimented briefly with a voluntary contribution model, which had ended the way voluntary contribution models always end — with a building fund that could not afford a building.

One-ninth was workable. It was enough to maintain the temple grounds, pay the bonesetters, stock the surgical theaters, fund the armor subsidy, and keep the archive of aspect registries current. It was not enough to make the temple wealthy. This was, in the opinion of Harad, who had served as the temple's treasurer for twenty-three years, exactly right.

Harad was not a priest. He had never aspected. He was a small, precise man who wore the same style of coat every day — wool, undyed, three buttons — and who could tell you, without consulting his ledgers, the current cost of horn-clearance surgery in six districts, the quarterly depreciation rate on bespoke aspect-rated armor, and the exact date the archive's ink budget would exceed its allocation. He had been hired because the previous treasurer had been a priest, and priests, in Harad's experience, believed that the sacred nature of the temple's mission exempted it from the profane necessity of balancing its accounts. The previous treasurer had left a deficit that took Harad four years to close.

The accounts told a story. Harad had known this for a long time, but it took him most of his career to understand what story they were telling.

Here is what the Temple of the Aspect Gracious spent its money on, in order of magnitude:

Armor subsidies: thirty-one percent. The temple maintained a fund that covered half the cost of bespoke armor for any aspected citizen who could not afford full price. The armor had to be refitted on average every fourteen months, because aspects changed. A horn that grew two inches in a year required a new helmet channel. A tail that thickened required restructured hip plates. Scaling that spread from the forearms to the torso required an entirely new cuirass. The subsidy was the temple's largest single expenditure and had been for as long as the records existed.

Surgical services: twenty-two percent. The bonesetters worked out of the temple's east wing. Horn-related procedures accounted for most of the volume — alignment corrections, growth management, the occasional emergency intervention when a horn grew inward instead of outward. The mortality rate had been declining for decades but was still, in Harad's private opinion, unacceptable. One procedure in eleven resulted in a fatality. The bonesetters considered this excellent. Harad had buried the numbers in an appendix of his annual report where the priests would not read them.

Infrastructure maintenance: eighteen percent. Wider doorframes. Higher ceilings. Reinforced floors for citizens whose scaling made them significantly heavier than baseline. Adjustable furniture. The temple itself was built to accommodate aspect variation, which meant nothing in it was standard. Standard costs less. The temple paid the difference.

The archive: nine percent. A complete registry of every aspect manifestation in the district, going back one hundred and forty years. Updated quarterly. Cross-referenced by family, geography, aspect type, and severity. The archive was Harad's private pride. He did not know what it was for. He maintained it because the data existed and because not maintaining it seemed like a waste. Twenty-three years of meticulous record-keeping in search of a question.

Staff, administration, and ritual: the remaining twenty percent.

These numbers were not secret. Harad published them annually. Nobody read them. The priests cared about the rituals. The citizens cared about the armor subsidy. The bonesetters cared about their surgical theater. Nobody cared about the numbers, which was, Harad reflected, the usual relationship between an institution and the person counting its money.

Then a woman named Vesna came to see him.

Vesna was an armorer — the best in the district, though she would not have said so. She worked out of a forge on the eastern wall and she had been receiving the temple's armor subsidy payments for nineteen years. She came to Harad's office on a Tuesday afternoon in autumn, sat in the chair across from his desk, and said: "I need to show you something."

She had brought a ledger of her own. Not accounts — measurements. Nineteen years of detailed dimensional records for every aspected citizen she had fitted. Horn angles. Scale density. Tail circumference. Wing-case dimensions. Shoulder width adjustments by quarter. Growth rates plotted against age, sex, family line, and geographic origin within the district.

Harad looked at her data for a long time.

"You see it," she said.

He did.

The aspects were converging. Over nineteen years — a short window, admittedly, but Vesna's measurements were precise to tolerances that the temple's own archive could not match — the variety of aspect forms in the district had been narrowing. Fewer horn geometries. Fewer scale patterns. The growth rates were stabilizing around a tighter band of values. The bodies she armored were becoming, slowly but measurably, more similar to each other.

Harad went to his archive. He had one hundred and forty years of aspect registries. He had never analyzed them for convergence because he had never had a reason to. He spent three weeks on the analysis.

Vesna was right.

The data was unambiguous. In the earliest decades of the archive, the district had produced over fifty distinct aspect types. In the most recent decade, the number was nineteen. The decline was not uniform — it had accelerated in the last forty years. And the aspects that survived were not random. They were the ones that appeared in the Crucible Knights.

Horn curvature matching the standard knight profile. Tail articulation matching the knight combat forms. Scale patterns matching the knight ceremonial armor specifications. Wing-case geometry matching the knight aerial incantation postures. The district's population was not producing fewer aspects. It was producing fewer kinds of aspects. And the kinds it was converging toward were the kinds that the Crucible Knights had been training for centuries.

Harad sat with his ledgers and considered what this meant.

The temple's theology held that aspects were gifts — bestowed by the Crucible's primordial energy on those it favored, distributed without pattern, each one sacred and unique. The randomness was the point. The sacred was not a system. It was grace.

Harad's numbers said otherwise. The sacred was a system. It had patterns. It had trends. It was converging on a template. And the template looked like a Crucible Knight.

He brought the analysis to the head priest, a woman named Tura who had served the temple for thirty years and who had twin horn ridges along her brow that gave her the permanent expression of someone about to ask a difficult question.

"You are telling me," Tura said, "that the Crucible is making fewer kinds of people."

"I am telling you that the data shows a decline in aspect diversity consistent with directional selection toward a specific phenotypic profile."

"In language that a priest can use, Harad."

"The Crucible is making fewer kinds of people."

Tura was silent for a long time. She looked at the charts Harad had drawn — careful, precise charts on good paper, because the numbers deserved good paper.

"What does this mean for the budget?" she asked.

Harad almost smiled. Twenty-three years and the institution's first question was always the same.

"If the trend continues," he said, "the armor subsidy will decrease. Fewer aspect types means more standardization. Standardized armor costs less than bespoke. The surgical budget will decrease as well — the converging aspects are the stable ones, the ones that grow predictably and rarely require intervention. Infrastructure costs will stabilize. In thirty years, if the trend holds, the temple's operating budget could decrease by a third."

"That sounds like good news."

"It is efficient."

"But?"

Harad paused. He was a treasurer. He did not do theology and he did not do prophecy and he did not, as a rule, speculate about matters outside his ledgers. But he had spent three weeks inside the data and the data had shown him something he could not un-see.

"The tithe of one-ninth," he said, "was calculated on the assumption that the Crucible produces infinite variety. That is the theological basis for the temple's mission — we exist to accommodate every possible form the sacred might take. Our costs are high because the forms are unpredictable. Our bonesetters train for every contingency. Our armorers build for bodies that have never existed before. Our doorframes are wider than they need to be because we do not know what will walk through them."

"Yes."

"If the Crucible is converging, then in time there will be only a few forms. Predictable forms. The doorframes will not need to be wide. The armor will not need to be bespoke. The bonesetters will not need to train for every contingency. They will need to learn one procedure. Perhaps two."

"And the temple?"

"The temple will not need to exist."

The silence in the room was the specific silence of an institution being told that its purpose is temporary. Harad had heard it before — not in temples, but in counting houses, in trade guilds, in any organization that had built its identity around solving a problem that was quietly solving itself.

Tura studied the charts again. She was not a stupid woman. She could read a trend line.

"How long?" she asked.

"At the current rate of convergence, the aspect diversity in this district will reach functional uniformity within eight to twelve generations. Perhaps three hundred years. Perhaps less, if the acceleration continues."

"And then?"

"And then there will be one kind of aspected person. Or perhaps two kinds. They will look like Crucible Knights. Their armor will be standard. Their surgeries will be routine. Their doorframes will be the same width as everyone else's."

"And the temple?"

"Will be a building," Harad said. "A well-maintained building with very accurate records and no purpose."

Tura did not argue. Harad had expected argument — theological objection, institutional denial, the reflexive defense of a mission against the data that rendered it obsolete. Instead she sat quietly and looked at her hands. She had long fingers. The horn ridges on her brow caught the light.

"There is another interpretation," she said.

Harad waited.

"If the Crucible is converging — if there is a template, a final form — then perhaps the temple's mission was never to accommodate infinite variety. Perhaps it was to shepherd the convergence. To keep people alive and armored and surgically intact while their bodies figured out what they were becoming."

"That is a more expensive interpretation," Harad said.

"How so?"

"Because it means the mission has an endpoint. And an institution with an endpoint must plan for its own obsolescence. The tithe of one-ninth funds a permanent operation. If the operation is temporary, the tithe should be higher now — to build a reserve — and zero later. The transition requires a financial restructuring that the current governance model is not designed to execute."

"You are telling me we need to save for our own funeral."

"I am telling you that a responsible institution plans for the possibility that it will not always be needed. And that the planning costs money. And that nobody wants to pay for it, because paying for your own obsolescence is, in my experience, the single most difficult expenditure to authorize."

Tura looked at him. Then she said: "Will you draw up the plan?"

The next morning, a delegation from the eastern provinces arrived. Three men in fine robes with an escort of temple guards. They had come to propose a merger — the eastern temple's voluntary contribution model had collapsed for the fourth time, and they wanted to fold their district into the Aspect Gracious's tithe system.

The lead delegate's name was Orryn. He was an expansionist. He saw in the merger an opportunity to extend the temple's reach, consolidate aspect services across a wider territory, and — though he did not say this — increase the institution's prestige. He spoke for forty minutes about synergies.

(A note about synergies, because the word is doing a great deal of work and deserves to be examined. In Orryn's usage, "synergies" meant: we have a district full of aspected citizens who need armor and surgery and wider doorframes, and you have the infrastructure to provide those things, and if we combine our populations the per-citizen cost decreases because fixed costs are spread across a larger base. This is true in principle. It is the logic of every institutional merger in every era. It is also, in Harad's experience, never true in practice, because the institution proposing the merger invariably understates its liabilities by exactly the amount needed to make the arithmetic work. The eastern temple's presented figures showed deferred maintenance costs of eight hundred units. Harad estimated the actual figure at closer to three thousand. This is not dishonesty. It is the specific blindness of institutions that have stopped auditing themselves — they genuinely do not know how much they owe because they have not looked. The merger would transfer that ignorance to the Aspect Gracious's balance sheet, where it would become knowledge, and knowledge is expensive.)

Harad listened. He took notes. He calculated.

When Orryn finished, Harad said: "No."

The room went quiet.

"The merger would increase our revenue by approximately twenty percent," Harad said. "It would increase our obligations by approximately forty percent. The eastern district has deferred maintenance on its surgical theaters for a decade. Its aspect registry has not been updated in seven years. Its armor subsidy operates on a first-come basis with no means testing, which means the wealthiest aspected citizens consume the largest share of the fund. Absorbing these obligations would require either raising the tithe — which our citizens will not accept — or drawing down reserves that we need for a different purpose."

"What purpose?" Orryn asked.

Harad looked at Tura. She looked back.

"Planning," Harad said.

He did not explain further. Orryn argued. The delegation argued. Tura, to her credit, said nothing. At the end of the day the delegation left without a merger. Orryn shook Harad's hand on the way out. The grip was firm, the smile professional. He would be back in a year with better numbers. They always came back with better numbers.

That evening, Harad sat in his office and opened Vesna's measurement ledger again. He turned to the most recent entries. A boy of fourteen whose horn geometry matched the Crucible Knight standard profile to within two degrees of arc. A woman of thirty whose scaling had stabilized into a pattern indistinguishable from the knight ceremonial template. An infant whose tail, measured at three days old, already showed the articulation signature that the knights spent years training to achieve.

He turned to his own archive. The tithe records going back twenty-three years. Every payment, every disbursement, every expenditure, every cost. The price of maintaining bodies in flux. The price of accommodation. The price of building wider doorframes and training bonesetters for every possible configuration of the human form.

He thought about what Tura had said — that the temple's mission might not be infinite accommodation but finite shepherding. That the cost might be temporary. That there was an end point, and that reaching it meant the work had succeeded, not failed.

He thought about the merger he had refused. Twenty percent more revenue, forty percent more obligation. The mathematics of expansion when the mission is contracting. The institutional instinct to grow when the correct move is to plan for decline.

He closed the ledger. He began to draft the obsolescence plan.

There was one more thing. A detail he had noticed in Vesna's measurements but had not mentioned to Tura, because it was not a budget item and Harad dealt in budget items.

The boy of fourteen — the one whose horn geometry matched the knight standard to within two degrees — was the son of a woman Harad had known for years. She came to the temple every quarter to pay her tithe. She had wild, asymmetric scaling across her shoulders and neck, no two patches the same texture, a body that looked like it had been designed by a committee that could not agree on anything. She was one of the last citizens in the district whose aspects did not fit a template. Her armor cost more than anyone else's. Her son's would cost almost nothing.

She was the generation the Crucible was leaving behind. Her son was the generation it was converging toward. And the difference between them — the mother's glorious, expensive, unrepeatable chaos and the son's clean, affordable, standard-issue divinity — was the difference between a temple that needed to exist and a building with accurate records.

Harad wrote that down too. Not in the obsolescence plan. In the margin of his personal ledger, where the priests would not read it.

It would take the rest of his career. He would not live to see the temple close. But the numbers would be right. The reserves would be adequate. The transition would be orderly. The bonesetters would be retrained. The armorers would shift to standard production. The archive — his archive, his twenty-three years of meticulous data — would be the record of a convergence that nobody had noticed until a treasurer asked the wrong question and an armorer brought the right measurements.

The tithe of one-ninth would decrease, and then it would end.

And the doorframes — the beautiful, generous, unnecessary doorframes — would be rebuilt to standard width, and nobody would remember why they had ever been wider.

That was the cost of convergence. It was not expensive. It was not cheap.

It was exact.
