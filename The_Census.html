The Census

by Eitan

The Litany of Forms says that every aspect is a prayer made flesh. I want you to hold that thought while I tell you about the boy with the vestigial wing.

It was on the left side. A single wing, roughly the size and shape of a large plucked chicken, sprouting from his scapula at an angle that would have made it aerodynamically useless even if it had been full-sized, which it was not. The bone structure was sound — I checked, because I always check, because the alternative is trusting the temple assessors and the last time I trusted a temple assessor he classified a tail as "nascent caudal finning" and I spent three weeks redesigning a formation to accommodate a man who could not sit in a saddle.

The boy was eleven. He was also, and I want to be precise about this, terrified.

Not of me, though I am aware that I am terrifying. (Six foot four, broad enough to block a doorway without trying, hands that look like they were designed for a purpose other than writing census reports. The Litany says the body is a vessel of the sacred. My body is a vessel of the inconvenient.) He was terrified of Archivist Maren, who had been explaining to him for the better part of an hour that his wing was a blessing.

I have met a lot of people who are being told they are blessed. They have a specific expression. It looks like nausea.

Maren and I had been walking the northern circuit for nine days. The temple had assigned her to me, or possibly me to her — the paperwork was ambiguous, which in my experience means both parties believe they are in charge and whoever drafted the orders knew it and was entertained. The assignment was straightforward: census the aspected population of the Greenvale district, assess each individual for knight candidacy, and compile a report for the Chapter. I would handle the military evaluation. Maren would handle the liturgical classification. Together we would produce a document that satisfied both the garrison and the temple.

Together. I want you to appreciate that word for a moment.

In nine days we had examined forty-three aspected individuals across six villages. We had agreed on the classification of exactly eight.

The problem, if you are curious, is not that Maren is wrong. The problem is much worse than that. Maren is right. The Litany does say every aspect is sacred, and the Litany is correct, and I believe the Litany, and none of that changes the fact that a vestigial wing on a boy's left scapula means he cannot wear standard plate without a custom fitting that will cost the garrison four months of an armorer's time and produce a suit that will need to be refitted every six months as the wing grows or doesn't.

The Litany does not have a section on armorer scheduling.

I checked.

Here is what the northern circuit looks like, for those of you who have never had the pleasure. You leave the garrison at Thornwall before dawn, because the roads are better in the cold and because I do not like talking to people before I have had something to drink, and Maren is the kind of person who talks at dawn, and the only defense against someone who talks at dawn is to already be walking fast enough that conversation requires effort. The Greenvale road follows the river Ash northward through increasingly sparse farmland. The villages are strung along the valley floor like beads on a fraying cord — each one smaller than the last, each one more suspicious of visitors in armor.

I should tell you what I mean by aspected, because we are a long way from the great temples and the word means something different out here.

In Thornwall, an aspected individual is someone who has manifested at least one trait of the primordial Crucible — horns, tail, scales, wings, dense bone, altered musculature, the full catalogue. In the great temples they classify these into seventeen categories with subcategories and sub-subcategories, and somewhere in the records there is a clerk who can tell you the difference between a Type III caudal extension and a Type IV, and that clerk has never met a person with a tail.

In Greenvale, aspected means your kid woke up with something growing out of them and you don't know what it is.

The distinction matters.

It matters because the temple's classification system assumes a baseline of knowledge that these families do not have. The great temples train assessors who can look at a horn and tell you its growth curve, its likely final dimensions, its load-bearing capacity. Maren is one of these assessors. She is excellent at her job. She can glance at a boy's wing and tell you it is a secondary vestigial manifestation, Class VII, subcategory B, expected growth plateau at roughly twice its current span, structurally analogous to the greater pinion but lacking the musculature for sustained flight.

She can tell the boy all of this. What she cannot tell him is what to do about it.

That is my job.

The military evaluation is simpler than the liturgical classification and harder to stomach. I am looking for three things. First: can the aspect be integrated into combat training? A full tail is useful. Dense bone is useful. Wings — real wings, the kind that work — transform a knight into something the infantry has no answer for. Second: can the individual be trained around the aspect if it is not combat-useful? A partial horn that does not interfere with a helm is not an advantage, but it is not a disqualifier. Third: will the aspect actively impede service? A wing that throws off a soldier's balance, scales that grow inward and cause pain, horns at angles that make it impossible to wear standard-issue equipment.

The first category I mark as candidates. The second I mark as conditional. The third I mark as liturgical — which is the military's polite way of saying "not our problem, send them to the temple."

I do not enjoy this. I do not enjoy any part of this. I am telling you about it because you asked, or because you are reading this report, or because I am talking to myself on a road in the Greenvale at dusk and the Archivist is finally quiet and I need to hear someone's voice, even if it is my own.

The boy with the wing. His name was Edric. He lived in the fourth village, a place called Ashmere that consisted of eleven houses, a grain store, and a temple the size of my bedroom at Thornwall, which is to say not very large. His mother had brought him to the temple when the wing appeared six months ago. The temple had classified it, blessed it, and told her it was sacred. Then they told her to wait for the census.

She had been waiting for six months. The wing had grown. Edric could no longer sleep on his back.

Maren examined the wing with the careful reverence she brings to every examination. She measured it. She palpated the bone structure. She made notes in the liturgical register using abbreviations I have never learned and she has never offered to teach me. When she was done she looked at me with the expression that meant she was about to tell me something I was going to disagree with.

"Class VII-B," she said. "Non-functional but structurally developing. The Litany is clear. A developing aspect is a prayer in progress."

"A prayer in progress," I said.

"The sacred does not arrive complete. It unfolds."

"It unfolds," I said, and I meant it as a question, but it came out flat, which is a problem I have. Things that are questions in my head arrive as statements in the air. Maren has learned to read the difference. Most people have not. This is why most people find me rude and Maren merely finds me exasperating.

"You're going to say it's a liability."

"It's a liability."

"The Litany — "

"The Litany does not have a section on load-bearing armor. I've checked. There is a beautiful passage about the tree dreaming forms into flesh, and another about the sacred obligation to honor what the tree provides, and a third about the sin of trimming what the Crucible has grown. None of them address the question of what happens when a formation of thirty-two knights has to accommodate one soldier who lists to the left because he has a wing that serves no purpose except to make his cuirass fit badly."

Maren closed the register. She does this when she is about to say something she considers important. It is the liturgical equivalent of a throat-clearing.

"Commander Veran," she said. "The question is not whether the wing is useful."

"The question is always whether the wing is useful. I am a military officer conducting a military census for a military purpose. Usefulness is the question."

"The question," she said, with the patience of someone who has been having this argument for nine days and intends to have it for nine more, "is whether you believe the Litany."

This is the part where I am supposed to say yes. I am a sworn knight of the Crucible. I have taken the oaths. I recite the Forms at dawn with the rest of the chapter, and I mean them, and the Litany of Forms is the oldest and most beautiful text in the tradition, and it says — it says with a clarity that leaves no room for interpretation — that every aspect is sacred because every aspect is a fragment of the original life, the great undifferentiated everything that existed before form, before species, before the tree sorted the world into categories.

Every aspect. Not the useful ones. Not the combat-ready ones. Every one.

I believe this.

I also have to file a report that sorts eleven-year-old boys into categories.

The Litany does not address this contradiction. The Litany was written by people who worshiped the Crucible from the inside, who lived in a time when the monstrous and the divine were the same word, when a child growing a wing was cause for celebration and not for a six-month wait in a village of eleven houses while a military bureaucracy decided whether the wing counted.

I told the boy he was conditional. This meant he would be reevaluated in a year. If the wing stabilized, he could train with modification. If it continued to grow at its current rate, he would be reclassified as liturgical. Maren noted the classification without comment. She disagreed. I knew she disagreed. She knew I knew.

We walked to the next village in silence.

I should tell you about the other cases, or at least the ones that stayed with me. A woman in the second village, Briarford, had scales along both forearms — dense, overlapping, the color of river stone. Beautiful work, if you think of the Crucible as a craftsman, which the Litany does. The scales had come in over a period of years, starting when she was sixteen. She was thirty-one now. They had stabilized completely. I marked her as a full candidate in less time than it takes to eat breakfast.

She did not want to be a knight. She was a potter. The scales made her forearms resistant to kiln heat, which she considered more useful than anything the garrison could offer, and she told me this with the direct confidence of a person who has thought about it and is not interested in being argued with. I wrote "declined" next to her candidacy, which is a classification the form technically does not have. I invented it. The Chapter will not like that either.

In the third village there was an old man — seventy, maybe older — whose horns had been growing his entire life. They curved back from his temples in a double spiral, massive, the weight of them visibly pulling at the muscles of his neck. He had never been assessed. He had never been to a temple. He had lived his whole life in a village of nine houses and worked as a shepherd and his horns had been, to him, simply the shape of his head. When Maren began her examination he asked her, politely, what she was looking for.

She told him she was classifying his aspect.

He asked what that meant.

I walked outside and stood in the road for a while. There are moments in this job when the distance between the institution and the person it claims to serve becomes so vast that you can feel the ground shift under your feet, as though the terrain itself is embarrassed.

There is a passage in the Litany — I am going from memory here, which means I am probably getting two words wrong, which Maren would correct and I am grateful she is not reading this — that describes the Crucible as "the forge that does not choose its steel." The image is of a great fire that takes in everything and produces forms without preference or plan. A horn and a wing and a tail and a thickness of bone are all the same to the Crucible. It does not rank them. It does not evaluate them for military utility. It creates, and what it creates is sacred by the fact of its creation.

This is the most dangerous passage in the Litany, and I say this as someone who loves it.

It is dangerous because it is true, and the truth of it makes every practical decision I have to make feel like a betrayal. Every time I classify someone as "liturgical" — which means "not suited for combat training" — I am imposing a hierarchy on something the Crucible did not create hierarchically. I am saying: this aspect is less than that aspect. Not in sacred terms. In practical ones. But the practical and the sacred are not as separate as the temple would like to believe, because the practical categories are the ones that determine whether you eat regular meals and sleep in a garrison or go home to a village and wait.

The Litany says the Crucible does not choose its steel.

I choose. That is my job.

We finished the circuit in eleven days. Forty-three aspected individuals examined. Eight full candidates. Fourteen conditional. Twenty-one liturgical. Maren filed the liturgical register. I filed the military assessment. The two documents described the same forty-three people and had almost nothing in common.

On the last night before we returned to Thornwall, we camped by the river. Maren was reading the Litany by firelight — she does this every evening, the way I used to sharpen my sword before I started writing reports instead. I was writing the summary. The summary is the part of the census that goes to the Chapter Commander, and it is the part where I have to turn forty-three human beings into numbers that fit in columns.

"Veran," she said.

"Mm."

"The boy in Ashmere. Edric."

"Conditional. Reevaluation in twelve months."

"His mother asked me something, after you left. She asked whether the wing would keep growing."

"What did you tell her?"

"The truth. I told her I didn't know."

I put down the pen. This was unusual. Maren always knows. The liturgical classification system is designed to produce certainty — a Class VII-B has a predicted growth curve, a plateau, a set of expectations. The whole point of the system is to transform the chaos of the Crucible into something the temple can organize.

"The wing doesn't match the curve," she said. "It should have plateaued two months ago. It hasn't. The bone is still developing. The musculature is —" She paused. "The musculature is starting to develop."

"Starting to."

"It won't fly. I don't think. But it's not vestigial either. It's becoming something, and I don't have a classification for something that's becoming."

I understood then why she had been arguing with me for eleven days. Not because she thought every aspect was useful. Because she didn't know. The system she believed in — the categories, the curves, the predictions — worked for the aspects that had been catalogued and studied and fit into the seventeen types. But Edric's wing was doing something the system hadn't seen before. And the Litany said the Crucible does not choose its steel.

Maybe the Crucible doesn't choose its categories either.

"Write it up," I said.

"As what?"

"As what it is. An aspect in progress. Classification pending. Note that the musculature is developing beyond the predicted curve for VII-B and recommend extended observation."

"The Chapter won't like that. They want numbers."

"The Chapter wants numbers because numbers fit in the columns I am required to fill in. The columns were designed by someone at Thornwall who has never walked the northern circuit and does not know that a boy in Ashmere has a wing that is becoming something the columns do not have a name for."

Maren looked at me for a long time. She has a particular look — not warm exactly, but recognizing. The look of someone who has just realized that the military officer she was assigned to might actually believe the Litany.

I do.

I also filled in the column. I wrote "conditional" next to Edric's name, because that is what the form required, and the form is what the Chapter reads, and the Chapter is what determines whether a boy in a village of eleven houses gets a chance.

There is a word in the Litany for this. For the act of holding two truths at once — the sacred and the practical, the doctrine and the column — without resolving them. The word is korrath. Maren would translate it as "the patience of the unfinished." The Chapter Commander would not translate it at all, because the Chapter Commander does not read the Litany for vocabulary.

Korrath.

The forge that does not choose its steel.

I chose. The report is filed. Edric is conditional. The wing is becoming.
