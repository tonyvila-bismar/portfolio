The Lockkeeper of Liurnia

by Tess Jyanssen

Everybody tells you the Academy of Raya Lucaria is about sorcery.

It is not about sorcery. Sorcery is the product. The Academy is about access — who gets to study the stars, who gets to read the old texts, who gets to stand in a room full of glintstone and feel the cosmos hum through their skull like a tuning fork made of frozen light. The sorcery is what happens after. The access is the thing itself, and the access is controlled by a locked gate, and the gate has a key, and the key is held by people who have very strong opinions about who deserves to use it.

I know this because I tried to get in.

(I should back up. I was not, at this point, interested in sorcery. I was interested in the lake.)

Liurnia of the Lakes is — and I realize this sounds like a sales pitch, but I promise you it is not, or at least it is not

only

a sales pitch — one of the most extraordinary bodies of water I have encountered on any continent, in any age, under any number of moons. It stretches from the cliffs below Stormveil to the foothills of the plateau, shallow enough to wade in most places, deep enough to drown in a few, and the water is not precisely water. There's something in it. Some mineral or residue or ambient magical contamination that makes the surface reflect light at angles that should not be geometrically possible. I watched a sunset over Liurnia from the rooftops of the Village of the Albinaurics — long story, don't ask about the ladder — and the lake turned the sky into a kaleidoscope of golds and silvers and one shade of violet that I have never seen before or since and that I am fairly sure was the color of sorcery leaking into the visible spectrum.

The point is, the lake is a trade route. Or it should be. It connects the southern lowlands to the northern highlands, it's navigable by shallow-draft boat, and the settlements along its banks — the manor houses, the villages, the bizarre little communities of people living in half-submerged ruins — are all producing goods that somebody, somewhere, would pay good money for. Glintstone fragments. Carian heraldry. Crystal snails. Those enormous crabs — listen, I've sold stranger things to hungrier markets, and crab meat is crab meat regardless of whether the crab is the size of a horse.

But nobody trades on Liurnia. Not anymore.

The reason is the Academy. When Raya Lucaria sealed its gates — after Radagon left, after Rennala broke, after the whole scholarly apparatus decided that the best response to a collapsing civilization was to lock the door and pretend nobody was home — they didn't just seal the Academy. They sealed the

road

. The main passage from southern Liurnia to the Altus Plateau runs through Academy territory. The Cuckoo soldiers who used to patrol the lake are still patrolling the lake, but now they're not protecting trade. They're preventing it. Anyone who approaches the Academy gets turned away. Anyone who tries to move goods across the lake gets harassed, taxed, or stabbed, depending on the Cuckoo's mood and the day of the week and, I suspect, how recently they'd been fed.

This is a textbook licensing regime. You don't ban the commerce. You make the commerce so expensive and dangerous that only people with institutional backing can afford to engage in it. The Academy doesn't need to sell glintstone. It just needs to make sure nobody

else

can sell glintstone without going through the Academy. The Cuckoo patrols are not a military. They are a tariff.

(Not that anyone asked me, but — I have seen this exact structure in operation on four continents and under three different systems of divine law, and it always smells the same. It smells like mildew and entitlement.)

So I was trying to get in. Not to study. To talk to whoever was running the supply chain, because

someone

inside those walls was still eating, still lighting fires, still maintaining the glintstone apparatus, and that someone was buying supplies from

somewhere

, and wherever that somewhere was, the markup was going to be extraordinary, and I wanted a piece of it.

The gate was locked. Obviously.

The key was a Glintstone Key — a little crystalline disc that doubled as both a physical key and a magical credential, which is the most academically pretentious security system I've ever encountered and I once broke into a library that required you to solve a differential equation to open the front door. You could find discarded keys here and there. Dead scholars dropped them. Living scholars lost them. I'd heard a sorcerer on the far side of the lake had one behind a magic seal, but the seal required a different key, which required a

different

seal, and at that point you're not looking at security, you're looking at a bureaucracy that has automated itself into a recursive loop.

I found my key in the pocket of a dead woman near the South Raya Lucaria Gate.

She was sitting against the wall. She'd been there a while. Her robes were the Academy's blue — a student or a junior scholar, not a full sorcerer. The glintstone staff beside her was cracked. She'd made it out of the Academy but not much further.

The key was in her breast pocket, wrapped in a scrap of paper that said nothing useful — a partial supply list, maybe, or lecture notes, the ink too faded to read. Her hands were folded in her lap, which meant either someone had arranged her after death or she had arranged herself before it.

I took the key.

I stood there for a while.

She had gotten out. Past the seals, past the Cuckoo patrols, past whatever internal politics had locked the Academy tight as a miser's purse. She had gotten out, and then she had sat down against a wall and died anyway, because getting out of an institution doesn't mean the institution is done with you. Sometimes you carry the locked gate in your chest and it doesn't matter how far you walk.

I knew a woman like that once. Got out of her Tower with nothing but talent and fury and spent fifteen years trying to stop running. Never did stop. Fury is a wonderful fuel. It is not a destination.

I put the key in my pocket.

I went around the Academy instead. Took a boat across the lake's western shallows, bribed a fisherman for passage through the canyon, spent two days picking my way through terrain that was technically impassable and practically just very annoying, and came out on the Altus Plateau with blistered feet and a working knowledge of six new overland routes that bypassed the Academy entirely.

The glintstone market could wait. What I had now was better — a map of every path the Academy didn't control. The paths the Cuckoo soldiers didn't patrol because they didn't know they existed. The paths that traders would use once someone showed them the way, and once the traders came, the villages on the lake would start selling their crab meat and their crystal snails and their fragments of fallen stars, and the Academy could sit behind its locked gate and pretend the world wasn't moving without them.

I sold the first route map to a merchant caravan outside Altus for twice what the glintstone data would have been worth.

I kept the key though. You never know.
